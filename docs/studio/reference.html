LastFleet Studio

LastFleet Studio is the designers' tool. It's its own program, separate from the game, and it edits the game's data files right there in the repository.

Why not just edit the JSON by hand? You could, but you'd be guessing. Studio runs the game's own code alongside the data, so a change shows up right away: across thousands of rolls, in a weapon's numbers in play, and in your hands on the range! And nothing gets written to the files until you save.

What you can add as data, and what still needs a programmer, is covered in adding-content.md.

This page is the reference. Want to learn it by doing? The Studio guides walk you through real tasks in every mode, step by step, with screenshots.

There are ten modes, on the bar at the top:

  • Loot: what drops and how rare it is. Rarities (their odds, how they scale with item level, their bonuses), loot tables (what each source drops, with weights, and how it favors makers and rarities) and relics.
  • Armory: the guns. Frames (archetypes: the models file each one is built from, how it fires, rate of fire, magazine, damage and the rest of its numbers, base stats and clamps, whether it's in the stock kit, and how an enemy soldier fights with it), makers (their paint, bias, model names and signature traits), parts (and the model each one shows as), affixes, the trait rules, and impact (what a round's push does to what it hits, impact.json). A 3D panel shows the gun, and every part model, as exported from Blender.
  • Items: everything else that lies around. The items themselves (medkits, ammo packs, armor plates, stims, shield cells, breaching charges, the cargo pod beacon, weapon parts, raw materials for the refinery, and the fleet's supplies: fuel, food, water, medical supplies, munitions, alloy, electronics), and the item tables that a level's supply piles, footlockers, cases and cargo crates roll from (content/defs/loot/items.json). Plus what the items do (effects.json), how the fleet uses its supplies (supplies.json), and crafting: the grades, refinery tiers, recipes and fabrication (crafting.json).
  • Flow: the loot data as one big picture, read left to right: where things are found, the items, what the refinery and the fleet make from them, the grades, what the armory fabricates and what that rolls. Click a box to edit it right next to the chart.
  • Missions: the mission templates (content/defs/missions). Every setting for the procedural levels, with a level generated from the template and drawn from above so you can click through it, plus many seeds generated and counted. Below the templates is what's the same for every mission: how windows break (breaches.json), how rooms' doors behave (doors.json) and what the fleet finds to raid on the star map (campaign.json).
  • Levels: what levels are generated from (content/defs/procgen). Its tags (the words rules use to say where things go), its surface styles (how rooms and halls are built and how they look, drawn as they build), its room archetypes (the kinds of room built in generated shapes, or a hand-drawn one, each built on a turntable), its placement rules and the props they place, its networks, where a level's loot goes, how rooms are typed, the plans that structure-first levels are laid out by, and the districts their rooms come from.
  • Bodies: a body type's model playing its animation graph (content/anim), driven by the graph's parameters as sliders. You can check blends, pops, foot sliding and the IK without ever starting the game. It also has the editors for the graph (its nodes on a canvas), the rig (content/rigs) and the reloads' steps (content/defs/reloads.json).
  • Behavior: how enemies fight. Their behavior trees (content/defs/ai/soldier.json and any you add), the scoring that picks an intent in a fight, the squad personalities, the soldiers' difficulty (tuning.json), and the enemy variants (enemies.json) that the levels, the mission and the director spawn. You try all of it out while watching soldiers fight in a sandbox.
  • Look: the surface materials every model is drawn with (content/defs/materials.json): painted metal, bare metal, glass and the rest. You tune them on test shapes in a test room, under a station's lights, a flashlight in the dark, an alarm's red, a sodium hall's orange or the sun. See materials.md. It also tunes the skies that windows show (content/defs/skies.json).
  • Models: what each prop model is collided with wherever it's placed (content/props/colliders.json): its drawn mesh, its authored boxes, one box or cylinder around it, or nothing. You see the collider's outline over the model as it turns, along with what's worth fixing.

Running it

From the repository:

dotnet run --project src/LastFleet.Studio

Studio edits content/defs/loot, content/defs/ai, content/defs/missions and the rest of content it has editors for (content/defs/procgen, content/anim, content/rigs, materials.json...) in whichever repository it's run from. How does it find the repository? It looks for LastFleet.slnx above the program, or above the folder you run it in.

A few handy flags:

  • A different copy: to edit a different copy of the first three, pass --loot <folder>, --ai <folder> and --missions <folder>.
  • Open in a mode: --mode armory (or items, flow, missions, levels, bodies, behavior, look, models) opens in that mode.
  • Open on something: --select <id> opens with that definition selected (a part, a rarity, a loot table...; use supplies:water to select it in its own list). --pick model then opens the model picker on its model.
  • Everything else: --help lists the rest.

Editing

F11 (or View > Full screen) goes full screen. Press it again to go back to a window.

Every pane can be resized. Drag the bar between two panes to change how the space is split between them. A faint grip marks the middle of the bar, and the cursor changes when you're over it. This works in every mode: lists against their editors, previews against what's next to or under them, the Bodies timeline against the turntable.

  • The panels pinned to an edge (Look's, Behavior's and the selected soldier's) get wider and narrower by dragging that edge.
  • The model picker's and a prop's preview get taller by dragging the bar under them.
  • Double-click a bar to reset it.

Sizes are stored as fractions of the window, so they still fit when the window changes size. They're saved next to the game's settings (studio-layout.json), so Studio opens just the way you left it. A smoke run ignores them.

Ctrl+Z undoes the last change, in any mode and any file (Edit > Undo shows which file). Ctrl+Y or Ctrl+Shift+Z redoes it. What counts as "one change"? A drag (a node, a curve's key, a slider) or typing in a field, from when you grab it to when you let go. A few more details:

  • While you're typing in a field, Ctrl+Z undoes your typing in that field.
  • Undoing all the way back to what's on disk counts as saved.
  • The last 200 changes are kept.
  • Revert, or a file being reloaded from disk, clears the changes for that file.
  • An undo or redo clears the selection in a graph.

The left column lists every definition by its id. Pick one and you edit it in the middle. Above its fields it tells you what the list is for and how to add to it. Hover over a field to see what it does.

About these fields shows the comment at the top of the file (right after its opening brace), line for line: // lines, or a /* ... */ block (with the leading * on each line dropped), keeping blank lines and indentation. Studio writes it back unchanged. Heads up though: comments anywhere else in a file are lost when Studio saves it.

Here's how each kind of field works:

  • Numbers: drag left and right, or double-click to type. Counts (a magazine, a stat's points) stay whole numbers.
  • Text is typed, except where it refers to something defined elsewhere. A rarity, a maker, a frame, a part, an affix, a relic, a table, a trait, a stat, a slot or a part's model is picked from a list of what exists. A list of those names (a maker's signature traits, a relic's parts, a tank's supplies, a hall style's accents) has a picker for each one and + add for another. A name the data doesn't recognize is marked in red. A color (#rrggbb, or three numbers where Studio knows the field is a color) gets a color picker. A pair [min, max] is two fields side by side. A list of names that don't refer to anything (a maker's weapon names) has a field for each one, x to remove one and + add for another. It's never one text box you split with commas!
  • Fields left out use a default. Studio shows them greyed out, with set to write a value in (a maker's color, a part's model, a frame's frameStats).
  • Lists of things (a table's entries, a part's mods) open as a tree. x removes a row, and + add adds one like the last.
  • Maps (a table's maker and rarity bias, a maker's model names) take new keys: type the key and press + add.
  • About these fields shows the file's own notes on what each field means.

+ New under each list's title adds an entry at the end of the list. It starts as a copy of the selected one (or the first one), with the id new. Give it an id and a name, then change whatever makes it different. Duplicate copies the selected entry right after itself with its own id (..._2). Delete removes it, and anything that refers to it will stop loading.

After every change, Studio rebuilds the game's loot data. What if it doesn't build anymore (a table naming a frame that doesn't exist, a part with no slot, a missing field)? The bottom of the window tells you what's wrong, using the game's own error messages. Until you fix it, the previews show the last version that built. The game would refuse the data in that state, so fix it before saving.

File > Save all (Ctrl+S) writes the changed files. File > Revert to saved throws your changes away. The bottom line shows which files have changes, and if you close Studio with unsaved changes, it asks first.

Studio writes the files in the same layout they're already in, comments included, so a save only changes the lines you edited and the diff is easy to review. Edited a file by hand? Run LastFleetStudio --format (or dotnet run --project src/LastFleet.Studio -- --format) to put it back in that layout. The tests check that every file is in it.

Seeing a change

Editing numbers blind isn't much fun, so Studio gives you a bunch of ways to see what a change actually does:

  • Loot Lab (Loot mode, on the right): pick a loot table and an item level and roll 1,000 or 10,000. It shows rarity, maker and frame shares, each stat's spread and the power curve. After every change it re-rolls the same batch automatically, so you can watch the odds move as you drag a weight. Roll one shows a single weapon in full.
  • Preview (Armory mode, on the right): a weapon rolled from the current data. Pick its frame, maker and rarity, or leave them on Any. When you're editing a frame or a maker, it previews that one. The card shows its name, stats, traits, parts and affixes. Below that are its numbers in play: damage per shot, precision, rate of fire, damage per second (with and without reloads), magazine, reload, falloff, zoom, spread, kick, aim and swap times. Next to each number is what the same roll gave with the saved data, in green where it got better and red where it got worse. Next seed rolls another one.
  • Damage over distance (Armory, above a frame's fields): the frame's falloff drawn as a curve of damage (as a fraction of full) over meters. It's full out to where the falloff starts, drops in a straight line to its lowest, and stays flat after that. It's drawn for a gun with range stat 0 and with 100 (the start point moves with range), and for the previewed gun if it uses this frame. You can drag its keys: where it starts at range 0 or 100 (falloffStart), or where it ends, sideways for falloffSpan and up or down for falloffMinimumMultiplier. Hover over the curve to read how much damage a hit does at that distance.
  • The 3D panel (Armory, top right):
    • The rolled gun spins the previewed weapon on a turntable, put together from its part models the way the game does it.
    • Hand points marks where hands go on it (the pistol grip, the fore grip, the magazine's grab and well, the charging handle) and its muzzle.
    • A gun with an optic also shows Through the sight: the reticle as it looks aimed (the part's reticle), and its second zoom beside it if it has one (secondZoom).
    • Part models lists every model for the previewed frame's slot (Receiver, Barrel, Magazine...) and shows the selected one, along with the data parts that use it. Drag to rotate it, and use the mouse wheel to bring it closer. The models come from the repository's content/weapons, so when you export from Blender, Studio reloads the file right away.
    • Recipe shows the part's recipe under it (content/weapons/forge.json), one shape per line, in millimeters. You edit it the same way as furniture recipes (Levels > Props, Its recipe, with the gizmo on the stage; see below).
  • Picking a model: a field that refers to a model (a part's model) shows the model's icon and name. Click it to open the list of models it can use, below it. They're grouped by file, each with its icon, and there's a filter to narrow them down. Next to the list, the model spins in 3D (the one under the mouse, or else the selected one; drag to rotate it, and use the mouse wheel to bring it closer).
    • Under it are the model's paint slots (materials named Paint_<Slot> in Blender, see props.md). If the thing picks its own colors, each slot gets a swatch, saved by slot name in a paint map next to the field (as in Blender sets a slot back to its color in the file). Otherwise the slots are shown the way Blender colored them.
    • Every top-level object in every file in content/props and content/weapons is listed, named file/Object (items/Medkit), and a new file shows up without any code. Too easy!
    • A part's model names just the object (the game finds its frame's file), and the picker offers that slot's models.
    • Icons are drawn from the models (architecture.md), never by hand.
  • Files changed outside Studio (an exported model, a data file edited by hand or pulled from Git) are reloaded as soon as they're written. The status line says what was reloaded. If a data file has unsaved changes in Studio, your version is kept. Save to keep yours, or Revert to take the version on disk.
  • Try it on the range: puts you on the game's range with that weapon in your hands, playing just like the game, with targets to shoot (no soldiers). A card shows its numbers, your shots and your target hits. Esc takes you back to Studio.

Items: what lies about

Items mode edits content/defs/loot/items.json, effects.json, supplies.json and crafting.json:

  • Items: every item, including the fleet's supplies and raw materials. Each one has:

    • an id;
    • a name (as the HUD shows it);
    • a model (picked from content/props, with its paint);
    • a color (its swatch and the band on its crates; color (pick) above the fields picks it);
    • a stack (how many fit in a slot);
    • flags for what it's for: consumable (with useSeconds and the effect it runs, picked from the effects), an optional passive (an effect you get while it's held: set it under the fields, then pick one), fleetSupply, refinable (a raw material for the refinery), weaponPart (with fitSeconds) and carried (held in both arms, like a crate).

    Each flag is a checkbox, and the editor only shows the fields that the item's flags turn on. Tick consumable and its use time and effect appear with their defaults; untick it and they're removed from the data. The list and The items in play show each item's icon, drawn from its model in its paint.

    Wondering where an item ends up? Above it, Used by says what uses it: the item tables it's found in (and how often), the recipes that refine it, the refinery upgrades it pays for (each one a button that takes you there), and what the game's own rules do with it (the starting kit, a soldier's drop, the stations' tanks, the fleet's needs).

  • Links: a field that refers to another entry (an item table's item, a recipe's or a fabrication's grade, a refinery tier's grades, the items in a recipe's inputs or a tier's cost) has an open button next to it that takes you to that entry.

  • Effects (effects.json): what an item does, and when. Each effect has a trigger (use: when its item is used from a quick slot; passive: for as long as its item is held) and its actions, in order. Each action does one thing, picked in do:

    • heal (by its amount);
    • giveArmor (amount, up to max);
    • shield (amount, regenerating at perSecond after delaySeconds without taking damage);
    • changeStat (stat: moveSpeed, damageTaken or reloadSpeed, changed by a fraction, for seconds);
    • refillAmmo (magazines, added to every weapon's reserve);
    • callPod (sets a cargo pod down in front of you, if the mission is still going);
    • placeDevice (a charge placed on whatever the player is looking at, which goes off after fuseSeconds like a grenade: blastDamage over blastRadius, breaking anything breakable);
    • sound (a cue, picked from content/defs/sounds.json);
    • flash (a burst of light: color, brightness, radius, seconds).

    A passive effect can only shield and change stats. Picking a different kind of action swaps its fields for the new kind's, at their defaults. An effect is only used when one of its actions would actually do something (healing only when hurt, and so on). Used by lists the items that have it, and The items in play says what each one gives (a held item's passive shows as "held: ...").

  • Supplies (supplies.json): how the fleet uses each fleet supply. Every item with fleetSupply has rules here:

    • use: what a jump costs, in parts that add up (each a per: jump, ship, damagedShip, people, shipAlive, contact, with its amount);
    • short: what happens when you run short, in order (peopleLost, morale, shipWorn, message);
    • stocked: what having enough does (morale, repairShips, produce);
    • jumps (used up by the jump itself), boarders, startJumps and stations (the star map's stations that mostly hold this supply; which ones a station keeps in tanks is set by its mission's tankKinds).

    Picking a different kind swaps its fields, the same as an effect's actions. For a new fleet shows what a jump currently costs of it and what a fleet starts with. An item with rules links to them under Used by.

  • Item tables: what a supply pile, a footlocker, a case or a cargo crate holds. Each roll picks one entry, by weight, with a count ([fewest, most]) and perDeck extra for each deck deeper. A mission names the tables its level uses (pileTable, footlockerTable, caseTable, cargoTable in its loot). Each table's shares are drawn above its fields.

  • Crafting (crafting.json): what the fleet makes of raw materials, and what the armory makes of that.

    • Grades of refined material, lowest first, each with the itemLevel of what's fabricated from it.
    • Refinery tiers (a fleet starts at the first), each with its cost in the fleet's supplies, the grades it can make and its batches a jump.
    • Recipes, a batch taking its inputs (raw materials) and giving so many of a grade.
    • Fabrication, what the armory bench makes (a weapon rolled from a loot table, or a part of a slot) for cost of its grade.

    Flow mode draws all of it.

  • What a table gives (on the right): a table rolled 10,000 times at the deck you pick, and rolled again after every change. It shows how often each item comes up, and how many. Selecting a table on the left shows it here. The items in play lists every item as the game currently has it.

Each time a fight starts, every pile, container and crate is rolled fresh from its table (a weapon part at the deck's item level), the same as the weapons.

Flow: how it all connects

The loot data is spread across a handful of files, and it's hard to keep the whole chain in your head. So how does a raw material you pick up turn into a gun? Flow mode draws it for you, as boxes and arrows in columns from left to right:

  1. Where it is found: the item tables, each with the missions that roll their piles, footlockers, cases and cargo crates from it.
  2. Items, each with what it's for (used in the field, a weapon part, a fleet supply, raw material...).
  3. What they become: the refinery's recipes, and below them the fleet's supplies, each with what the fleet uses of it (per ship, per people, the jump itself).
  4. Grades of material, and below them the refinery tiers.
  5. What the armory makes: the fabrications.
  6. What that rolls: the loot table a fabricated weapon is rolled from (and the tables missions roll weapons from), or the kind of part.

Arrows go from what's found, paid or used to what it gives, buys or makes. The colors mean:

  • white: gives (a table gives its items, with their share; a recipe gives its grade);
  • amber: costs (a recipe's inputs, a fabrication's grade, a refinery tier's supplies, each with how many);
  • blue: unlocks (a refinery tier unlocks the grades it's the first to refine);
  • green: the fleet's (an item to its supply's needs; water growing food).

Hover over a box to highlight its arrows with their amounts, and everything else dims. Click it to edit it in the panel on the right, where every change shows up on the chart right away.

A name that doesn't exist (a recipe naming an item nobody defined, a fabrication with a misspelled grade) shows as a red box with red arrows, and the bar above the chart counts them. The chart is read straight from the files, so it still shows even when the data doesn't build. Find the red, fix the name, and the rest of Studio builds again!

Drag the chart to move it, use the mouse wheel to zoom, and double-click an empty spot (or See it all) to fit it on screen. Amounts show on every arrow once you zoom in close.

Missions: the procedural levels

Missions mode edits the mission templates, one file each in content/defs/missions. A level is generated from the current template, using the current procgen data from Levels mode, and drawn on the right. It's regenerated after every change to either, so you're never looking at a stale level.

A deck theme (hallThemes) says how big and open its halls are, and names the surface style they're built in (style). How they look is edited in Levels mode's Styles.

  • The template (in the middle): every setting the generator reads, in plain words (hover over one to see what it does). Decks (zones), rooms along the way, side rooms and loot rooms per deck, loops, soldiers, stories, the rooms built from the seed, and the loot: the weapon tables and how many, and the item tables for supply piles, footlockers, cases and cargo. + New mission (a copy) starts a new template from the selected one.

    The station's fixtures are props picked in 3D, the same way as an item's model: each of the tankKinds (its model, the supplies it can hold, its radius and height, a weight), the reservoir's model, outlet and valve, and the airlock's intake and chute (docs/blender/props.md, station.gltf). If a model is missing or doesn't have its empties, the level can't be generated, and Studio tells you why.

  • The level: its seed (Next, Random), and the lobby settings a lobby applies to the template (decks, soldiers %, weapons %, stories, built rooms, what the cargo is mostly made of, cargo %). They aren't saved; they just show what a lobby would get. --mode missions --seed <n> opens Studio on that seed. Above the map:

    • If the level had to be regenerated because its template requires reservoirs it couldn't place, it says so, along with how many it has (red if it's still short).
    • A line on how linear it is: the share of its rooms on a loop and the share of dead ends (compared against the template's loopRules, red when it falls short), the longest chain of dead ends, its one-way shortcuts, and how long the mission's route is and how much of it you walk twice.

  • The map (on the right): the level from above, one story at a time (rooms on other stories are drawn as outlines). Rooms are colored by what they're for and labeled with their deck (L d2 is a loot room on deck 2). Doorways are white, gates red, and one-way shortcuts blue, with a dot on the side they open from.

    The marks are:

    • insertion (I), objective (O), extraction (X);
    • keycards (yellow diamonds), soldiers (red), weapons (orange triangles);
    • supply piles (dots in the color of what they hold), footlockers and cases (brown), cargo crates (in the color of their supply);
    • hull breaches (purple rings), a deep reservoir's valves (red squares) and the reservoir (a ring);
    • and networks. Each network route is orange (faint when it's off the story shown, with a dot where it goes up or down), the cheapest route it didn't take is grey beside it, and there's an orange diamond at its middle.

    Show... hides any kind of mark. Drag to move, use the mouse wheel to zoom, and Fit to see it all. Map, Types and Recipe pick what's shown.

    Recipe shows the mission's recipe as nodes, left to right. First its steps (Main path, Keycards, Side rooms, Loot rooms, each saying what it adds and on which decks), then Loops, Type rooms and The level. Click a node to edit it below (a step's fields: rooms, the last deck's share, the detour chance, how many, how often two deep, which decks) and to see the level as it is after that node: the mission's graph after a step, the map after the loops, the room types after typing, and the finished map at the end. It's a great way to see exactly where something went sideways! Under the nodes, + side rooms and + loot rooms add a step after the selected one, < Earlier and Later > move it, and Remove the step deletes it (the main path and the keycard stay). A template that grows from its room counts shows the recipe those counts produce, and Write the recipe writes it into the template so you can change it.

    Types instead shows the mission's rooms as they're connected (the insertion first, each room to the right of the one you reach it from, gates in red), each colored by the type it was given (the kit's own rooms grey, each archetype its own color, with a legend on the left). Hover over one to see why, and click it to see it below.

  • Click a mark (or a room) to see it below the map: what it is, its deck and the deck's theme (a hall built in the theme is marked as such), and what the fight rolls for it (on Run 0, the first run; each restart rolls fresh), for example a footlocker's piles or a crate's supply and amount.

    • A room says why it got its type (drawn by weight, or the only one the rules left), what else it could have been and how likely, and what ruled out each other type, with a Rule button that opens a typing rule in Levels mode.
    • A room also lists how each placement rule did there: how many it placed out of how many it wanted, and if it fell short, why the spots it tried didn't work (too tall, something already there, it would block the path, it can't be reached...). A Rule button opens the rule in Levels mode. Hovering over the Rule button shows the sub-seed it drew from: the level's seed, the placement stage, the room and the rule.
    • A network says how long its pipe is, what it cost and what it runs along, and what the route it didn't take would have cost, with a Network button that opens it in Levels mode.
    • For a pile, a container, a crate or a weapon, you can pick the table that everything of its kind in this mission rolls from (this changes the template's loot; a loot assignment that names its own table wins, see Levels > Loot), or Edit the table, which opens it in Items mode (or Loot mode for a weapon's table).

  • Play it (above the map): plays the level shown just like the game, with the loot, items and AI as they currently are in Studio, saved or not. Esc brings you back.

  • Checks (under the template): whether every table named by the template and the loot assignments exists, and Make 12, 100 or 1000 levels and count. One level looking good doesn't tell you much, so this generates that many seeds starting from the one shown, with the lobby settings (a bar shows progress, and Stop keeps what's been made so far).

    • Each level is counted (rooms, soldiers, weapons, piles, containers, crates, keycards, the percent of rooms on a loop and of dead ends, one-way shortcuts), with the fewest, average and most.
    • It also shows whether every level meets the loop rules, and any that can't be generated (the first 100 seeds are listed; click one to show it).
    • A heat map under it shows where placement rules fell short: rules down the side, kinds of room (an archetype, or what a room is for) across the top, and each cell showing how often, hotter meaning more.

  • A template laid out by a plan (plan, as ship_raid.json is) works a bit differently:

    • It has its plans and their weights, how many zones, how long the way through should be, where keycards go (room tags, doors off the way through, the chance of a long detour), the objective (its kind, the room tags it can be in, and the archetype a room is made into when none has them; or objectives, the kinds a level picks among by weight, each with how long it takes and, for a self-destruct, its countdown and how often a compartment seals, added filled in with the three kinds' defaults), the exits extraction tries in order, and spawn closets per zone.
    • The map then draws the plan's level with the mission read into it: the airlock's vestibule (I), each zone's rooms labeled with their deck, the gates through the bulkheads in red, the keycards, the objective (O), the arena, extraction (X) and escape pods (orange squares). The recipe, room counts, loops and stories above aren't used by such a template.
    • Its threat says how hard its places are defended: the first and last zones' threat, scales by district and by room tag, and the patrols it sets (seconds between them and soldiers in one, at threat 0 and at 1), with the room tags patrols would rather come from beside. The map's Threat box shades each room by its threat, cool to red, and picking a room names its threat and whether patrols would rather come from it.
    • Its breaches say how a window blown out goes: the chance (permanentChance) it seals its compartment for good, when the mission can still be finished without it and every compartment already lost (else the shutter closes and the doors unseal), and the districts (never) whose compartments are never lost.
    • Its fleet is the rest of the fleet seen from the windows: how many ships, how far off and how long.

  • Breaches (under Every mission, at the bottom of the list): how windows onto space break and what a breach does, the same for every mission (content/defs/breaches.json).

    • A window's toughness, and the shares of it taken when its cracks show and spread.
    • The pull: how fast it drags someone at the breach, how far it reaches, how near someone is caught in it and what that does to them a second, and how near a dropped weapon or item is pulled out.
    • The timers (the doors sealing, the shutter closing, the air coming back).
    • Its look: how long the shutter takes to slide down, how much mist streams out, and how fast the lamps over sealed doors flash.

    A line under it sums it up: how many hits of a grenade a window takes, and how long a breach lasts. Want to see it for real? In play, the developer overlay's Blow out window breaks the window nearest you.

  • Doors (under Every mission, after Breaches): how rooms' own doors behave in a ship's level (content/defs/doors.json).

    • A sliding door's reach (how near anyone comes for it to open), how long it waits to shut, and how near its middle anyone keeps it open.
    • A door opened by hand: its reach for a player, and how near and how long a soldier stands at it to open it.
    • How much battering the swarm does to one, and how much it takes before it's broken open for good (and can no longer seal).

    Which rooms' doors open by hand is each archetype's roomDoor (Levels > Archetypes).

  • Campaign (under Every mission, after Doors): what the fleet finds to raid in each system of the star map (content/defs/campaign.json). Each target has its id (one must be station), the name the map gives a ship of it, the mission it's raided with (station_raid, or ship_raid with the plan it's laid out by, picked from Levels > Plans), its weight and the first sector it turns up in. Below, each target's share of a sector's systems in sectors 1, 2, 3 and 5. A sector's first system and its gate are always stations.

  • The template's loops and dead ends (loopRules): how far apart a loop's ends are, the longest corridor that can be routed around corners for one, the share of rooms that should be on a loop and the most that should be dead ends (loops are added until both are met, or no more fit), and how many one-way shortcuts cross each gate.

Levels: what levels are made from

Levels mode edits the designers' procgen data in content/defs/procgen. It grows along with the procgen revamp (../plans/procgen-revamp.md). Each of these is a tab on the left:

  • the tags (tags.json);
  • the surface styles (styles.json);
  • the room archetypes (archetypes.json);
  • the placement rules and the props they place (rules.json, props.json);
  • the networks (networks.json);
  • the loot assignments (loot.json);
  • the room typing (typing.json);
  • the level plans (plans.json);
  • and the districts their rooms come from (districts.json).

Tags

Tags are how rules say where things go. A tag is a dotted name made of lowercase letters, digits and underscores, such as room.storage.cold. Tags nest: a rule asking for room.storage also matches a room tagged room.storage.cold. Every parent a name implies (room, room.storage) counts as a tag even if it isn't listed.

  • The list (on the left): every tag as a tree, filtered by what you type. Built-in tags are marked and dimmed, because the generator sets them itself (role.*, what a room is for in the mission; layout.main_path, layout.dead_end, layout.on_loop). Implied tags are dimmed too. A tag with a problem (a bad name, defined twice, using a built-in's name) is red. Hover over it to see why.
  • + New tag adds one to tags.json as new_tag. Rename it and describe what it means on the right.
  • The tag picked (on the right): where it comes from, its name and description (editable only for your own tags), Remove the tag, and what it's nested under and what's nested under it (click one to go to it). An implied tag has Describe it, which adds it to tags.json so it can have a description.

Styles

A surface style is how the generator builds a room and how the room looks: its walls, floor and ceiling, the bands around its walls, the duct and pipes under its ceiling, its light panels, and, for a hall built in the style, what stands along the hall and the lights in its floor.

So who models the walls? Nobody! The generator works out a room's walls from its shape and builds them in the room's style. A style only lists what's different from its base, which is another style, and ultimately station.

  • The list: every style. The ones styles.json doesn't list are built in (station, plus service, public, industrial and research for the default deck themes' halls), and they're dimmed, shown the way the game builds them. A style based on another one shows < base. A style with a problem is red. Hover over it to see why.

  • + New style adds one to styles.json as new_style, based on the selected style.

  • Graph shows the styles as a graph instead. Each style sits to the right of its base, with the room parts it sets itself, wired to what's built in it on the right (the archetypes, and each mission's deck themes). Click a style to edit it.

  • The style picked: drawn as it builds, and redrawn with every change. It shows a stretch of a room's wall (a doorway and its lintel, the baseboard and stripe, the duct, pipes and panels) and a hall built in the style, seen down its length. Pick the deck and room to see the trims in that deck's color or that room role's color.

    Below that are its base, id, description and every part it changes, all editable. Colors have a picker; materials and a hall's structure and floor are picked from what exists; a trim's color is either fixed or follows the deck or the role; a hall's accents are picked, any of them. Say how it differs... adds a part it currently inherits from its base, copied as the base has it, so you can change it. Take from its base... removes one again. Remove the style deletes it.

  • A built-in style has List it in styles.json, which writes it out in full, as built in, so you can change it.

One thing a style doesn't touch: sizes the game depends on (where walls stand, how thick and tall they are, a doorway's height, a panel's size). A style decides how a room looks, not where people and shots can go.

Archetypes

A room archetype is a kind of room the generator builds from the level's seed. It sets:

  • the shape the room is built in;
  • its tags, and which parts of a mission it can play;
  • the style it's built in, and its furniture;
  • whether its corners are cut, and whether pillars hold up its ceiling;
  • how many stories tall it is, with a floor (or none) on each;
  • and (roomDoor) the door its doorways onto halls get in a ship's level: auto, sliding open for whoever comes near, or interact, opened and shut by hand (quarters, offices, storage, security and server rooms ship as interact).

bay makes it a hangar ships come and go from (flight_hangar ships so). In a ship's level each of its walls wholly on the hull is a ship entrance, open the room's full height behind a faint field you see space through but can't walk through, with the lane from it to the dropship's pad kept clear of furniture, props, loot and crates. Two walls across from each other make a hangar a dropship flies straight through (the strike carrier's).

A mission template names the archetypes its built rooms are drawn from (Missions mode, archetypes, picked from the list).

Every room is an archetype. The station's kit (the insertion bay, the corridors and stairwell, the spawn closet, the keycard, objective and extraction rooms, and the rest that every level has) is the set of archetypes whose kit is station, each built once from its id. A kit's archetypes aren't offered to templates.

  • The list: every archetype in archetypes.json, with its shape. + New archetype adds a plain room with two to four cells per side. Copy adds a new one like the selected one.

  • The archetype picked: six footprints its recipe makes from seeds in turn (Reroll for different ones). Click one to build it below the way the generator does: seen from above, with its doorways in yellow and its cut corners, and on a turntable next to it (drag to rotate it, and use the mouse wheel to get closer). A tall room has a story slider under the plan. Each story shows its floor, its railings, the doorways onto it, and the stairs climbing from it or through it (with a bridge where they cross open space to the floor).

  • Its form:

    • its shape's kind and ranges (cells across and deep, how much a cut takes, a blob's lumps, the corner cut);
    • its tags (the content's own, picked), roles, style and furniture (picked);
    • its stories (a slider from 1 to 4: raising it adds open stories at the top, lowering it removes the top ones);
    • each story's floor above the ground (vertical.floors: its kind, none for an open story, its depth and the stairs' kind; a stairwell's landing);
    • its weight;
    • for a kit room, its kit and, for a rectangle of a fixed size, its doors (each a side, a cell and a level).

    Fields it can leave out are offered with their defaults. A stamp is drawn by hand: a grid of the largest size a room can be appears above the form, and clicking a cell adds or removes it. The cells have to touch side by side, with two or more each way. Remove the archetype deletes it.

Rules

What goes in rooms lives in rules.json. It's split in two:

  • First, each furnishing's rules: how a built room with that furnishing gets furnished as it's built. Archetypes name a furnishing.
  • Then the level's rules, in the order they place (highest priority first).

+ New rule adds one to the level's rules (a footlocker against a wall now and then). + New furnishing adds a furnishing with one rule. + rule under a furnishing adds one of its rules. Copy adds one like the selected one.

  • The rule picked: a card that says in plain words what it does: what it places (each prop's share), in which rooms, where in them, how many, the clear space around each one and the spacing between them, whether it blocks the way (if so it leaves every path through open), the floor space it keeps clear in front for using it, and whether it's required. Next to it is its first prop's footprint, the same as on the Props tab.

  • Its form: what (props picked from props.json, each with a weight), where (tags picked: any, all, none, including the generator's role.* and layout.*), its anchor and fallback (picked), count, clear, spacing, priority, blocks, interaction and required. Fields it can leave out are offered with their defaults. Remove the rule deletes it (a furnishing left with no rules is deleted too).

  • arrange: places a furniture rule's props as a group, rather than one at a time.

    • It can be a row (columns: a range for how many pieces) or a grid (columns and rows).
    • gap is the meters between pieces in a row (0 for a run of server racks).
    • aisle is the meters between rows (at least 1.1 m, so a player and a soldier can pass).
    • along sets the room's length, across it, or its wall.
    • backToBack pairs rows facing away from each other (lab benches).
    • fill places as many as fit instead of a drawn number.

    A galley, for example, is a grid of mess tables with chairs attached. The group is placed whole or not at all: if any piece would block off a doorway, none of them are placed. Each group counts once toward the rule's count. The card says what it makes (a grid of 2–3 by 1–4, 0.4 m apart, 2 m aisles, along the room).

Props

What rules place lives in props.json, each with its kind. The selected prop is drawn to scale from above (its front, the side facing into the room, in yellow; on the Rules tab, its rule's clear space shaded around it; plus the half-meter grid props are placed on) and from the side (against a room's 3.6 m walls and the highest a prop can reach, 0.6 m below the ceiling to leave room for ducts and lights).

Next to that are the rules that place it, and its form: kind (picked), size, and for furniture its color, what it breaks into (picked) and its piece's name. A tank's size comes from its kind in the mission template.

A window (onto space, on a plan level's hull walls) has a color (its frame's), its size (its top over the floor as its height) and a sill, how high its glass starts; a rule places windows alone, at anchor hull. + New prop, Copy and Remove the prop work as elsewhere.

Furniture can use a model (picked from the models in every prop file, furniture/Mess_Table). Its size then comes from the model, and the model spins on the stage with its loot sockets and attach points marked, each group in its own color. A model with paint slots takes a color for each one (paint). A furniture model's problems (a solid part that isn't a box, a breakable piece with more than one box, a base that's off its origin) show as the prop's problem in the list.

  • sockets: loot for a group of the model's sockets (Top, Shelf2). You set the chance it holds anything, the fewest and most sockets that get loot, the item table, the weapon table and the share of weapons, and item levels on top of the level's own. Setting a table to room uses the room's own table (a stash room's weapons, a deck's piles), as loot.json assigns them. The line under it says what one piece yields on average. A group with no loot is a surface other rules place things on (a case on a desk). Loot on furniture that breaks falls to the floor.
  • attach: a prop that stands at a group of the model's attach points (a chair at each Chair). You set its chance, the share that get pulled out, how far (up to 1.5 m), and a turn of up to 45° either way. A piece that would block a path, poke through a wall or stand in the aisle is left out.
  • Its recipe: a forged model (furniture.gltf) can be edited here the same way a gun is in Armory mode: its parts, their shapes and materials, and its solid boxes. Write files re-forges the file, and the stage shows the change.
    • On the stage: pick a line (with the button on its left, or by clicking into it), a marker or a solid box (the buttons under the lines). It's outlined in yellow, with a gizmo. Move (W) shows arrows along x (red), y (green) and z (blue); drag one to move it that way. Scale (E) shows a handle at the end of each axis it can grow along. Drag one out to make it longer in that direction, with both ends moving equally (a rotated box scales along its own axes; a prism, tube or rail scales its length and, across, its radius). Drag the white box in the middle to scale it as a whole. Markers can only move. Vents and bolts can only move too (a bolt's head scales as a whole). The line updates as you drag, and so does the model.
    • Drags snap to 5 mm (1 mm for a gun part). Hold Shift for finer steps or Ctrl for coarser ones. Escape or the right mouse button cancels a drag. Undo reverts the last drag, or the last line added or removed. While something is selected, the stage stays still and keeps its view as the model changes. Drag on the stage, away from the gizmo, to rotate it.
    • Nothing is saved until you press Write files. Read again throws away every change.

Networks

Networks are what connect parts of a level, in networks.json, each with its kind. reservoir_feed is the one the generator lays, from a deep reservoir to its outlet, and it can't be removed.

So how do you tell a pipe where to go? You don't, exactly: you tell it what each way costs. The selected network shows what its route pays: a slider for each way the route can run (along a wall's band, over a doorway, under the ceiling, standing off a wall, across an open ceiling, up a wall, through a wall, through a lintel, through a service cell) and one for each bend. The cheapest route wins, so raising a cost keeps the pipe from running that way (ctrl-click to type a value above the slider's 200).

Below that is its form: its kind, its limits (the longest open span, the most service cells), its pipe (size, color, material, bracket spacing) and how far apart bundled pipes run. A level's route, and the route it didn't take, are shown on the Missions mode map. + New network, Copy and Remove the network work as elsewhere.

Loot

Where a level's loot goes, in loot.json. On the left are the assignments in the order they apply (each showing what it gives: cache, stash, tables, levels), Each deck's stashes, and every table a level's loot is rolled from.

The selected assignment shows a card (the rooms it applies to, and in words what they get), then its form:

  • where: tags picked (any, all, none; deck.1 to deck.6 for a deck);
  • cache: its weapons table and count (if left out, the mission's);
  • stash: its weight against the deck's other rooms, with more per room deeper off the main path and per soldier holding it, and its table;
  • tables: the item tables its piles, footlockers, cases and cargo roll from;
  • levels: item levels added to its caches, stashes, piles and containers.

Every assignment that matches a room applies. The first cache and the first of each table win, while weights and levels add up, so the order matters (the arrows move the selected one). Each deck's stashes gives the first decks their own counts (the rest use the mission's stashesPerZone).

A table shows what's rolled from it, and the assignments (with the rooms they name) and missions that use it. It's red if the loot data has no table by that name.

No loot.json? Then the generator uses its built-in assignments (the same as the shipped file). Write loot.json or + New assignment writes the file with them. + New assignment, Copy and Remove the assignment work as elsewhere.

Typing

How a level's rooms are given types before they're laid out, in typing.json. Each room in the mission becomes either one of the kit's own rooms or one of the mission's archetypes. On the left are:

  • the kit's own share (of the rooms that could be an archetype, before the rules apply);
  • + count, + adjacent and + distance to add a rule of that kind (Copy copies the selected one);
  • the rules;
  • and the Adjacency table.

A selected rule shows a card saying what it asks for, then its form:

  • a count: where (tags picked), per (deck or level), min and max;
  • an adjacency: a and b;
  • a distance: a, b, min and max steps along the mission's paths (no limit unless set).

Here, a room's tags are its role, its deck, layout.main_path, and its type's tags: an archetype's tags or type.kit. The Adjacency table lists every archetype's tags and type.kit against each other. Tick a cell to keep rooms of those two from being connected (it writes a simple adjacency rule; unticking removes it). If a rule set can't be satisfied, the level stops, and Missions mode shows why.

Plans

Plans are the shapes of structure-first levels, in plans.json (../plans/structure-first.md): a frigate, a carrier, a station of hub and spokes, an accreted station (spokes, bolted-on modules and an arm bending to a second hub), a ring station, or one of your own.

A plan is a list of parts, applied in order, each adding cells (4 m) to its envelope or cutting them out. Plan coordinates run along a ship from its bow (x) and across it (z), with the centerline at z = 0; north is toward −z, the top of the picture.

A plan can have decks, bottom to top a story apart, each with a name and, if not the plan's, its sections' districts; every deck is cut into the same sections. With more than one, each part and hall has a tick box per deck it's on (all ticked: every deck), the previews have a deck picker, and stairs show in light purple.

Its cores are stair cores between a deck and the one above: a frame along a part (on, at, across) and the deck it climbs from. Each is built as near there as two by three cells fit clear of both decks' halls, its foot and landing joined to corridors.

Its skin (+ skin) is how its outside looks from its windows: the plating's color and trim, how much its panels vary, how often a running light shows along its top deck, and the lights' two colors. Left out, it's grey plating with green and red lights.

The kinds of part:

  • hull: a ship's hull from its bow's tip, its length and beam picked from their ranges, and its profile: how wide it is from the bow (0) to the stern (1). Each point is a row of sliders (along, width); + point adds one in the widest gap, and the outline below shows the shape.
  • box: a rectangle from its corner (or its middle, if centered), mirrored across the centerline if ticked (a pair of sponsons from one part).
  • arm: a straight run out from a cell, east, south, west or north, staying on its line however long it's drawn: a station's arm, a ring station's bridge.
  • bridge: a straight run out from a center at any angle (degrees from east toward south), from, to cells out: a ring station's bridge from its core to its ring. A spine laid on it runs smooth along its line on any slant.
  • ring: a ring round its center; as wide as its radius, it's a disc.
  • mask: cells painted as rows of # (a cell) and . (none), or painted on the preview (see Paint below).

Every part has adds or cuts and a chance, for parts some ships have and others don't. up, down and remove reorder and take out parts, and Add a part adds one of each kind.

Its halls

Its halls are carved into the envelope in order, corridors first and then service ways round them. Each is laid on a part and follows the shape that part was drawn at, so a spine runs the length of whatever hull the seed drew.

  • Its tier is primary (a main corridor), secondary (a passageway, a cross passage) or service (a service way, its own compartment, joined to the corridors by hatches).
  • Its width is how many cells across it is.
  • Its style is the surface style it's built in (Levels > Styles; the plan's own if left as it is).

A hall two or more cells across gets what that style's halls have standing along them, at every cell along it: frames across it (posts against both walls and a beam), arches, a colonnade down the middle (two rows from three cells across) or pillars against the walls, frames if the style has none, each with a lamp etched up its faces toward the hall in the style's hall glow. Nothing stands in a doorway or cuts the way through. The carrier's and strike carrier's main passages are two cells wide (frames, and arches in industrial), and so is the spoke station's hub concourse (a colonnade, in public).

The kinds of hall:

  • spine: along the part's line, from from to to (shares of its length; past 0 or 1 reaches into the parts beside it), at an offset across it (+ is right of it looking along it), or counted in from the edge (1 the outermost row).
    • mirrored adds a second at the opposite offset: a pair of passageways.
    • inset keeps that many cells of envelope either side for rooms, so a passageway stops where the hull narrows.
    • meets halls runs on past its ends to meet a hall carved before it: a bridge to the rings either end.
    • shortest leaves it out when it would be shorter than that share of its part, so a passageway that has room only beside a sponson isn't left stranded.
  • cross: straight across the part at a share of its length, out to its reach either side, or (unticked) as far as the envelope allows less its inset.
  • ring: round a ring part, from outside cells in from its outside, or (unticked) the middle of its width, and across meters between its walls. It's built smooth round its bend rather than stepped on the grid, on every cell the bend touches, so a wider ring hall takes more cells; rooms beside it open onto it through a short throat.

Its sections

Its sections are where the bulkheads cut it. There are two ways to do it:

  • bulkheads across a part: sliders for where each is along it (a share of its length), + bulkhead halfway along the longest section, and how many cells each may jitter by seed.
  • each part its own section: a station's hub, each arm, each bridge. With each part its own section, a ring can be cut at east, south, west or north (tick two or more): bulkheads out from its center where it runs square to the grid, each piece after the first a section of its own with the ring's district.

districts say what each section is, in order.

The preview tints each section, and draws the halls (main corridors palest, service ways brown), bulkheads in red, pressure doors (where a corridor crosses a bulkhead) in yellow and hatches in orange. Every section a service way passes gets a hatch onto one of its corridors, through a short stub if need be.

Below the size are the plan's problems for that seed: a section with no corridor, halls that don't join the rest, a service way with no corridor near enough for a hatch.

Its envelope

Its envelope shows what the plan makes for four seeds. Click one to see it bigger, with its size, floor area, sections, compartments and doors. Reroll shows other seeds. Hover over a part to outline its cells (green where it adds, red where it cuts), or over a hall to outline it in blue. An envelope in more than one piece, or one more than 800 m across, shows in red. + New plan starts from a plain hull; Copy and Remove the plan work as elsewhere.

Over the bigger one, you pick what a press does:

  • Look: nothing; just look.
  • Paint: draw a hull freehand! Pick the mask to paint into (one on the deck shown, or a new mask that adds or cuts, made with your first stroke on that deck alone). Drag to paint cells in, right-drag to rub them out; the stroke is one change, one undo. A mirrored mask paints both sides of the centerline at once. The view widens while painting, so you can paint out past the hull; it grows to follow.
  • Pins: drag a pin where it goes. It holds to the part under it, the way a stair core does (along a line part as a share of its length and cells across; round a ring as cells from its center, growing with it), so it follows the part however a seed draws it.

show draws a check over it, on the level built for that seed:

  • sections: as above.
  • compartments: each its own tint: what a breach seals, between pressure doors.
  • could be lost: green for a compartment a breach could seal for good (the mission can still be finished without it), red for one it can't (the way through, a keycard, the airlock), grey for one in a district the recipe never loses.
  • rooms per corridor: each corridor run (the stretches of one hall) by the rooms opening onto it, the count on it: red none, amber one or two, green three or more, so a bare passage stands out.
  • the way through: the mission's route, in at the airlock, each gate's keycard (key n), the hack, and out.

Which mission does it build with? The first mission laid out by plans that names this plan (else the first laid out by plans, made to use it), so its airlock, keycards and way through are the game's. If the mission can't be laid out by it that seed, the checks that need one say why.

Its pins and rooms

Its pins are set pieces held in place.

  • A room pin cuts a room of its archetype round the plot cell nearest it: the bridge at the bow, the hangar across the stern. One naming its district's anchor's archetype is that section's anchor.
  • The boarding pin boards the squad at the airlock (or hangar) nearest it, rather than the one nearest an end.

+ room pin and + boarding pin add one halfway along part 1 on the deck shown and switch to Pins so you can drag it. Each lists its kind, part and deck, and a room pin its archetype.

Its rooms builds the picked seed's level: the space the halls leave in each section is cut into rooms from its district (see Districts), each room's doors facing its hall, and every room and stretch of hall built as a room module. It says how many rooms, stretches of hall and doorways it has, how many cells are left as void (too small for a room, or with no way in from a hall), and how long each stage took.

The plan above draws the rooms' walls, doors in white and suite doors (through the room in front) in grey. Below, the whole level stands on a stage, looked at from above with the ceilings open. A level with more than a stage can draw at once leaves out its smallest pieces (furniture, fittings), and says how many.

Want to check a plan without opening the window? --mode levels --select <plan> --seed <n> --plan-png <file.png> draws its structure as a picture, prints its problems and exits (exit code 1 if it has any, or its envelope is in more than one piece).

Districts

What each district of a plan is made of, in districts.json. A plan's section names its district. Its rooms are cut from the space its halls leave, across each plot's length so their walls line up along the hall, each picked from the district's rooms by weight.

  • anchor: the set piece the section is built round (a mess, a cargo hold, the bridge), put in the section's biggest plot where it's deepest. Its width can go up to 16 cells, and it isn't split across either way below that, so a hangar can fill a band from the hull to a passageway (a carrier's is [16, 16]: its whole plot). It may be a tall archetype (the hangar, two stories open to its ceiling): it stands its full height where no deck is over it, and a story tall, with a problem saying so, where one is.
  • rooms: each an archetype (picked from those one story high: its style, furnishing and tags; its shape comes from the plot), its width (the fewest and most cells it takes along its hall), its weight, whether it runs (three to six in a row the same width, like cabins), and whether it's private (somewhere you go into rather than through, so it rarely gets a second door onto another hall; a shared room usually does).

Below the form are the plans whose sections use it (click one to go to it). + New district, Copy and Remove the district work as elsewhere.

--mode levels --select <name> opens Studio on the tag with that name. If there's no such tag, it opens the style, archetype, rule (a furnishing's rule as furnishing/rule), prop, network, loot assignment, typing rule, plan or district with that id.

Bodies: animation on a turntable, the graph and the rig

Want to check a blend, a pop or a foot sliding without starting the game? That's what this mode is for. Bodies mode puts a body type (an animation graph, content/anim/<id>.json; see animation-graphs.md) on a model of its rig (content/characters), playing the rig's clips the way the game blends them.

It has four tabs:

  • Preview to watch it;
  • Graph to edit its graph;
  • Rig to edit its rig;
  • Reloads to edit the reloads' steps.

The graph and the rig are data like everything else: saved with Ctrl+S, undone with Ctrl+Z, and reloaded when they change on disk (unless you have unsaved changes to them here). The preview's sliders aren't saved; they're just for looking.

  • The bar: the body type, the model, an enemy Variant of the body type (its model, playing its own clips: Behavior > Enemies, clips), a model to Compare it with, and the gun it's holding. The gun's grips come from the receiver's markers (weapons.md), and its frame's grip picks the clips (a hand cannon uses its Pistol_ ones, see animation-graphs.md). The view's toggles are on the row below.

    • Compare stands a second model of the rig next to the first, playing the same graph with the same sliders and gun. Each one keeps its own build as the clips play (the rig's translation per bone, see rigs.md), so you can see a clip on a soldier and a lankier body side by side.
    • Turn spins it (drag to rotate it by hand, and use the mouse wheel to get closer).
    • Body hides the mesh. Bones draws the skeleton (bones in an IK chain are orange). IK targets draws what the IK reaches for in yellow: the gun's fore grip, the floor under each foot. Hit shapes draws the shapes shots hit on top of the body, so you can select and drag them (see below).
    • Pause and Time slow it down so you can look closely. Step raises (or lowers) the floor under the left foot, so you can see the foot land on it and the hips drop.

  • The parameters (on the right): every parameter the graph declares, as a slider over its range or a checkbox for a flag (hover over one to see what it means). In the game, the client sets them from what the body is doing. Here YOU set them, so the left hand only lets go when leftHandOnGun is off.

    Below them are the graph's Actions. Play one to see it once over its length (a reload lasts as long as the held gun's reload), drag its slider to hold it at a point, or Stop it. While an action plays, the left hand lets go of the gun the same way it does in the game (or follows the gun's reload steps). Defaults resets them.

    Above the parameters are the clips being played in place of the graph's (Pistol_Idle for Idle), if the gun's grip or the variant swaps any. Below, the events its clips passed (footsteps), with when they happened and how heavy they were.

  • The clip timeline (under the body): shows one of the graph's clip nodes or actions, picked above it. A clip the graph plays in two places counts as two nodes, each with its own events. An action is shown on its clip, with its events stored as a fraction of it so they stay in place however long it lasts. The timeline shows the frames (a long tick every five), the playhead, and the clip node's events (a footstep) as diamonds.

    • Click or drag on it to move the playhead. Tick Clip alone to pose the body in just that clip at the playhead (no blending, no IK, so you can see exactly where an event falls), or Play it.
    • Drag an event to another frame. It snaps to 30 frames a second, the rate the clips are keyed at, and is written when you let go, as one change to undo. Right-click an event to remove it. Click it to rename it or type its time in seconds. + Event adds an event with the name next to it at the playhead.
    • A selected event also shows what it does: the Sound it plays (from the game's cues), its gain, the Effect it shows (dust, sparks) and At, where on the body it happens (one of the rig's sockets, or the feet). The game plays these as bodies pass the event.
    • Events belong to the graph, so a change to one is a change to the graph file, saved with Ctrl+S and undone with Ctrl+Z.

  • Read again: the graph, the rig, the clips and the models are reloaded when they change on disk (exported from Blender, or the graph's file edited), and the sliders are kept. If a file doesn't load, Studio says why.

You can open straight onto what you want to look at, too. --mode bodies --body humanoid --set speed=4.5 --action reload=0.4 opens on a body running and reloading, held at four tenths of the way through (--action reload plays it instead). A few more:

  • --body humanoid:lancer shows it on the lancer's model;
  • --compare player puts the players' body next to it;
  • --holding hand_cannon puts a gun in its hands (none for nothing);
  • --variant lancer shows an enemy variant;
  • --tab graph (or rig) opens on that tab;
  • --hit-shapes ticks Hit shapes.

The graph editor

If you've used Unreal's anim graph, you'll feel right at home: the Graph tab lets you wire the graph the same way. Poses flow left to right into the Output Pose, which is the pose the body is drawn in. Each node is a box with its output on the right and its inputs down its left side.

Each input is named for what it takes: a layer's Base pose and Layered pose, an additive's Base, Additive and Reference pose, IK's Pose, a select's states when dead ... otherwise, a blend's points at 1.8. A select or blend has a + to add a new state or point.

Next to the canvas, the body plays the graph and shows every change right away, with the parameters as sliders beside it (Try it) and the actions you can play, so you can see what each one does. Below them is the selected node, for editing. If the graph can't be read (for example an input left empty, shown in orange), it says why, and the body keeps playing the last version that worked.

So how do you read the humanoid's graph? From the right:

  1. The Output Pose is fed by the select between dead, downed and alive.
  2. Alive is the hands on the gun (reach),
  3. fed by the aim (look),
  4. fed by the feet planted on the floor (ground),
  5. fed by the added leans,
  6. fed by the slot where the body's actions play (a reload over the legs),
  7. fed by blind fire's layer and the arms' hold,
  8. fed by the legs' walk and run blends.

Each step works on the pose from the one to its left.

  • Placing: drag a node to put it anywhere. Its position is saved in the file (pos), but the game doesn't read it. A node you've never placed sits to the left of the node it feeds and follows it around. Right-click empty canvas and pick Tidy up to lay them all out again.
  • Wiring: drag from a node's output onto another node's input to plug it in. Whatever was plugged in there before comes out and is left loose where it was, and the spot the moved node came from is left empty. You can also drag from an input onto a node to plug that node in. A wire turns green where it's allowed and red where it isn't (a node can never feed itself). Alt-click an input to unplug it.
  • New nodes: right-click empty canvas to add a loose node there. It's faded and marked "not plugged in", and kept in the file (loose) until you plug it in or delete it. To add one already plugged in, drop a wire from an input onto empty canvas, or click an empty input or a +. Dropping a wire from an output onto empty canvas wraps that node inside a new one (a layer over it, IK on it...).
  • Right-click a node to wrap it inside another kind, unwrap it (its first input takes its place), unplug its output, delete it (or press Delete; whatever fed it is left loose), copy it, or paste over it.
  • Drag empty canvas (or use the middle mouse button) to pan, use the wheel to zoom, and double-click empty canvas to see everything.

The selected node, next to the canvas:

  • Its fields: a clip node's clip (from the rig), its time (its clock, the ground covered, its end, a number or a parameter) and the rest; a layer's mask; IK's chain, target and socket; and any weight, axis, pitch or yaw, as a number or a parameter.
  • Its points or states: each point's position on the axis, and each state's condition (a parameter on or off, or otherwise for the last one). You can edit them (Edit selects one), move them up or down, or remove them. + Point or + State adds one.
  • Put inside...: wraps the node inside a new layer, additive, reach, look, ground, select or blend, with the node underneath and the rest at defaults for you to set. Take out does the reverse: the first node under it takes its place. Make a clip replaces it, and everything under it, with a clip.
  • The graph: what its body can do, its parameters and targets (above): the graph's own fields. Its rig, its clips file and its groundSpeed are picked from what exists, not typed. Its capabilities are what a body of this type can visibly do, picked from fire, reload, throw, crouch, lean and blindFire. An enemy variant's behavior can only need these, and fire, reload and throw also need an action with that name. Then its parameters (with their ranges for the sliders) and the targets its IK reaches for.
  • The body's actions (played into the slot): its actions, each with a name (the game's name: reload, throw, fire, use), the rig clip it plays and the mask it covers (picked, not typed), and its fadeIn and fadeOut. The graph's single Slot (actions) node is where they play. If the game asks for an action the graph doesn't have, nothing shows. A gun's grip can play its own clip for one (Pistol_Reload).

The rig editor

The Rig tab edits the body type's rig (content/rigs/<id>.json) next to its skeleton as the file describes it. Each bone is drawn from its head to its tail (bones in an IK chain are orange), its sockets and eyes are yellow, its size is shown as grey rings, and the shapes shots hit are red (see below). Pick a mask to see its bones in green, or a bone to see it in white.

Its fields are:

  • the bones, each listed after its parent, with a name, parent, head and tail, and its translation. The translation is where the bone's offset from its parent comes from when a clip plays on a model: skeleton (the model's own; the default, shown until you set one), scaled (the clip's, scaled to the model; used for the hips) or animation (the clip's, unchanged);
  • sockets;
  • IK chains (their bones, pole and target);
  • masks (from some bones, except others);
  • the mask hidden in first person;
  • the body's numbers;
  • the clips.

Models made on the rig still have to match it: the same bones under the same parents, each pointing the same way as the rig's, and the eye where the rig has it (the bones can be longer or shorter). The Blender add-on builds the armature from this file and checks models against it (rigs.md).

Want a second kind of body, like a quadruped? That's a new rig file and a new graph, made here or by copying the humanoid's.

Reloads

The Reloads tab edits content/defs/reloads.json: what the left hand and the magazine do over a reload, for every gun of the frames it names (the frames' model, typed with a comma between), whatever its parts. Each reload is a table of steps:

  • to is where the hand goes (grip, grab, out, drop, pouch, belowWell, well, charge);
  • until is the share of the reload it ends at (0 to 1, so a faster reload speeds all of it);
  • magazine is where the magazine is meanwhile (in, hand, gone);
  • from empty marks a step only done after the last round (the bolt pulled).

+ adds a step after one, the same, and - removes it. Every change plays right away on the Preview tab's body (with its reloading and reloadProgress sliders).

One gotcha: this tab has its own Save and Read again. It isn't saved with Ctrl+S or undone with Ctrl+Z.

Fitting the shapes shots hit

Shots hit the simulation's shapes, never the mesh: a head sphere around the eye and a body capsule under it, standing or crouched. They come from the humanoid rig's body, so a designer fits them here. The settings are:

  • headRadius (the sphere's size);
  • headAbove and crouchHeadAbove (how far the sphere's center is above the eye, standing and crouched);
  • hitRadius (the capsule's radius, no wider than radius).

The Rig tab draws them in red, standing, with the crouched version beside it. The game, Studio's sandbox and Studio's level play all use the shapes as you edit them (once saved, the game reads them when it starts).

On the Preview tab, tick Hit shapes to see them on the playing body, standing or crouched and leaning according to the sliders. They're drawn on top of the body, so they show through it. The head sphere and body capsule are red, the eye from the animation is a cyan ring, and the game's eye is an orange diamond (they sit on top of each other when the clip is right). Hover over one to highlight it, and click to select it:

  • The head sphere: drag its center (or its outline) up or down to set how far above the eye it sits, standing or crouched depending on what's shown. Drag the dot on its edge in or out to resize it.
  • The body capsule: drag the dot on its edge (or its outline) in or out to set its radius.
  • The eyes: when selected, they say what they are. You can't drag either one (the game's eye belongs to the simulation, and the drawn one comes from the clip).

The numbers are also under the parameters (Hit shapes), as sliders, with the selected one highlighted. Every change goes into the rig (content/rigs/humanoid.json, saved with Ctrl+S and undone with Ctrl+Z), so it applies to every humanoid, and Studio starts shooting at the new shape right away. Selecting a shape stops the turntable; tick Turn to start it again.

Under the parameters, Eye drawn ... cm up, ... cm across shows how far apart the two eyes are. It turns orange past 5 cm up or 10 cm across. Why does that matter? A clip marked "eye": "standing" or "crouched" in the rig has to keep the eye there (the client's tests and the Blender add-on's Check both measure it), because a head drawn away from the simulation's eye gets shot where it isn't drawn. So fix the clip (move its hips), not the shapes. The shapes are for setting the size of the head and helmet.

Behavior: how enemies fight

An enemy is a variant (Soldier, Lancer...) that thinks using a behavior. Both are data in content/defs/ai. A variant's body and model are what it's drawn as (Bodies mode shows them).

The behavior tree

A behavior's tree decides what a soldier does at each moment. Every tick, the soldier runs it from the root. Each node either succeeds or fails:

  • Flow nodes run their children top to bottom. A Selector stops at the first child that succeeds ("the first one that works"). A Sequence stops at the first child that fails ("all of them, in order"). Put conditions first and an action after them, and it reads as if this, then do that. Do all runs every child no matter what each one returns. Not flips its one child's result.

  • Conditions check things and don't change anything. For example: the soldier's awareness (idle, alerted, engaging, hunting, searching), whether it sees an enemy, its distance to the enemy, a value it weighs (its health, the range...), how many of its squad are still standing, whether the level has cover, and its current intent.

  • Actions do things, using the game's own code: Roam (at the start of the level, soldiers wander around their post), Watch the enemy, Choose intent, Hold cover, Bound forward, Push the enemy, Fall back, Fight in the open, Clear rooms, Go to the enemy, Search where he went, Look around, Top up the magazine, Throw a grenade and Dodge grenades. (Fight from cover combines the four cover actions in one node, for a short tree.)

    An action can fail: Clear rooms when there are no rooms to clear, Bound forward when it isn't the soldier's turn or there's no cover ahead, Search where he went when there's nowhere left to look. When that happens, a selector just moves on to its next child.

How a soldier notices the enemy (idle, then alerted, engaging, hunting, searching) is handled by the game's code, and the tree reads it with Awareness is.

Searching for a lost enemy

So the squad has lost track of you. Where do they look? Going straight to where you were last seen is the obvious answer, but you're probably not there anymore! Search where he went looks for him where he most likely went, not just where he was.

From where he was last seen and the direction he was moving, the squad guesses places:

  • just past each doorway around him (more likely the more it lies in his direction, and the closer it is);
  • straight ahead along his path;
  • and where he was.

Its setting, rooms out (2), is how far from where he was it guesses: 1 for the neighboring rooms, 2 for those plus the rooms past them.

The squad splits up the guesses. Each soldier takes the most likely one near it that no squadmate has taken, goes there with its gun up, and looks around before marking it checked. The node fails once every guess has been checked. In the shipped tree, the soldier then looks around and stands down after a while. Seeing him ends the search, and hearing a shot restarts it from where the shot was.

A Check a value on searchLeads (the places left to check) branches on whether the squad still has somewhere to look. How long a soldier looks around once its squad has nothing left to check, and the longest it searches at all, are tuning values (the Tuning tab, below: "Look round after search", "Search at most").

Tuning: how hard each side is

The Tuning tab is the soldiers' difficulty (content/defs/ai/tuning.json). It shows every value, grouped (Awareness, Lethality, Tactics, Searching, Indoors, Taking hits, At ease), and hovering over one tells you what it does. These are all of the game's F3 overlay AI sliders, as data. Difficulty comes from reactions, aim and coordination, never from health. No bullet sponges here!

Tuning is per side. Shared holds the values every side's soldiers fight with. Pick Station (the garrison: enemy variants with faction hostile) or Choir (the lancers, who fight alongside the horde) to see that side's values.

  • Tick a value to change it for that side only (it turns amber), and untick it to go back to the shared value.
  • "Only what this side changes" lists just those, and "Share every value" resets them all.
  • In the file, soldiers holds the shared values and factions holds what each side changes.

Every edit is saved and undone like any other, and the Sandbox tab's soldiers use it right away, so spawn an enemy from the side you're tuning to try it.

The game reads the file when it starts. Its F3 overlay (AI) tunes a side live while it fights. Copy AI values copies what that side changes to the clipboard, in the same format as the file, so you can paste it under factions.

Scoring: choosing an intent

Choose intent is the one action that weighs things up. It scores four things a soldier could do from its cover and picks the best:

  • hold it;
  • advance to cover closer to the enemy;
  • push the enemy while he's vulnerable;
  • or fall back.

It doesn't move the soldier or change the tree's flow. It only records the intent, about every half second, and always succeeds. The nodes after it act on the intent. In the shipped tree that's a selector of Intent is push then Push the enemy, Intent is advance then Bound forward, Intent is fall back then Fall back, and finally Hold cover. In Unreal's terms, Choose intent is a service writing to the blackboard, and Intent is is a decorator reading it.

Scoring is utility AI. Its numbers are the behavior's scoring, under Scoring on the toolbar. Here's how a score gets built:

  • Each intent starts at its base (or its baseByRole: a rifleman advances more readily than a suppressor).
  • It adds its terms. These are weighted votes, not if-blocks. A term is a weight times a curve over one input (for example range from 20 to 30 m: nothing at 20, the full weight at 30 and beyond), or a weight by role. A term only counts when its when conditions are true (targetHealth < 0.6).
  • It's multiplied by its factors. These are curves too (when pinned, a soldier advances at 0.4 of its score), or values by role.
  • An intent whose gates aren't all true scores 0 (nobody pushes an enemy they can't see).
  • The highest score wins. The soldier's current intent gets the stickiness (0.1) added on top, so it doesn't keep flipping between two close scores. The term that added the most is the reason shown over its head.

The inputs a curve or condition can read are:

  • ownHealth and targetHealth, from 0 to 1 (the target's is -1 when it's out of sight);
  • range, in meters, and sinceSeen, in seconds;
  • suppressed, coveringFire, targetReloading and squadPushing, each 0 or 1;
  • advanceBlocked, which is 1 when the soldier isn't allowed to bound forward right now: either the soldiers further back go first, or it recently found no cover ahead. The shipped advance has a gate advanceBlocked < 1, so a soldier that can't move up holds instead, and its label says so. It also has a gate range > 12, so a soldier that's close enough to fight from where it is holds there (a push at a vulnerable enemy still closes in);
  • searchLeads, how many of the squad's guesses about where a lost enemy went haven't been checked yet (0 when they've all been checked, or none were made);
  • role;
  • score, the intent's total so far, for a term that only adds to something already there.

Personalities

Each squad gets a personality, so no two squads have to fight quite the same. It sets:

  • a scale on each intent's score;
  • how many soldiers in a squad of three or more go around the side (flankers);
  • cohesion: how far, in meters toward the enemy, its soldiers can get ahead of or behind the rest of the squad when they take cover (10 unless set).

The game picks one for each squad by share, based on the squad's number and the level. The shipped ones are:

  • steady: half the squads; the scores unchanged, cohesion 10;
  • aggressive: cohesion 14;
  • cautious: no flankers, cohesion 7;
  • flanking: two flankers, cohesion 12.

How a squad moves up

So how does Bound forward actually work? The squad leapfrogs:

  1. The soldier furthest back goes first, to cover near or a little ahead of the squad's lead (a dash of up to 16 m, or however far it takes to catch up to the lead), while the rest cover it.
  2. The suppressor stays back as the base of fire and doesn't take a turn, and a soldier that finds no cover ahead passes its turn to the next one.
  3. Then the next soldier furthest back goes, and so on.

A few rules keep it tidy:

  • At most a third of the squad is moving at once.
  • Nobody takes cover further ahead of the rest than the cohesion allows, though a bound can always come up level with the lead.
  • A soldier that doesn't have a turn, or has no cover ahead, holds. One that has just bounded (and so isn't at the back anymore) holds where it ended up.
  • A soldier on its way to cover gets there before it thinks again. A new role or intent doesn't turn it around halfway; only the cover no longer protecting it, getting stuck, or falling back does.
  • Within 12 m of the enemy, the squad stops advancing and fights from where it is.
  • Push the enemy is the only thing that deliberately leaves the squad. Afterwards the soldier takes the cover closest to the squad that it can.

Roles (who suppresses, who flanks) rotate every roleSeconds (6), set in the scoring's general settings. A flanker that's still on its way around keeps its role until it gets there, and only calls out "Flanking left/right!" as it sets off.

The scoring graph

The scoring graph reads left to right, the same way a score is built:

  • the inputs;
  • the conditions, terms and factors, with their curves drawn;
  • the four intents, each with three pins: requires all of (its conditions), adds (its terms) and multiplied by (its factors);
  • the personalities, and the choice.

A term's or factor's own conditions go on its only if all of pin. Hover over a node to read what it does in plain words ("adds 0.35 while covering fire is 1"). Double-click a Choose intent node in the tree to open its scoring. A behavior made from scratch has no scoring until its tree uses Choose intent. Give it scoring starts it as a copy of the soldier's.

It's put together the same way as the tree:

  • Right-click the canvas for the palette: a term, factor, condition or personality. A new term, factor or condition starts loose: dimmed, marked "not wired", and doing nothing in the game.
  • Wire it in. Drag from its right-hand pin onto an intent's or term's pin. Drag from an input onto a curve or condition to make it read that input. Let go on empty canvas to add a node that reads it.
  • Edit a curve by its keys, like in Unreal's curve editor. Click a term or factor and its curve is drawn large in the panel. Drag a key to move it, double-click to add a key at the mouse, right-click a key to remove it (a curve always keeps at least two), and click a key to type its exact input and value. The curve runs in a straight line between keys and stays flat past the first and last. Two keys at the same input make a step. In the file, a keyed curve is keys ([[20, 0], [25, 0.1], [30, 1]]). A curve written as from, to, low and high still works, and turns into keys the first time you edit it.
  • Take it out (leave it loose), on a node's buttons, unwires it without deleting it. Remove the curve turns a term into just its weight.
  • Click a node to edit it. The panel uses plain names for fields ("how often" for a personality's share, "value at end" for a curve's high), and each field's help gives its name in the file. What a node reads is set by its wire, not picked from a list.
  • Drag a node to move it. Node positions are saved in the file. Once you change anything, every node stays where it is.
  • Tidy up (on the right-click menu) lays everything out again and routes the wires like a circuit diagram. Each input's wires leave it horizontally and run down a lane of their own (the top input's lane is furthest right, so no wire crosses another input's lane to reach its own), then turn off horizontally at the pin they feed, going around any node in the way. The intents' wires into the choice share one lane next to it. The top of each lane is a reroute you can branch more wires from, and a wire's corners show up when the mouse is near, so you can drag them. Ctrl+Z undoes a Tidy up.
  • Double-click a wire to put a reroute node on it, then drag the node to lead the wire around other nodes. Like in Unreal's blueprints, you can drag from a reroute's small pin on its right to wire what it carries into something else too, so one input can feed several curves and conditions through one reroute. Right-click a reroute to remove it (the wires through it go straight again).

Node positions, reroutes and loose nodes are stored in the behavior file (x, y, via, inputVia, reroutes, layout, loose) only for Studio. The game never reads them, and they cost nothing while the AI runs.

The tree editor

The Behaviors tab is the editor. It shows the tree on a canvas, and on the right a view of the sandbox that follows the selected soldier.

  • Right-click the canvas for the palette of nodes, grouped into Flow, Conditions and Actions. Type to search, and Enter picks the first match.
  • Drag from a flow node's right-hand pin to another node's left pin to make it a child. If you let go on empty canvas, the palette opens so you can add a new child there.
  • Children run top to bottom: drag a node up or down to change its order. The small number next to each wire is its position.
  • Click a node to edit it on the right: what it does, and its settings. Settings are picked from lists, never typed: an awareness, a value and a comparison, a distance, an action. You can also give it a note, or rename its id. The buttons, also on a node's right-click menu, are Add a child, Make it the root, Take it from its parent, Duplicate and Delete. The Delete key removes the selected node, and its children stay behind, loose. Loose nodes do nothing until they're wired in under the root.
  • Drag the background (or use the middle mouse button) to pan, and use the wheel to zoom. Tidy lays the tree out again, and Fit brings all of it into view. Node positions are saved with the nodes.

Next to the selected behavior, Studio shows what it needs of a body: the things its tree's actions make a soldier do that a body has to be able to show (Hold cover: fire, reload, crouch, lean and fire blind; Throw grenade: throw). An enemy variant that thinks with it needs a body type that can do those things (Bodies, Graph tab: what its body can do). The Enemies tab flags a variant whose body can't, and the game logs it.

+ New behavior makes one from scratch. Give it a name, and you get a root with nothing under it yet. Duplicate copies the one shown. Delete removes it at the next save. The soldier's own behavior can't be deleted, and Studio warns you if a variant still uses the one you're deleting.

See it live. This is the fun part! Select a soldier, either in the Sandbox tab or by clicking it in the view. The view follows it, and the editor shows the tree that soldier is running, lit up with what it's doing at each moment: green for succeeded, red for failed, grey for not reached. Think as this behavior gives the selected soldier the behavior shown, so you can try a tree on it right away. In the Sandbox tab, Thinks as spawns a whole squad with a given behavior. Every edit reaches the soldiers immediately, before you save.

Enemy variants

The Enemies tab lists the variants (enemies.json). Each one has:

  • a behavior and a faction, picked from lists (hostile soldiers, or the choir that fights everyone);
  • a body: health, the damage it deals, grenades, run and sprint speeds, and whether it drops loot;
  • weapons: how often it carries each frame (the archetypes in Armory; a new frame shows up as an option as soon as it exists);
  • spawns: how often a squad from each source is this variant. The sources are a level's squads, the mission's reinforcements, the director's patrols, and the choir that comes with the horde;
  • its body type and model, and its own clips: each of the body's clips it replaces (picked from the rigs' clips) and the clip it plays instead, all from the rig's clips made in Blender. A gun's grip is applied first, so to change how the variant holds a pistol, replace Pistol_Idle, not Idle. Bodies mode shows it under Variant. The tab flags a clip name no rig has, and a behavior that needs something its body can't show (a behavior that throws grenades on a body that can't throw).

A squad is all one variant, picked by those weights.

So you want to add an enemy? Click + New, give it an id and a name, pick its behavior (make a new one under Behaviors first, if you want), then set its guns and where it spawns. That's it! In the Sandbox tab, Enemy spawns a squad of any variant.

The sandbox

The sandbox is the game's grey-box range, seen from a free camera. Hold the right mouse button to look around. W, A, S and D fly, Q and E go down and up, and Shift goes faster. On the left, the Sandbox tab says what a click in the world places:

  • a squad with the selected size, enemy variant, behavior and personality (or picked by share, as in the game). It hunts the target, or stands at ease like at the start of a level (untick Hunting);

  • a horde of the selected size, coming up out of the floor around the click (it fights everyone);

  • the target: the player, standing where you click, who can't be hurt. Tick The target strafes to give the soldiers a moving enemy;

  • cover: a box, either low (a soldier crouches behind it and rises over it to shoot) or high (he stands and leans out), placed with its broad side facing the target. Click a box to select it, and drag to move it. R rotates it (Shift: the other way) and Delete removes it. The panel sets its exact position, size and rotation, switches it between low and high, or duplicates it.

    Soldiers treat the boxes like the level's own walls and crates. They're solid, block sight, get walked around, and are used as cover as soon as you let go. Show the cover points marks every spot a soldier can choose: a yellow ring for low cover, blue for high, with a tick pointing toward the cover, brighter where a soldier is using it. The test cover isn't saved. It's for trying out the AI, not for making levels.

Paused and Speed slow the fight down so you can watch it. Clear the soldiers and the horde removes everyone. Over every soldier is what it's doing and why (in the intent's color), along with its squad's personality and its role. Click one to "read its mind" on the right: its inputs, each intent's score (and the stickiness), and what each personality would choose in its place at that moment.

Save writes the behavior files, enemies.json and tuning.json, and the game reads them when it starts. Shipped copies of the soldier's behavior and the variants are also built into the game, in case a file is missing or broken.

Look: the surface materials

The library of surface materials, and what each number does, is described in materials.md. Look mode lists the library down the left. Pick a material to see it on the sphere, the crate and the gun barrel, with a row of every material behind them to compare it with. Change its numbers and it updates right away, both there and on every model Studio draws.

  • Light picks the room's lighting, and model's color sets the shapes' color.
  • The room's reflections are re-captured whenever the shapes change, so a mirror-smooth metal shows the room right away. Under the flashlight, though, the room it reflects is dark, because the beam isn't included in reflections.
  • Drag in the room to look around, and use the wheel to get closer.
  • --mode look --select bare_metal --light flashlight opens on bare metal in the dark (station, flashlight, alarm, sodium or outdoors).

A material ticked space is a window onto space: it shows the sky (below) whatever its color. One ticked outside is out in space, on a ship's or station's outside (hull_plating, the skin seen from a window): it's lit by the sector's sun, not a room's fill, and no room's haze lies over it.

Skies

The Skies tab tunes the skies windows show (content/defs/skies.json), looked at from inside, drawn by the game's own shader. Pick a sky on the left, and these are all sliders and color pickers:

  • its stars (density, brightness, color);
  • its nebula (strength, scale, the color of its thick parts and of its thin edges);
  • its band, the galaxy's disc (strength, width, tilt, color);
  • its sun (intensity, size, glow, elevation, color).

Seen from is the preview's own, not saved: where across and down the sector's map the system is (the sun goes once round the sky across the map, and swings up or down 10 degrees down it) and the level seed its clouds and stars come from.

Sectors is the order the fleet meets them in, one a sector from the first, going round again past the last: move one up, remove it, or add the selected sky. Add, Duplicate and Remove work as elsewhere; renaming a sky renames it in the sector order. Drag to look round, and use the wheel to narrow the view. --mode look --sky violet_shoal opens on the Skies tab looking at Violet Shoal.

Models: what each model is collided with

What a placed model is collided with (by characters, and by shots, sight, cover and the navigation grid alike) is set per model in content/props/colliders.json, beside the models; see ../plans/model-colliders.md.

Models mode lists the prop models down the left by file, each with its collider ((file's) when it comes from its file's default). It also marks a model not placed when it's neither furniture (a prop in props.json names it) nor a station fixture (a mission template names it as a tank kind, the reservoir, its outlet or valve, or the airlock's intake or chute), so nothing sets it into a level.

Pick one to set Every model in its file (the file's default) and This model (its own, or as its file):

  • authored: the boxes in its file (..._CollisionOnly). The default.
  • mesh: what's drawn, exactly: for irregular furniture (a desk's hutch, a terminal's screen).
  • box and cylinder: one box, or one upright cylinder, around what's drawn: a cylinder for barrels and drums.
  • none: nothing; walked and shot through.

The model turns on the stage with its collider's outline over it in cyan, built exactly as the generator sets it into the level, as furniture or as a fixture. Orange marks surfaces characters step up onto: walkable, no higher than a step (0.4 m), and with room above them for a crouching character.

Under it, it says what it's made of and what's worth fixing:

  • why it won't do (an authored model whose solid isn't a box);
  • surfaces to step up onto;
  • a mesh that isn't closed (shots and sight only hit a face from the front, so they pass through a hole);
  • and a piece that breaks given a mesh, which keeps its box (it breaks through one solid part).

Every change reaches the furniture Studio builds levels from right away, saved or not, and Save writes colliders.json. --mode models --select furniture/Desk opens on the desk.

What it does not do yet

Studio covers a lot, but not everything. A few things still live outside it:

  • What a soldier drops besides its gun (a medkit or an ammo pack, one time in five, and a weapon part, 6% of the time) is still code. The full list of what's still code is in adding-content.md.
  • The running game doesn't pick up a save. Restart the mission (or the game) to play with the new data.
  • Behavior mode builds trees from the actions and conditions that already exist. A new kind of action (a new way to move or fight) needs code. The horde's behavior is still tuned in code (HordeTuning). The soldiers' (how they notice the enemy, their aim, reactions and perception) is data, in the Tuning tab.
  • There's no mode for the sound data yet.
