Architecture

The big idea here is that Core, Ecs, Simulation, AI, Loot, Content, Physics and ProcGen are headless. They never reference windowing, rendering, audio or UI. Why bother? Because it keeps gameplay testable in CI without a GPU, and it lets a host run the simulation without a window later. We don't just trust ourselves on this one either: ArchitectureTests in the Simulation, AI, Loot, Content, Physics and ProcGen test projects enforce it by checking assembly references. Content stays headless so the host, the tools and physics can all load the same meshes the renderer draws.

The client is the game's composition root. It's the only place in the game that knows about both the simulation and the renderer.

Rigs (LastFleet.Content.Rigs, content/rigs/<id>.json; docs/blender/rigs.md, docs/plans/animation.md) are the skeletons bodies are modeled and animated on. They're data, so a new kind of body is just a new file! Rig.Parse reads a rig and names every problem it finds. A rig has:

  • bones, with their rest pose, each listed after its parent. Each bone has a RigTranslation that says where its offset comes from when a clip plays on a model.
  • sockets (RigSocket): the eye, the grips.
  • IK chains (RigChain): a run of bones, a pole and a RigChainTarget.
  • masks (RigMask): from some bones down, minus others. One mask is the one hidden in first person.
  • the body the simulation uses (RigBody), with the same numbers as MovementSettings and HitVolumes. Shots never hit the animated mesh. The mesh is held to these shapes instead.
  • its clips (RigClip).

Rig.SkeletonProblems checks a model's joints against the rig. RigFit.Problems checks that and also the model's eye at rest: a model's bones can be longer or shorter than the rig's, but its eye can't move.

The Blender add-on (tools/blender/lastfleet_rigs.py) builds the armature from the same file and checks models and clips (bones, weights, 30 fps, in place, loops closed). Here's how the files fit together:

  • A model (content/characters/<model>.gltf) is its meshes plus the armature.
  • A rig's clips (content/anim/<rig>.gltf) are the armature and its actions, and every model of the rig shares them.
  • The sources are Blender files in content/raw/characters/ (Git LFS): a <model>.blend for each model and a <rig>_clips.blend for each rig's clips.

You export them with the add-on's buttons, or all at once with tools/blender/export_characters.py (export_source checks a file, then writes it). The starter files (the grey-box soldier, the lancer, the player and the 22 clips) were written by tools/blender/generate_soldier.py, which won't overwrite them unless forced.

The skeletal runtime (LastFleet.Content.Skeletal) plays them:

  • SkinnedModel reads a model's skinned meshes as flat triangles in the bind pose (SkinnedVertex: position, face normal, color, four joints packed in a uint, weights), plus its Skeleton (joints ordered parents first, rest pose, inverse binds).
  • ClipSet binds a rig's clips to a skeleton by joint name. A ClipChoice swaps the clips a graph names for the ones that fit a gun's grip, for example Pistol_Idle for Idle based on WeaponArchetype.Grip, and then for an enemy variant's own EnemyVariant.Clips. AnimPlayer.Choose makes one per grip and enemy variant, and it's set on AnimInstance.Clips. SkinnedBodies and Studio's BodyStage pick them from the held gun and the variant (docs/animation-graphs.md).
  • The rig retargets the clips to the model (docs/plans/animation-2.md). A Skeleton joint's translation keys are dropped, so it keeps the model's offset. A Scaled joint's keys are scaled by the model's rest offset divided by the clips' rest offset. ClipSet.Problems names any joint that's rotated at rest compared with the clips' armature.
  • A SkeletalClip can be sampled at any time between its keys, looping or held. NodeChannel is shared with AnimatedModel's bake.
  • A Pose holds every joint's JointTransform. Poses can be blended (Blend, with a mask of joint weights from Skeleton.Mask, which gives you a layer), added to (Add: an additive clip's difference from a reference pose), and turned into the matrices that skin the mesh (Skin: inverse bind, pose, placement). None of this allocates per frame.

The renderer skins on the GPU. AddSkinnedMesh uploads a body's vertices once (SkinStore). Each frame, FrameGeometry.SkinMatrices (at most MaxSkinMatrices) go into a storage buffer, one per frame in flight. Each SkinnedDraw is one draw of its mesh, and its first instance is the index where its matrices start (content/shaders/skinned.slang, lit the same as the level; skinned_shadow.slang draws into the shadow maps).

Animation graphs (LastFleet.Content.Skeletal.Graph, content/anim/<id>.json; docs/animation-graphs.md) are data too. AnimGraph.Parse reads a body type's parameters (AnimParameter) and its tree of nodes, and checks them against the rig. The node types are:

  • clips (ClipNode): looping or held. A clip's time comes from its clock, from the ground covered at a given stride, from a parameter, or runs to its end. Clips can have events.
  • blend spaces (Blend1DNode, and Blend2DNode using gradient bands). A synced blend keeps the strides under it in phase.
  • selects (SelectNode): picks the first state whose AnimCondition holds and fades to it.
  • layers on a rig mask (LayerNode), with the layer's weight eased.
  • the slot where a body's actions play (SlotNode). It plays the graph's AnimActions, such as a reload or a throw. Each action is a clip played by the action's progress on its mask, faded in and out. A body's AnimInstance.Play says which action is playing, by name, from the bridge's HandActionKind.
  • additives (AdditiveNode).
  • IK on the rig's chains: ReachNode moves a two-bone chain's tip or socket to a target, LookNode turns a chain toward an aim, and GroundNode sets each foot on the ground under it, drops the pelvis to the lowest foot and moves it by the instance's BodyOffset, and keeps the feet planted.

IK nodes ask an IAnimWorld (passed to the player) for targets and the ground, and solve with PoseIk in model space. JointPoint is a rig socket placed on a skeleton, at the same distance from its model bone's head as the rig has it from the rig's bone.

An AnimPlayer binds a graph to a skeleton and a clip set, and poses any number of bodies into a Pose. Each body is an AnimInstance: its parameters, plus what the nodes keep for it from frame to frame (clocks, phases, the chosen state and its fade). It keeps a pair of scratch poses per depth, so a frame allocates nothing. The events a body passed during the frame are in AnimInstance.Events.

Drawing bodies. The client draws soldiers and the Choir's lancers this way. With --box-soldiers, or when a body doesn't load, it falls back to CharacterModels' boxes.

  • ModelBody loads each body type and model pair once (an enemy variant's Body and Model). GameClient uploads every variant's mesh.
  • SkinnedBodies keeps each character's instance, which way its feet are going, and any reload or throw still playing between the host's messages about it.
  • HumanoidBridge is the one place where the simulation's state becomes the graph's parameters: MovementState, Health, the aim's pitch and BodyActions. Body actions play as the graph's action with the same name (HumanoidBridge.ActionName). They come from a HandAction, which is what the hands are doing: reloading, throwing, using an item or firing. HandActions.Of works it out from the weapon, grenades and items, and it's sent to clients in two bytes that stay the same while the action lasts: its kind and length, and the tick it began.
  • BodyWorld answers the IK queries from the level's collision and the held WeaponModel. The gun hangs by its GripRight from the right hand along the aim, with the left hand on its GripLeft.

In cover, the body is held to the hit volumes. Here's the problem. Shots hit HitVolumes, which are built from the simulation's eye. CoverSystem puts the eye wherever the cover needs it (ducked below low cover, raised to see over it, leaned round an edge)... but the clips only have a crouching eye and a standing one. So what do we do about the gap?

Each frame SkinnedBodies compares the drawn eye (the rig's Eye socket) with the simulation's eye and moves the body by the difference (AnimInstance.BodyOffset, eased, limited to half a meter down, a third of a meter up and under half a meter sideways). HumanoidBridge draws a crouched body in cover with the standing clips once its eye is closer to standing height. Out of cover, the offset eases off and the clips set the pose.

When firing blind, the gun sits on the line the simulation's shots leave along (CoverSystem.Muzzle, held a little out along it) and the hands reach for it (BodyWorld.PlaceHolding):

  • Over low cover it uses the held-up BlindFire clip.
  • Round a right-hand edge it uses both hands.
  • Round a left-hand edge it uses the left hand only, because the right arm can't reach across.

The first-person gun goes out to the side by the same amount. F3's Hit volumes option draws every other character's capsule and head sphere over its body. Other players are drawn the same way, with the players' body (ModelBody.Players, player.gltf).

Your own body in first person (SkinnedBodies.AddSelf, off with --floating-gun):

  • It's the players' body under the camera, which is the simulation's eye. The body is moved sideways under it and held down with BodyOffset (feet kept planted), so its eye is never above the camera. Why? Without that, the camera drops to a crouch and dips on landing faster than the clips fade, which puts the shoulders around the camera. Not a great look!
  • Its head is shrunk to a point (the rig's FirstPersonHides).
  • Nothing within OwnCutRadius (0.2 m) of the eye is drawn (SkinnedDraw.CutRadius: skinned.slang and ray_prepass_body.slang discard it, but its shadow stays whole). In the poses the clips hold, nothing in view is that close, so this is a guard for what the animation does while moving between them.
  • The gun is where ViewModel holds it (ViewModel.Held, at a nearer hip, HeldByBody), and both hands reach for it (BodyWorld.PlaceHolding: the graph's rightGrip target and rightHandOnGun). The left hand slides back toward the magazine well when the fore grip is out of reach, and a reach's swing brings the shoulder forward.
  • It's a SkinnedDraw.Near, drawn with the view model in the near end of the depth range. The gun is then seen through the world's projection (FrameGeometry.ViewModelInWorld) so the hands and gun line up.

Studio's Bodies mode (BodiesMode, BodyStage) plays any body type's graph on a model of its rig, driven by sliders over its parameters (AnimParameter.Min, Max, Flag). It can also play it on a second model beside the first that follows it (BodyStage.Follow: Compare).

Under the stage is the ClipTimeline, where you drag, add and remove a clip node's or action's events and give them a sound, effect and socket. The body can be posed in that clip alone with BodyStage.Solo. The body is skinned on the CPU (SkinnedModel.Skin) into a ModelStage, and its IK gets a held gun (BodyWorld without collision) and a floor with a step. The shapes shots hit for the current stance (BodyHitShapes) are drawn over the stage on top of the body (ModelStage.ToScreen), and you can pick and drag them there (HitShapeEditor, which writes the rig's body).

Animation events reach the game. Each ClipEvent can name a Sound, Gain, Effect and Socket. SkinnedBodies collects the events each body passed (BodyEvent; with strides blended, each one counted once; placed at the socket or the feet), along with the characters whose graph plays step (Stepping). On the next frame GameView.PlayBodyEvents hands them to ImpactEffects.AddEffect and GameAudio.PlayBodies, which then leaves out those characters' distance-based footsteps.

The Graph tab edits a graph's file as a tree (LastFleet.Studio.Bodies):

  • GraphTree reads the JSON into GraphTreeNodes, each with its GraphSlot.
  • GraphCanvas draws them the way Unreal's anim graph does, with poses flowing left to right into the Output Pose and each node's named inputs as a GraphPin.
  • GraphWiring connects them: plugging in and out, keeping loose nodes in the file's loose list, storing positions in each node's pos, and never putting a node under itself.
  • GraphInspector edits a node's fields, offering the rig's and the graph's names (GraphVocabulary).
  • GraphEdits wraps and unwraps nodes, and adds and moves points and states.

The Rig tab edits a rig's file (JsonEditor) beside its skeleton (RigPreview). Both are data workspaces like the loot's (RigWorkspace, GraphWorkspace; each graph is read against its rig as the rigs currently are in Studio), so they can be saved, undone and reloaded from disk. Their files are written in a compact layout (DataDocument.Compact: an object or list goes on one line where it fits).

LastFleet Studio (LastFleet.Studio, docs/studio/reference.md) is a second executable: the designers' tool. It ships and runs separately from the game. It references the client for its view code (GameView, LocalSession, UiLayer, the Loot Lab), so the range it fires a weapon on is the game's own, and it builds the same shaders (src/Shaders.targets, shared by both projects).

It edits the loot data files in the repository. Each file is a DataDocument: its JSON as a node tree. The comment block at the top of the file is written back as it was, and the rest is written the way System.Text.Json indents it, which is the layout the files are kept in (LastFleetStudio --format restores it). LootWorkspace holds all of them and tracks what isn't saved.

Every edit rebuilds the game's LootCatalog from the documents' text (LootCatalog.Parse), with an in-memory loot table, studio.preview, that rolls the frame, maker and rarity you picked. A few things make this nice to work with:

  • If a rebuild fails, Studio keeps the last catalog that built and shows the problems.
  • A catalog built from the saved files is kept alongside it, so you can compare a roll with it.
  • LootContent.Use hands the new catalog to the client's code (and clears its rolls and the guns WeaponModels has kept), so the HUD's cards, the gun models and the range all follow the data as you edit it.
  • The forms are generated from the JSON itself (JsonEditor), so a new field in the data needs no Studio code. Too easy!

Every data folder Studio edits is a DataWorkspace: LootWorkspace, AiWorkspace (which builds every behavior, SoldierBehavior.Parse, and the EnemyRoster), and the workspaces for rigs, graphs, missions, procgen data, materials, skies, doors, breaches, colliders and the campaign. DataWorkspace.Create and Delete add and remove a file, which is written or deleted at the next save.

Behavior mode's sandbox. Sandbox is a LocalSession of the range with no fight of its own. It's drawn by a GameView whose Spectator camera stands apart from the player. The player's body is drawn like anyone else's, with no gun in hand and no HUD, and it's driven by PuppetCommand (standing still, or strafing), so it's a target that can't be hurt. Squads (SoldierSpawner.SpawnHunting, of the picked variant, with their personality and any behavior you're trying out set on their brains) and hordes (HordeSystem.CallHere) spawn where a click's ray (CameraMath.ScreenToRay) hits the level. Every AI edit goes straight to the session's SoldierAiSystem.Behaviors.

The scoring graph. A behavior's scoring graph (UtilityGraph) is rebuilt from its file's nodes every frame, so it never gets out of step with an edit or a revert. Each node is a JSON object (a term, a condition, and so on), and every edit in the graph changes that object and then calls DataWorkspace.Changed. With a soldier picked, the graph shows that soldier's TacticalInputs run through the built UtilityModel (UtilityModel.Values, then each rule's terms and factors in the order the file lists them). It's drawn with ImGui's draw lists, with no node-editor library.

So where does the layout go? Right on the objects themselves:

  • Where the designer places a node is written on its object (x, y; the inputs and the choice are in the root's layout).
  • So is the route a wire takes: via on the item the wire leaves, or inputVia on the item an input's wire reaches. These are ids of reroutes, stored once in the root's reroutes so wires can share them and branch from them, or bare points.
  • Nodes added from the palette but not yet wired in go in the root's loose list, each marked as term, factor or condition.

The graph lays itself out in columns until the first such edit, which pins every node where it is (Freeze). Won't all that extra stuff slow the game down? Nope. The game's parsers skip all of these keys (System.Text.Json ignores members the file type doesn't have), so they cost nothing at run time. A test scores the shipped scoring with all of them added and without them, and gets the same result.

The tree editor (BehaviorGraph) works the same way. It's a card for each node in the file's flat list, rebuilt from the JSON every frame and wired parent to child. Every edit changes the nodes' objects and then calls DataWorkspace.Changed, so the soldiers run the edited tree on the next tick. The edits are:

  • connecting (which refuses a cycle),
  • moving (which sorts the parent's children by height, since they run top to bottom),
  • adding from the palette,
  • and deleting.

The tree shows the picked soldier's last run: SoldierAiSystem.TracedSoldier makes RunTree keep each node's result (Trace) for that one soldier.

The picture beside the editor is the sandbox's own view, drawn with a camera that follows the soldier (BehaviorMode.PipCamera). How do we fit it in the picture without a second render target? Its projection is shifted (CameraView.Shift, added to the projection's third column) so its center falls inside the picture. It's the same view drawn off center, undistorted, with no second render target. The rest of the screen behind the editor is painted over. CameraMath.ScreenToRay removes the shift again, so clicking in the picture picks a soldier there.

The sandbox's TestCover boxes are added to physics as static meshes (JoltWorld.AddStaticMesh). A moved box becomes a new body, and the old one is put away with SetSurfaceSolid. They're drawn through GameView.ExtraBoxes and taken out of the navigation grid with NavGrid.SetObstacle. That's an overlay on the walkability the grid was built with, and the pathfinder, its path smoothing and the cover map all read it. When obstacles might close the corridor the pathfinder's clusters chose, it searches every cell. After that, SoldierAiSystem.RebuildCover finds cover again and makes every soldier choose again. An edit becomes solid when it ends, not while you're dragging a box.

Flow mode (FlowMode, LastFleet.Studio.Flow) reads the loot files themselves, not the catalog, into a FlowChart. That's FlowNode boxes in columns and lanes, and FlowLink arrows with a FlowLinkKind and an amount. A name that doesn't exist becomes a Missing box, and its links are Broken. FlowLayout lays it out: each column is ordered and placed by the boxes its arrows come from, sweeping right, then left, then right. FlowView draws it with ImGui's draw lists. It's rebuilt whenever DataWorkspace.Edits changes (every edit, undo and reload, whether or not the data then builds), and the selected box's entry is edited beside it with the mode's SectionEditor (DrawSelected).

The frame

Here's what happens every time the client draws a frame:

sequenceDiagram
    participant Loop as Client main loop
    participant Input as InputState
    participant Step as FixedTimestep
    participant World as GameWorld
    participant Renderer as VulkanRenderer

    Loop->>Input: BeginFrame, then PollEvents
    Loop->>Step: Advance(frame time)
    Step-->>Loop: n ticks (0 to 8)
    loop each tick
        Loop->>World: write PlayerCommand from input
        Loop->>World: Step(): systems in fixed order
        Loop->>Loop: remember the eye position before and after
    end
    Loop->>Loop: camera: eye lerped by Alpha, view angles plus unapplied mouse movement
    Loop->>Loop: hit results into CombatFeedback (markers, impacts, tracers, target flashes)
    Loop->>Loop: effects into an EffectBuilder, part tints, HUD and hit marker into an OverlayBuilder
    Loop->>Loop: ImGui frame: developer overlay, menu when the mouse is free
    Loop->>Renderer: RenderFrame(camera, FrameGeometry with ImGui draw data)

  • Fixed ticks, smooth frames. The simulation always advances in whole 60 Hz ticks. The renderer runs as fast as the display allows and draws the eye position interpolated between the last two ticks with FixedTimestep.Alpha, so motion is smooth at 144 Hz.

  • The mouse. Mouse movement accumulates between frames and is applied by the first tick of a frame, so a frame that runs two ticks doesn't turn twice. The camera also adds the movement no tick has applied yet (InputState.PeekMouseDelta), so the view follows the mouse every frame, not every tick. Recoil comes from the simulation's view angles, so it's never lost.

  • View feel (ViewFeel) is presentation only, and it always decays back to what the simulation says. It's all the little stuff that makes moving feel good:

    • Eye height glides between stances.
    • A step's vertical snap is eased in (a vertical move in one tick that's steeper than any walkable slope counts as a step).
    • Hard landings dip the view on a damped spring.
    • Aim is interpolated between ticks and eased for the zoom and the gun.
    • The gun sways against turning and bobs with speed, and the reticle opens quickly and settles more slowly.
    • While aiming, look speed is divided by the zoom (times the aim sensitivity setting), so the zoomed view moves the same distance on screen for the same mouse movement.

  • The model library (ModelLibrary, Content) holds every model made in Blender for the game's things. That's each top-level object of each .gltf in content/props/ and content/weapons/, named file/Object (ModelId: items/Medkit, auto_rifle/Receiver), so a file dropped into either folder is picked up with no code! A file that doesn't load is kept, empty, with the reason, and the rest still load.

    Each model lists its paint slots (PaintSlots): materials named Paint_<Slot>. The data picks the color of each slot for each thing that uses the model, and the default is the material's own color. A few names are the game's own colors and are reserved (PaintSlots.ReservedForProps: Glint, Rarity, Supply, Light; ReservedForWeapons: Body, BodyDark, BodyShade, Accent, Reticle, Lamp). PaintSlots.Problem says what's wrong with a misused one, the same way the Blender add-ons' Check does. The prop and weapon part libraries below are read through it.

  • Icons (IconRenderer, IconAtlas, ModelIcons, Client) are drawn from the models, never by hand. Each model is seen from a fixed three-quarter angle (right side and front, a gun's muzzle pointing right, a little from above), orthographic and as large as fits, lit by lights fixed to the view, on a transparent background.

    Why not draw them on the GPU? They're rasterized on the CPU (3 × 3 samples per pixel, sRGB, straight alpha), so icons are the same on every machine and can be drawn in tests without a GPU. ModelIcons keeps one icon per model and paint colors in an IconAtlas: one picture with square cells that doubles in height when it's full. Its Version changes whenever its pixels do, so the GPU copy knows when to update. An icon is only drawn again when its model is reloaded or its paint changes. A prop is drawn the way it looks lying around, and a weapon part in gunmetal.

    The HUD's icons (GameIcons, in the ViewShell) are drawn the same way from the things themselves:

    • A weapon's icon comes from the gun as rolled (WeaponModels.Of: its frame and parts, its maker's paint, its rarity's glow) and is kept by its roll and frame.
    • An item's icon comes from its model as it lies around (CharacterModels.ItemVertices) and is kept by its kind and rarity.
    • At most two are drawn per frame (about 3 ms each). The 96 least recently used make room for new ones, and all of them are redrawn when the loot data, the items or frames in play, or the part models change.
    • The weapon card, the inventory and the quick bar show them, and IconTexture copies the atlas to the GPU after each frame.

  • Weapon models (WeaponModels, WeaponPartLibrary, Client; ModelParts, Content) are forged from recipes (content/weapons/forge.json, LastFleet.Content.Forge: ForgeRecipes builds each part from its shapes, ForgeShapes builds the shapes, and ForgeFile writes the glTF; run it with tools/LastFleet.WeaponForge, or from Studio's armory with its recipe editor, ForgeEditor; docs/plans/weapons.md phase 5). Where a part has no recipe it's made in Blender instead (docs/blender/weapons.md, the add-on tools/blender/lastfleet_weapons.py). There's one glTF file of parts per frame in content/weapons/, plus shared.gltf for parts any frame can use.

    A forged part has a Detail child mesh (rail ridges, vents, bolts; only on your own gun) and a Far one (no bevels or detail, round parts with six sides), which other players' guns and dropped guns use (WeaponModels.Of(weapon, detail: false)). That's about 1,000 to 2,400 triangles in your hands, and 250 to 500 in anyone else's.

    A gun is the frame's Receiver with one part for each slot at each of its sockets (empties):

    • Each rolled part shows as the model its data names (WeaponPart.Model, WeaponModels.ModelOf). That's either a full name or the end of a shape name, for example Drum for Magazine_2_Drum, so a part can name its look on every frame. If the frame has no such shape it uses the frame's first, None means nothing in the slot, a part that names nothing gets one picked from its id, and a stock weapon gets the first.
    • The optic is the optic part's kind (iron, red dot, holographic, scope; WeaponModels.SightOf), but a gun that magnifies 2× or more always carries a scope, whatever is fitted.
    • A part's paint slots take its data's colors (WeaponPart.Paint).
    • Barrels stretch with range and magazines with capacity.
    • Materials named Body, BodyDark and BodyShade take the maker's finish, and Accent takes the rarity's color.

    Each gun is put together once and kept. The view model, soldiers' guns and weapons lying in the world all draw from it. The starting files are the old box guns exported box for box (tools/greybox/generate_weapon_parts.py). Characters are only drawn in rooms that can be seen (RoomVisibility).

  • Prop models (PropLibrary, PropModel, Client) are made in Blender too (docs/blender/props.md, the add-on tools/blender/lastfleet_props.py). The files are:

    • content/props/items.gltf: each item lying around.
    • containers.gltf: the supply case, the footlocker's inside, and their lids, which hinge at the body's Hinge empty and swing up to open.
    • cargo.gltf: the crate, the cargo pod and its lid, and the drone and its rotor blade, which spins at each Rotor empty.

    Every top-level object of every file there is a prop, found by its name (or by file/Object). Each is baked once into flat-shaded vertices. Each vertex records whether it keeps its own color or takes one the game gives it (PropMaterial: Glint, Rarity, Supply and Light, from a PropPaint), and its paint slot, which is colored by the colors passed to PropModel.AppendTo (or its color in the file when none is passed). CharacterModels draws the props from them, and ViewModel draws the crate in your arms.

    What if a prop is missing from the files? It's drawn from the boxes in code, so a missing file costs looks, not the prop. The starting files are those boxes exported (tools/greybox/generate_props.py), and PropModelTests checks every prop against the sizes the game's rules use and against its markers.

  • The weapon in your hands (ViewModel, Client) is the gun's model, placed each frame and drawn in the renderer's view-model pass. It takes the view feel's sway and the aim from ViewFeel. It sits low and right at the hip, turned in towards the reticle, and comes up until its sight is on the eye line when you aim. It drops and cants for sprinting, sliding and swapping, and kicks back for 0.14 s with a 60 ms muzzle flash.

    Reloads. For a reload it dips and rolls while the magazine follows its frame's reload steps (content/defs/reloads.json, ReloadLibrary, built into LastFleet.Content; ReloadHands; docs/plans/weapons.md phases 3 and 4). Picture it step by step: the left hand goes to the magazine's grab point, the magazine slides out along its well with the hand and is dropped, the hand goes to the body's pouch (the rig's Pouch socket), a new magazine is lined up under the well and pushed home, the charging handle is pulled after the last round, and the hand goes back to the fore grip.

    Each step takes its share of the simulation's reload progress, so a faster reload speeds up all of it (the host never animates). The points come from the gun's parts (WeaponModel):

    • the fore grip: a fore grip module's Hand.L, or the grip itself, else a barrel's Hand.L, else the receiver's Grip.L;
    • the magazine: its Grab, else low on its front;
    • the well: at Socket_Magazine, out along its -Y;
    • the charging handle: the receiver's Charge, else high on its right side.

    So the same steps fit any gun! Your own body's left hand goes where the view model's magazine is (BodyWorld.Reloading). Soldiers and other players follow the same steps (without knowing whether it's an empty reload), with their magazines moving along (SkinnedBodies.Magazine). Meanwhile the bridge keeps the hand on the gun (HumanoidBridge.HandOnGun), and the Reload clip moves the rest of the upper body.

    A frame with no steps (the shotgun, hand cannon, grenade launcher) works the old way: the hand comes off the gun with the clip, and the magazine drops out and comes back. Studio's Bodies mode plays reloads with its reload action (Actions: Play reload, or held part way) and "reload from empty", and edits them in its Reloads tab (ReloadEditor), playing changes at once. The armory draws a rolled gun's hand points on its turntable.

    Sights. When you aim, you look through the sight, not at it (ViewModel.SightKind): the optic's Sight marker is on the eye line.

    • Iron sights are a notch with a post, and the post's tip is the aim point.
    • A red dot and a holographic sight are frames on a mount around a pane of sight glass (WeaponModels.AddSightGlass, which replaces the model's own Reticle triangles; the materials sight_dot, sight_ring and sight_chevron, SurfaceMaterial.Sight). The glass is clear except for its reticle, which world_shading.slang's sightGlass draws on the aim point whatever the gun is doing, the way a real one sits at infinity. The glass sways and kicks around it, and the shots go where the reticle is (shots go where the camera points; the gun's sway is only visual). The sight line runs through the window, clear of the barrel, and is held closer to the eye for bigger optics.
    • A gun with 2× zoom or more has a scope: a tube on rings that, once raised, gives way to its lens. The tonemap draws the lens over the zoomed frame (ScopeLens in FrameParameters, GameView.ScopeLensOf; tonemap.slang's scopeLens). It's a round window that opens as the scope comes up. The scene inside is pulled in a little towards the rim and fringed there, and darkened towards it. It's shadowed on the side the gun's sway moved it away from (the "eye box"). The screen around it is blurred and dark, and its reticle (ScopeReticle: duplex, mil-dot or chevron) sits on the aim point.

    An optic part's reticle picks the reticle for either kind (WeaponModels.ReticleOf), and Studio's armory previews it with "Through the sight" (ReticlePreview). The HUD's own reticle disappears once the sights are up.

    Want to look at a particular gun? --weapon <frame>[,<part>...] gives you a rolled gun of a frame with the given parts fitted (ChosenWeapon), and a smoke camera's ,aim aims it. ViewModelTests casts rays from the eye to check that only the reticle is on the line of sight and the view around it is clear.

  • Long stalls. After a long stall the timestep runs at most 8 ticks and drops the rest, so the game hitches briefly instead of spiralling.

  • Smoke runs. --smoke <frames> runs exactly one tick per frame with scripted input, so a smoke run is the same on every machine. It shows the splash for its first 30 frames, then plays with the developer overlay open.

  • Before gameplay the loop shows the BRUTAL splash and runs no ticks. Gameplay starts, and the mouse is captured, when the splash ends.

Input, settings and UI

  • Bindings. InputBindings maps each InputAction (move, jump, sprint, crouch, dash, fire, aim, reload, grenade, weapon slots and swap, interact, drag, ping, flashlight, quick slots, inventory, zoom, lean, fire mode) to two InputBindings, each a key or mouse button. Binding something that's already in use takes it away from its old action. Escape, F3 and F11 (full screen) are reserved. InputMapper reads actions, not keys, to build the PlayerCommand.
  • Settings. GameSettings holds sensitivity (and aim sensitivity), invert Y, field of view, overlay visibility, the toggles for aim, crouch and sprint, the display and video settings (window mode, vsync, shadows, antialiasing, bloom, ray tracing and its quality, the renderer, path tracing and DLSS), difficulty, sound and subtitles, and the bindings. It saves as JSON (System.Text.Json, source-generated) to the application-data folder whenever the menu changes it, by writing a temporary file and moving it into place. Loading never fails the game: out-of-range values are clamped, bad entries are skipped with a warning, and unreadable files are replaced by defaults. Smoke runs ignore the file.
  • Dear ImGui. UiLayer owns the ImGui and ImPlot contexts and feeds them GLFW events itself, so the BRUTAL GLFW/OpenGL backends aren't used. While the mouse is captured, ImGui gets no mouse input, so an overlay can never swallow a shot. The renderer draws ImGui's output (see Renderer) and handles ImGui 1.92's texture requests, so the font atlas lives in the same texture store as the logo.
  • Interpolated strings passed to ImGui (ImGui.Text($"...")) are formatted into BRUTAL's per-frame arena, so the overlay allocates nothing per frame. The menu, which is only open while paused, does allocate.

Simulation rules

These are the rules that keep the simulation deterministic, cheap and testable. Break one and something downstream (snapshots, checksums, co-op) breaks with it.

  • Components are plain unmanaged structs with public fields: no references and no behavior. That makes them cheap to copy, checksum, snapshot and send over the network.
  • Systems hold queries, not state. They implement ISimulationSystem and run in the order they were added to the GameWorld. That order is part of the determinism contract.
  • No allocations per tick. Iterate with query.Chunks and spans, not ForEachEntity with a capturing lambda. SimulationTests.Step_DoesNotAllocate measures it.
  • Input is a command. PlayerCommand is the only way input reaches the simulation. In co-op, clients send commands to the host.
  • Randomness and seeds. Use DeterministicRandom.ForStream(runSeed, "stream.name") so each consumer gets its own independent stream. Turn content IDs into numbers with StableHash.Fnv1a64, never GetHashCode, which is randomized per process.
  • Profiling. GameWorld times every system with Stopwatch each tick for the developer overlay. The times are only read for display and never feed back into the simulation, so they can't break determinism.
  • Checksums. SimulationChecksum.Compute fingerprints the world. The same commands give the same checksum on the same platform. Windows and Linux can differ in the last bit of trigonometric functions. Is that a problem? Not for us: the design doesn't need cross-platform equality, because the host is authoritative.

  • The logic is in pure static functions. PlayerController.Update, Mantle and WeaponHandling.Update take ref structs and know nothing about the ECS, so the tests drive them directly, tick by tick. The systems just fetch components and call them.

  • Buttons are held state. PlayerCommand carries which buttons are down, not which were pressed. Why? The simulation keeps last tick's buttons in MovementState and WeaponState and works out presses itself. That keeps commands idempotent for the network, and a lost packet can't swallow a press. The client latches taps, so a press shorter than a tick still reaches one tick.

  • Weapons are data. A WeaponProfile (archetype plus 0–100 stats, and scales on the frame's damage, magazine and zoom) is resolved once by WeaponStatsResolver into WeaponStats with physical units. Procedural loot produces profiles, never raw stats (see Loot).

  • Frames (WeaponArchetypes) are the loot data's archetypes in archetypes.json. Each has an id, a Model file, Firing (hitscan, pellets or projectile), a shot Sound, Kit and Sidearm flags, Soldier use and frameStats. WeaponArchetypes.Use puts them in play when LootContent loads the data, so the stock kit and soldiers' guns follow them too. WeaponArchetypes.Shipped keeps the built-in ones, and an archetype with the same id starts from the built-in one. A weapon's WeaponStats.Frame is a WeaponFrame: the frame's index among those in play, sent as a byte (protocol version 38). Loadouts are saved by frame id.

    The frames are: auto rifle and hand cannon (the starting loadout), pulse rifle, SMG (950 RPM, short falloff), shotgun, marksman rifle (3.5× zoom, a head shot drops a soldier), grenade launcher and machine gun (a hundred-round belt at 720 RPM, a round a little lighter than a rifle's, slow to aim and wide from the hip, a 4.2 s reload; protocol version 16).

    A shotgun round is eight pellets (Pellets, from the frame's frameStats). Each pellet is its own ShotRecord with the round's ShotIndex, in a fixed pattern (one in the middle, and rings around it at the golden angle) rotated by the round's hash. It's the same every time for the same weapon and round, so a client can predict it.

    A launcher round is a shot with a ProjectileSpeed. Hitscan skips it, and the grenade system launches it as a Grenade marked Impact. It falls with a third of a frag's gravity and bursts on the first wall, floor or body it meets (within 0.45 m of the segment from a character's knees to its eyes, not counting its shooter), dealing the frame's damage at the center of its BlastRadius. Grenade carries its blast, so the snapshot does too (protocol version 8).

  • Shots are events, not entities. WeaponSystem appends a ShotRecord to the ShotLog for every shot: origin, direction, spread and damage profile. The log is cleared each tick and preallocated, so firing allocates nothing.

  • Hitscan runs in the tick. ShotResolutionSystem casts every shot up to 300 m, right after the weapon fires and before anyone moves, so the shot leaves from where the shooter stood. Each result goes into the HitLog (HitRecord: point, normal, distance, surface, target, precision, damage, kill), which the client reads for feedback and co-op will send to clients.

  • Targets are level parts. A Target names a body surface and an optional precision surface. A ray hit carries the surface (level part) it struck, so the system matches it to a target without any extra collision. Damage is DamageProfile.DamageAt(distance, precision). A destroyed target stays down, blocking shots but taking no damage, until TargetSystem stands it back up healed. Enemies will get their own hit volumes; the matching stays the same.

  • Characters are hit through HitVolumes: a body capsule a little narrower than the collision capsule, and a head sphere for precision hits. Both are built from the character's stance, so crouching makes a smaller target. They're exact ray math in the simulation, never physics-engine queries.

    Their sizes are a HitShape (the head sphere's radius, how far it sits above the eye standing and crouched, and the capsule's share of the collision radius), stored in HitVolumes.Shape. It comes from the humanoid rig's body (content/rigs/humanoid.json, RigBody). The client puts it in play at startup (HitShapes.Load, with host and clients reading the same file), and Studio does it as the rig is edited (RigWorkspace). Otherwise it's HitShape.Default.

    The drawn bodies keep their eye where the simulation has it. A rig's clip marked with an eye (RigClip.Eye: standing or crouched) keeps the Eye socket near the simulation's eye (checked by HitShapesTests through the graph, and by the Blender add-on's Check), so the head gets shot where it's drawn. A shot stops at whichever is nearest: the level or any living character. A character on the shooter's side stops it without taking damage.

  • Health (Health) regenerates Destiny-style after a delay without damage (players only, for now). LifeSystem clears a dead character's commands and removes its collision body so the corpse doesn't block anyone. Players with a SpawnPoint respawn after three seconds; soldiers stay dead.

  • Recoil is eased, not instant. A shot adds its kick to WeaponState.RecoilPending, and each tick moves most of what's pending into the view (a 25 ms time constant), so the view climbs over a few ticks. WeaponState.LastRecoilStep is how far recoil moved the view on the last tick. The camera subtracts the part of it the frame hasn't reached yet, so recoil is smooth between ticks too. Recovery starts once a shot interval and a quarter has passed and the kick has fully landed.

  • Bloom recovers every tick, at 15% of its rate while firing, so sustained fire settles below the maximum. Aiming down sights scales bloom down to 30%.

  • Items as data (ItemDefinition, Items, ItemKind, ItemRoles; protocol version 34). Every item is data, in one list in content/defs/loot/items.json (LootCatalog.Items, put in play by Items.Use; without data it's Items.Shipped, which matches the repository's list). Each item has an id (snake_case), name, model (file/Object in content/props), paint (colors for the model's paint slots), color, stack size, and flags for what it's for (ItemRoles). Each flag turns on its own fields:

    • consumable: used from a quick slot. It has useSeconds and the effect it runs, effect (see effects, below).
    • fleetSupply: one of the fleet's supplies, used by the needs board, the hold and pods.
    • refinable: a raw material for the refinery.
    • weaponPart: a loose part, fitted in fitSeconds.
    • carried: held in both arms as a cargo crate, never in the pack. One laid down by SimulationSetup.SpawnItemDrop is a crate.

    In play, an item is its index in the list (ItemKind, a byte starting at 1), and that's how it's stored and sent (ItemPile, Inventory.Using, CargoLoad, SupplyTank.Supply, FleetHaul by item; at most Items.Max, 64). Host and clients must play with the same items.

    So how does code find, say, a medkit without hard-coding a name? By what its effect does (Items.FirstThat<HealAction>(): the starting kit's medkits, ammo pack and beacon, a soldier's drop) or by what it's for (Items.WeaponPart, Items.FleetSupplies), never by name. What the fleet does with each supply is data too (see supplies, under the fleet).

  • Effects as data (LastFleet.Simulation/Effects: EffectDefinition, EffectAction, EffectRegistry, EffectRunner; EffectSystem, PlayerEffects; protocol version 36). What an item does is an effect, defined in content/defs/loot/effects.json (LootCatalog.Effects, read by EffectReader and put in play by EffectRegistry.Use; without data it's EffectRegistry.Shipped). An effect has an id, a name, a trigger and a list of actions. The trigger is EffectTrigger.Use (used from a quick slot) or Passive (active while its item is held, in a quick slot or the pack, once per kind of item). Each action is a record with its own numbers:

    • HealAction (amount)
    • GiveArmorAction (amount, up to max)
    • ShieldAction (amount, delay, recharge per second)
    • ChangeStatAction (a PlayerStat: move speed, damage taken or reload speed, by a fraction, for some seconds)
    • RefillAmmoAction (magazines, LoadoutSystem.AddAmmo)
    • CallPodAction
    • PlaceDeviceAction (fuse, blast radius and damage)
    • SoundAction (a cue in sounds.json)
    • FlashAction (color, brightness, radius, seconds)

    A passive effect can only shield and change stats. A consumable item names its use effect (ItemDefinition.Effect), and any item can name a passive one (ItemDefinition.Passive). The reader rejects an item that names the wrong kind.

    InventorySystem only uses a quick slot when EffectRunner.IsUseful says at least one action would do something: you're hurt, armor is below its max, a shield would be fuller, a stat change (always), a weapon has room, a mission is going. No wasting a medkit at full health! Then EffectRunner.Apply runs the actions in order. Anything that needs the world comes back as EffectRequests for InventorySystem to carry out:

    • a pod set down in front of the player;
    • a device placed on whatever the player is looking at within 1.6 m, or else on the floor ahead (InventorySystem.DeviceSpot). It's a resting Grenade marked Placed with the action's fuse and blast, so it goes off like a grenade does (enemies and the player who placed it get caught if they're in the open, and DestructionSystem breaks what's in reach), and it's sent, drawn (a flat blinking charge) and warned about like one;
    • sounds and flashes, logged as EffectCueRecords (EffectCueLog, passed to SimulationSetup.CreateWorld). Each names the effect by its index in EffectRegistry and the action by its own index. They're sent with the tick's events (the bark count says whether any follow, so a tick without any costs nothing extra) and played by GameAudio (the cue, if the sound library has it) and SceneLights.RecordCues.

    What lasts is kept in the player's PlayerEffects: up to eight timed stat changes (using the same one again renews it) and the shield a used effect gave. EffectSystem (after LifeSystem, before movement) counts them down, adds the passive effects of whatever is held (EffectSystem.Held), and writes the totals, each clamped from -75% to +300%, to where the game reads them: MovementSettings.SpeedChange (ground and air speed, so the client's prediction has it), Health.DamageTakenChange and PlayerEffects.ReloadSpeedChange (for WeaponHandling.Update's reload). The shield is the stronger of the used one and the held ones, and it recharges after its delay without damage.

    Damage always goes through the same layers. Health.TakeDamage scales damage by the damage-taken change, then the shield absorbs it, then armor (which doesn't come back), then health, so every source of damage goes through them. Going down ends all of it. PlayerEffects is sent raw to its own player next to Health. The HUD draws shield and armor as thin bars over health, and lists the running changes and the held items with what they give (EffectsHud). --give <item>[,<item>...] puts items in the inventory when a solo mission starts.

  • Inventory (Inventory, InventorySystem; protocol version 21). Besides the two weapons, a player carries four quick slots and a pack of twelve. Each holds a pile of one item (ItemPile: medkits stack to 3, ammo packs to 2, supplies to 10). Quick slots only take things used in a fight (Items.IsConsumable). Everything else goes in the pack, except things carried in both arms.

    An item lying on the floor is an ItemDrop entity (a pile and a yaw). Holding Interact at it, the same as at a weapon (WeaponPickupSystem picks what the player is looking at: see Looking at things below), takes what fits and leaves the rest.

    What a player does with the inventory is an op in their command (PlayerCommand.Op: two slot bytes and a serial). The ops are drop, move (onto a pile of the same item, otherwise swapping where both fit), use from a quick slot, and put down the weapon in hand (never the last one). How do we make that safe over a lossy network? The client sends each op for six ticks under one serial and the host applies each serial once, so a lost packet loses nothing and a repeated one doesn't happen twice. When downed or dead, ops are used up and not carried out.

    Using an item takes its useSeconds (1.4 s for a medkit, 1 s for an ammo pack) with the weapon down, and only works when it would do something: healing when hurt (a medkit's 60 health), refilling ammo when a weapon has room (an ammo pack's three magazines), or calling a pod while the mission is still going. A player who dies drops every pile in a ring where they fell and comes back with an empty inventory.

    Levels place supply piles in their rooms (SupplySpot: the position, the item table, the deck and the item level). The level's rules place them (StationPlacements), and designers can put them on furniture's sockets (FurnitureLootRolls). They're rolled when the fight is set up (LootCatalog.RollPile, from an ItemTable: items by id and weight, a count, and more per deck). LevelRolls seeds each one from the level's seed, its position and the run, so Studio's Missions mode can show what the fight will spawn. A soldier that dies leaves something that heals or refills ammo one time in five (SoldierLoot.ItemChance). The snapshot carries items lying around in their own section, and the recipient's inventory in its own player's section.

  • Raw materials and crafting (ItemRoles.Refinable, Client/Fleet/Refinery; protocol version 34). Raw materials are items with the refinable flag (scrap, ore, ...). They're found wherever item tables name them and brought back like the fleet's supplies (by pod, the hold or the dropship), and counted in the haul by item. After a mission the fleet keeps them in its stores by item id, along with its supplies (FleetState.Stores, FleetSim.Deliver).

    Each jump, the refinery runs as many batches as its tier allows (RefineryTier: what it costs from the fleet's supplies to reach, by item id, the grades it can make, and its batch count). It runs the recipes for those grades, best grade first (RefineryRecipe: inputs by item id, a grade, how many), and produces graded material (MaterialGrade: Mk I to Mk IV shipped, each with the item level of what's made from it; FleetState.Graded).

    The needs board shows the stores and the upgrade (RefineryPanel). Drag at the armory bench on the ship opens the fabricator (FabricatorScreen), which spends a grade on whatever the data offers (Fabrication: a weapon rolled from a loot table, or a part for a given slot or any slot, made by LootCatalog.Fabricate at the grade's item level and seeded from the fleet's seed and how many it has made). The result is laid on the bench. All of it is edited in Studio's Items mode.

  • Containers (Container; protocol version 23). Rooms have footlockers and supply cases to search, placed by the level's rules (see Placement rules):

    • A loot room gets a footlocker or two against its walls, and about one room in three of the others gets one (not corridors, closets, the insertion bay or the extraction).
    • There has to be room in front to open it, and none go where a soldier stands.
    • The footlocker's body is built into the level (LootLocker_ parts), so it's solid and the soldiers' grid goes around it.
    • A quarter of rooms (most loot rooms) also get a case on top of something (ContainerSpot).

    The fight fills each one fresh every run from the mission's item table for its kind (FightSession, LevelRolls.Container: medkits, ammo packs, weapon parts and supplies). Holding Interact at a closed one opens it, the same as a pickup (WeaponPickupSystem weighs containers alongside weapons and items). Its contents are laid out on top along its length as items to take, and it stays open. The snapshot carries each container's kind, yaw and whether it's open (its own section, 16 in all), but not what's inside, which stays on the host until it's lying in the world.

  • The airlock and the haul (Airlock, AirlockLayout, FleetHaul; protocol version 24). The squad starts in an airlock built into the insertion bay (Airlock.Build). It's a chamber around the player spawns, against a bay wall with no doorway: the hull (Airlock.HullSide). The outer hatch the dropship docked at is in that wall. The inner wall's door (Airlock_Door) is lock 0 of the mission, and it opens on a timer (MissionLockPlan.OpensAt, MissionTuning.AirlockSeconds = 5) rather than with a keycard. The HUD counts the cycle down. The layout keeps the two cells beyond the hull empty on every story (LayoutSolver, the dropship's berth), so the hatch always backs onto space.

    When the hack starts, the dropship casts off (with a notice), and the extraction is where it picks the squad up ("DROPSHIP"). Getting out counts the haul (MissionState.Haul, MissionSystem.CountHaul): the fleet supplies in the packs of everyone standing at the point, the crate in their arms, and every crate set down within the extraction's radius plus 2 m. The banner and the lobby's line show it. How the fleet keeps it is covered under the fleet.

  • Cargo (CargoCrate, CargoSystem; protocol version 24). Crates of a fleet supply stand against the walls (CargoLoad: which supply and how much, rolled from the mission's cargo item table when the fight is set up, fuller deeper in; most of it is whatever the station was chosen for). The level's rules place them: in two loot rooms out of five and one other room in ten, times the template's cargoShare, and half as often on the first deck.

    Holding Interact at a crate picks it up in both hands (WeaponPickupSystem, into Inventory.Carried). Carrying one is meant to hurt a little:

    • CargoSystem (after the rescue and ping systems, before movement) keeps the weapon down and takes Fire, Aim, Grenade and Reload out of the command.
    • The controller allows no sprint, slide or dash, and 70% of normal speed (MovementState.Carrying).
    • You can't use items, fit parts, or revive or drag anyone.
    • Drag (F) puts it down in front of you. Fire drops it at your feet and the weapon comes up 0.35 s later.
    • When downed or dead, it falls where you are (LifeSystem handles that too).

    Crates lying around have their own snapshot section (17 in all). The crate being carried is in the player's own inventory, and other players see a bit in the character's flags and draw the crate in its arms.

  • Cargo pods (CargoPod, PodPhase, CargoPodSystem; protocol version 26). Every player starts with a pod beacon (the first consumable whose effect does CallPodAction, usable only while a mission is still going). Using it sets a pod down in front of you (InventorySystem.PodSpot: 1.1 m out, stopping short of a wall). While it's loading, holding Interact at it (WeaponPickupSystem.LoadOrCall, which weighs pods with everything else) moves every fleet supply in your pack into it. If your pack has none and the pod has some, the same hold calls the drone (PodPhase.Called). A crate put down with Drag within 2.5 m goes in (CargoSystem).

    CargoPodSystem (a command system after the mission's and the Director's) then starts the defense:

    • The drone is 30 s out, plus 12 s for each deck the squad has opened (not counting the airlock; from the hack on, all of them plus one more: CargoPodSystem.DefenseSeconds).
    • A Director patrol of two, plus one per deck (up to six), comes out of the closet nearest the pod (MissionSystem.SendPatrol), and another two come halfway through a defense of 40 s or more.
    • A horde (out of the floor 14 to 32 m away by walking distance, HordeSystem.CallToPod) comes only if the mission's hordes are due now by the run's pacing (HordeSystem.Coming), so the pod never draws anything the level doesn't have yet.

    With an enemy within 6 m and no standing player within 8 m, the pod counts toward being overrun (5 s; it counts back down while held). Once overrun, it's smashed open and what it held is lost. If it's held until the drone lands, the drone takes it (PodPhase.Launched) and its contents are added to MissionState.Delivered, which goes to the fleet whatever happens in the mission (FleetSim.AfterMission takes what reached the fleet: on a win the haul plus the delivered; on a loss or abandon, just the delivered). A launched or overrun pod stays for 6 s (the drone lifting it away, or the wreck) and then it's gone.

    Pods have their own snapshot section (18 in all), with the component sent raw. The HUD marks each pod, counts the drone in with a bar and warns while it's being overrun. --pod <seconds> sets a loaded pod down in front of the player when a mission starts.

  • Fuel and water tanks (StationTanks, TankSpot, SupplyTank, TankPhase, TankSystem, TankPumpSystem; protocol version 27). Fuel and water aren't carried out in crates anymore (the station's cargo table holds neither). They're in big octagonal tanks built into the level's mesh (Tank_<i>: the sides and tops take rays, so the navigation grid sees them, and there's a body to bump into).

    The level's tank rule places them against the walls of rooms off the main route (not corridors, closets, the insertion, objective, arena or extraction), clear of doorways, breaches and anything else in the room, with space in front of the valve, one per room. They're only placed where the way back to the dropship's port, doorway to doorway, is within four fifths of the hose length and before the gate the hack opens (StationPlacements, StationTanks.Stamp).

    They're prop models (Phase 5's station props: content/props/station.gltf, made in Blender, docs/blender/props.md; StationProp, StationProps). The generator sets them into the level's mesh (LevelGenerator takes the props' ModelLibrary alongside the room kit) as a room's furniture. Each mesh object becomes a part named after the instance (Tank_<i>_Shell, Tank_<i>_Valve). What's solid is the model's collider in content/props/colliders.json (StationProp.Collider):

    • the shipped fixtures are their meshes, so a tank's octagonal shell, valve and gauge are solid as drawn;
    • given authored, each object is drawn only (_NoCollision), solid only (_CollisionOnly, a box) or both (a box);
    • given box or cylinder, one solid part (Tank_<i>_Solid) is around everything drawn.

    Supply and Light materials take the supply's color and the coupling's glow. The model's empties place the hose (Hose), the gauge's window (Gauge, Gauge_Top: TankSpot.Gauge, SupplyTank.GaugeBottom and GaugeTop, protocol version 37), the reservoir's valve and riser, the outlet's pipe, each valve's wheel and stand, and the airlock's port and chute.

    The template names the models. Tank kinds (tankKinds, TankKind) each have a model, the supplies they can hold, a radius and height, how much room their spot takes (a smaller kind stands just as close to its wall), and a weight against other kinds for the same supply (only used when there's a choice). The template also names the reservoir's model, outlet, valve, size and supplies, and the airlock's intake and chute (airlock, AirlockModels). What the kinds and the reservoir hold (MissionTemplate.TankSupplies) is everything a station keeps in tanks, and it's never in its crates. A template that names a model that doesn't exist, or one that's missing an empty, or has a solid that isn't a box, is rejected with every problem named (StationProps.Problems; Studio's Missions mode picks the models in 3D).

    The template also says:

    • how many tanks (tanks, 1 to 2);
    • how much a full one holds (tankAmount, 30 to 50 on the first deck, a tenth more per deck);
    • how full they are when found (tankFill, a quarter to full; empty with tankEmptyChance, 15%, which means its valve is dark and there's nothing to take). A gauge over the valve shows it: a bar of the supply's color as high as the tank is full, and a red lamp blinking when it's empty (SupplyTank.Center, protocol version 30);
    • the hose length (hoseLength, 150 m);
    • how fast it pumps (pumpRate, 1.5 per second).

    A station chosen for fuel or water has one more tank, holding only that supply. The airlock has the dropship's intake beside its hatch (AirlockLayout.Port).

    So how do you actually get the fuel out? Holding Interact at a tank's valve takes its hose (TankSystem.Take). It's dragged the way a crate is carried (slow, no sprint, weapon down; Drag puts it down, Fire drops it so you can fight, and it falls if you go down). It's laid behind the player every 2 m (walking back along it picks it up again; past 24 bends, the straightest one is removed), and at full length it's pulled out of your hands. A hose lying on the floor is picked up again where its end is.

    Brought within 1.6 m of the port, it couples and pumps (TankPumpSystem, a command system after the mission's). What's pumped goes to MissionState.Delivered right away, like a drone's pod. Of course the station doesn't just let you: it sends a patrol (two, plus one per deck, up to five) and a horde if hordes are due now. An enemy within 1.4 m of the hose for 2.5 s cuts it, and nothing pumps until a player fixes it (hold Interact at the cut). When the hack starts and the dropship casts off, every tank that isn't empty yet is left as it is (TankPhase.CastOff).

    The tanks belong to the mission (MissionPlan.Tanks, spawned and restarted with it) and have their own snapshot section (19 in all), with the component sent raw along with its hose. The hose is drawn from the valve along its bends (to the hands of whoever is dragging it). Pulses of the supply's color run along it while it pumps, and a cut glows. The HUD marks a hose that's lying or cut, the pumping progress, and the port while you're dragging one. Studio's Missions mode marks the tanks.

  • Looking at things (Interaction; protocol version 33). Everything you use with Interact is used by looking at it, not just standing next to it. You're looking at a thing when the line of sight from the eye (MovementState.EyeAt, with the view's yaw and pitch) passes within its size, treated as an upright column (Interaction.LooksAt). The sizes are: a weapon or items lying around, 0.45 and 0.4 m; a container 0.5; a crate 0.55; a pod 0.7; a tank's valve wheel, a hose's end, a reservoir's valve wheel and the hold's chute 0.5; the terminal 1.2; the hatch 1.1.

    It also has to be within 3.2 m along the line of sight, and within reach (1.6 m) of where it's used from. If there are several, the one most squarely in view and nearest is used, and the HUD's card only shows that one, so walking past things shows nothing. Starting the hack and leaving early work this way too (MissionSystem, TerminalSight and HatchSight). The gate tests' bot turns to face the terminal to start the hack.

  • Deep reservoirs (StationReservoirs, ReservoirSettings, TankPhase.Sealed, PipeValve; protocol version 32). A station has deep reservoirs at the template's chance (reservoir.chance, one in two), or always when the template requires them (reservoir.required). How many is drawn from reservoir.count ([fewest, most], 1 by default, at most 4). Each is in its own room with its own outlet and feed (feeds bundle together where they share a route). If a template requires reservoirs and the level can't place the fewest, the level is generated again from seeds drawn from its own seed, up to six times, keeping the one that places the most (LevelGenerator, GeneratedLevel.Remade).

    Each reservoir is a big tank in a room further from the port than a hose reaches (walking distance, doorway to doorway). The room has one doorway, so it's never on the way through. The furthest of three candidates is picked, as long as it fits on the floor the other rules left free and the floor in front of its valve can be reached. It's placed after everything else in the level, from its own random stream, so it doesn't change anything else a seed produces.

    Its pipe is a network (see Networks), the reservoir feed. It's routed along the walls and ceilings to an outlet on a wall of a room on the way back to the port, with a clear stretch a short walk away (half the hose length; the first such place the pipe reaches, counting from the port's side). Along the way there are valves on the walls under the pipe (junctions, 1 to 2, at most 3; spread along it, where the squad can stand to turn them), plus the reservoir's own. It's a SupplyTank at the outlet (the gauge there shows the reservoir's level) holding the reservoir's contents (amount, 120 to 200). It's Sealed with its valves (SupplyTank.Valves, at most 4) until each one is opened.

    Holding Interact at a valve for valveSeconds (3 s; let go and it starts over; WeaponPickupSystem.NearestShut) opens it: the wheel turns and its lamp goes from red to green. While a valve is turning the station hears it (noise for the alert), and once the last one is open the station sends a patrol to the reservoir (TankPumpSystem). Then the outlet's hose is taken, dragged and pumped like a tank's, but faster (reservoir.pumpRate, 4 per second). The HUD marks shut valves within 20 m and tells you as each one opens. All of it is set in the mission template and Studio's Missions mode.

  • The dropship's hold (MissionState.Hatch; protocol version 28). While the dropship is docked (until the hack starts), holding Interact while looking at its cargo chute in the hull next to the hatch (AirlockLayout.Chute, MissionState.Chute; protocol version 33) unloads the fleet's supplies from your pack into it (WeaponPickupSystem.Unload). A crate put down with Drag within 2.5 m of it goes in too (CargoSystem). It works like a pod but with no defense: what goes in is added to MissionState.Delivered right away (HoldLoads counts how many times).

    So a squad has three ways to bank its finds, each with a cost:

    • going back to the airlock (which costs time, and the station's alert is counting);
    • calling a pod where it stands (which means a fight);
    • or carrying them to the extraction (lost if the squad wipes).

    The hold belongs to the mission (MissionPlan.Hold, the floor in front of the chute). It has no entity of its own, so a level's entity numbering doesn't change. The HUD marks it while the player is carrying a crate or fleet supplies, and says when something has gone in.

  • Leaving early (MissionSystem.LeaveEarly, MissionPhase.LeftEarly; the template's earlyExtraction). While the dropship is docked and its lock has cycled, the squad can leave: everyone alive within 4.5 m of the hatch (MissionState.Door) leaves when one of them holds Interact while looking at it for holdSeconds (5). Looking at the chute instead only unloads. What they carry and set down there goes aboard (MissionState.Haul).

    It counts as a success (Succeeded, with MissionState.Early set) when what the fleet got, banked plus carried, meets the mission's requisition (MissionState.Required and RequiredTotal, MissionState.Meets). The requisition is set when the mission starts, from the fleet's stores (FleetSim.Requisition: each supply with fewer than reserveJumps jumps of reserve is made up to that, at most mostPerSupply each, and minimumHaul in total). If it falls short, it's LeftEarly: the squad keeps the weapons it carried out and the fleet keeps what it brought, but the run's streak ends (LoadoutStore.Finish). While docked, the HUD shows the requisition against what's banked, and near the hatch it shows how to leave.

  • Weapon parts (PartFit, FittedParts, PartFitSystem; protocol version 22). A loose part is an item (one with weaponPart, Items.WeaponPart; one per slot, in the pack) that names its part by the hash of its id and carries a quality byte: each of the part's ranges sits at that fraction between its worst and best. A rolled weapon's WeaponIdentity carries the fitted parts that replace the ones it rolled, one per slot. LootCatalog.Roll(identity) rolls it as before and then rebuilds it with them (WeaponRoller.WithParts: same maker, rarity, affixes and name, with the stats worked out again), so a weapon a player has modified still travels, saves (loadouts.json) and draws from those bytes.

    Fitting is an inventory op, and works any time, anywhere. PartFitSystem lives in LastFleet.Loot because it needs the loot data, and it's a command system that runs before the inventory system. It checks that the part goes on that weapon (rolled, not a relic, the right maker if it's a maker's part, any rarity), takes the item's fitSeconds (2.5 s) with the weapon down, and stops if the player takes damage. Then it puts the part on and puts the replaced one in the part's slot (LootCatalog.FitOf: the rolled part at the quality its ranges came out at). The magazine and reserve keep what they hold, up to the new sizes.

    Parts are found in rooms (one item pile in eight) and dropped by soldiers (one in sixteen), and rolled by LootCatalog.RollPart: first a rarity, the same way weapons roll one (better deeper in), then a part that rarity allows. Stock weapons don't take parts.

  • Loadouts. A player carries up to two weapons (Loadout). The one in hand is the entity's WeaponStats and WeaponState, so everything that reads a weapon stays the same. PlayerCommand.WeaponSlot asks for a slot by number (absolute, so a repeated command can't switch twice). LoadoutSystem stows the weapon in hand into its slot (magazine, reserve, shot counter) and draws the new one, which isn't ready until its swap time is up (0.3–0.55 s depending on handling). A reload in progress is lost.

  • Ammunition. Reloads draw from WeaponState.Reserve (the frame's reserveMagazines: four for the rifles and the grenade launcher, five for the hand cannon, SMG, shotgun and marksman rifle, three for the machine gun). Soldiers' reserve is unlimited (MaxReserve 0). Characters with an AmmoDrop leave an AmmoPickup where they die, which lasts 30 s. Crates (SimulationSetup.SpawnAmmoCrate) come back after being taken. A player within 1.2 m takes a pickup if any carried weapon has room, and gets its magazines' worth for every weapon.

  • Flinch. A damaging hit kicks the victim's view up and to alternating sides: 0.3° plus 0.03° per point of damage, 1.4 times that for headshots, at most 3°. It throws off a player's aim and, because soldiers aim through the same view, a soldier's too.

  • Stagger (soldiers only, in SoldierAiSystem, based on the drop in health since the last tick). A hit of at least 15% of a soldier's health (a headshot, or a hand cannon, shotgun or marksman round; not a rifle body shot) staggers it for 1.2 s per share of its health, at most 0.6 s. While staggered it can't fire, it stumbles away from the shot, its aim gets knocked up and to the side, and then settles again from further off.

    Each hit builds poise (three times its share, at most 0.8, wearing off at 0.6 per second), and the stagger lasts (1 - poise) as long, so sustained fire knocks a soldier around without pinning it in place.

    Why not players too, and why the threshold? Players are never staggered: their movement is predicted, and a host-side stagger would force a correction. As for the threshold, letting every hit stagger cost soldiers over a quarter of their damage in the room benchmark; with the threshold, it costs nothing.

  • Shot randomness comes from StableHash(weapon seed, shot index), so every peer computes the same spread and recoil without sharing random state.

AI

How do you make soldiers that feel fair to fight? Our answer is to not let them cheat. Soldiers are characters exactly like the player (same components, controller, weapons and collision), plus a SoldierBrain and a SoldierTag. SoldierAiSystem runs first each tick and writes each soldier's PlayerCommand, so the AI can only do what a player could: move at the same speeds, turn at a limited rate, and fire the same weapons with the same spread.

  • Perception is honest. A soldier sees an enemy that's inside its view cone (130°, or within 3 m in any direction) and in range, with a clear line to the enemy's eye or chest. It hears enemy gunfire within 40 m, knows who shot it, and gets told about spotted enemies by squadmates within 25 m. It acts on the last known position, never the enemy's true position once it loses sight. No wallhacks!

    Enemies are anyone on another side: players, the Choir's Lancers and the horde (a swarmling is seen by its head and aimed at low). Only the nearest six in view get a line-of-sight check (SightChecks), so a horde in view costs no more rays than a squad.

  • States:

    • Idle: watching.
    • Alerted: the reaction delay, 0.35–0.55 s.
    • Engaging: aim, fire bursts, keep to 8–26 m, strafe unpredictably.
    • Hunting: walk to the last known position.
    • Searching: where the tree has Search for the enemy, check the squad's guesses about where he went; then look around, and stand down once there's nothing left to check and SearchSeconds have passed, or after MaxSearchSeconds whatever is left.

  • Fair aim. Each burst gets an aim error that starts at 6° and settles to 1.6° over 2.5 s of tracking, plus 0.5° per m/s of the target's speed, so moving players are harder to hit. Soldiers only fire once their (rate-limited) turn has brought the aim close.

  • Taking turns shooting to kill. Ten rifles all trying to hit you at once isn't fun, it's a death sentence. So at most two soldiers (MaxShootersPerTarget) aim to hit any one player at a time, each for 3 s, then they rest 2.5 s before asking again, so the pressure moves around the squad. The others still fire, but at a deliberate near miss 4–5.6° off (to the side or over the head). That's suppression you can see and hear, without the damage of ten rifles at once. Anything else (the horde, the Choir's Lancers) they just shoot at.

  • Spawn protection. A respawned player takes no damage for 3 s (Health.ProtectedSeconds), and the outpost's start is behind a wall that hides it from the courtyard.

  • Difficulty is data (SoldierTuning, read from content/defs/ai/tuning.json by SoldierTunings.Parse): reactions, aim, ranges, hearing and turn rate, following the design doc's difficulty table. It's per side. The file's soldiers values are shared, and its factions can change any of them for the station's soldiers (hostile) or the Choir's lancers (choir). SoldierAiSystem.Tunings holds them, and the system thinks for each soldier (and each squad, and each soldier hearing a callout) with its own side's values (SoldierTunings.For).

    TuningFields lists every value with its key, label, group, slider range and description. The file, the F3 overlay and Studio all go through it, and a test checks that it covers every SoldierTuning property. Soldiers' weapons do a third of normal damage; the threat is numbers and tactics.

  • Navigation is a layered grid (NavGrid, 0.25 m columns) sampled with rays through ICollisionWorld, so it sees exactly what characters collide with.

    • Each column keeps every floor in it (ground, mezzanine, catwalk, stair landing). A ray down finds the top surface, restarts just under it and passes out of the solid without hitting anything (faces are one-sided) to find the next one.
    • Below the top, a surface only counts as floor if an upward ray hits the underside of whatever is above before it gets back to the surface. Seeing nothing means it's inside a solid, such as the ground under a wall. So does seeing only the surface above itself (Jolt shows a ray the inside of a face it starts behind, so the ground under a wall that runs up to the slab of the next story sees the slab's underside right where the wall's top was).
    • A cell (one floor of one column) links to the floor within a step in each of its eight neighboring columns. It's walkable when it's floor, flat enough, has headroom, and every column within the agent radius can be reached through those links. So walls, cover and ledges block, and stairs and ramps don't.
    • The last walkable cell before a ledge gets one-way drop links to walkable floor up to 2.6 m below, as long as nothing is in the way at knee, waist or head height (a lintel over a catwalk's edge isn't a way down).
    • Pathfinder is A* over links and drops (eight-connected, no corner cutting; climbing and dropping cost their height) with string-pulling. It doesn't allocate after construction.
    • Why two steps? On a big level, a search over cells alone fills everything within reach before it finds a way around (half the searches between random spots in a station raid ran out of their 40,000 cells, at 5 to 10 ms each). Ouch. So it searches in two steps. NavClusters splits each 4 m square of each floor into the parts connected inside it (a few thousand clusters per level), and joins clusters wherever a link or a drop crosses between them. A search over clusters finds the corridor (or finds right away that there's no way through), and the cell search stays inside the corridor's cells, heading for the middle of the next cluster along it plus the corridor's length from there.
    • What never changes is built once per grid and shared (NavGrid.Topology, a NavTopology): each cell's column coordinates and the cluster map, of the floor as built (NavGrid.WasBuiltWalkable), so obstacles placed later don't cut it. Each Pathfinder (the soldiers', the Choir's, the mission bot's) and its corridor search (CorridorSearch) hold only their search memory, Pathfinder.BytesPerCell (41) bytes a cell; that's also what a search thread would need.
    • Shut doors. Shut doors and obstacles aren't on the cluster map, so each pathfinder also labels the level into the parts that still connect with its doors as they are (Reachability), again only when they change:
      • A cluster with nothing shut in it is one part, a cluster with a shut door or an obstacle is split cell by cell, and parts any move joins are joined (a union-find; the moves into a cluster that have no way straight back, drops mostly, are kept in the map for this).
      • A search between parts that don't connect is no way at once, without searching a cell (on a station behind its locked doors: 0.002-0.03 ms, where it used to flood every reachable cell, 7-8 ms). Labelling after a door changes takes well under a millisecond.
      • When a corridor's search fails, the search over every cell runs only if a cluster of the corridor has a shut door or an obstacle in it; otherwise nothing would get round it.
      • A soldier going after someone where it can't follow (on a crate it can't climb) goes to the nearest place within 3 m it can reach (Pathfinder.ReachableNear: the clusters it can get to, drops only downward, past its shut doors), not a search for no way.
      • String pulling doesn't cut through shut cells either (NavGrid.HasClearLine with the pathfinder's marks), so a way round a shut door isn't smoothed back through it.
    • So did it pay off? Over 400 random pairs of spots up to 80 m apart on one station raid, every path was found (the old search found half within its budget, and needed 8 to 16 ms per search without one), taking 1.5 to 2.5 ms on average and visiting 2 to 4 thousand cells. Paths were 1% longer than the shortest on average and 13% at worst.
    • A shut door the bot has blocked (SetBlocked) isn't on the cluster map. When it closes off the corridor, a search over every cell decides.
    • NearestWalkable prefers walkable floor at the point's own height within 1.5 m, so someone at a catwalk's edge is on the catwalk, not the floor under it.
    • Cell numbers run column by column, bottom floor first.

  • Deterministic. Random choices hash the soldier's seed with a draw counter, and the brain is plain data. The same seeds and commands give the same fight, which SameSeeds_GiveTheSameFight checks.

Tactics (LastFleet.AI.Tactics, and the SoldierAiSystem.Tactics partial):

  • Cover map. At load, every other walkable cell is probed with horizontal rays at crouch height in eight directions. A hit within 0.95 m makes it a cover point facing the obstacle. A second ray at standing height marks it as low (crouch to hide, stand to shoot over) or full height (step out to a precomputed peek spot to shoot). Points are thinned to one per meter per facing, keeping the snuggest first. The room has a few hundred.

  • Safe routes to cover. Soldiers never pick cover that's more than 95° around the enemy from the soldier's side (behind the player, from the soldier's point of view), or cover whose straight route passes within 7 m of the enemy (or 60% of the current range, when closer). Soldiers don't run past you or at you to reach cover.

  • Cost. Cover is where the AI spent most of its time, so this one's a long list of the tricks that keep it cheap:

    • Cover points are filed in 8 m squares (CoverMap.Near), so a search only looks at the squares within 22 m, not the five thousand points of a station raid.
    • A soldier with no cover available fights in the open and looks again after 0.5 s rather than every tick (that search, with its ray tests, was 90% of the AI's time on big levels).
    • Path searches are capped at four per tick across all soldiers, and by a budget of cells searched: a balance that each tick tops up to 4,000 (ExpansionsPerTick, never banking more), which a search may start while it's above nothing and pays for with the cells it searched, so one long search makes the next ticks wait instead of a tick taking several. The rest wait a tick with the path they have (counted in LastSearchesDeferred), and a search that found no way isn't repeated for 2 s.
    • A path goes stale when its goal has moved by a meter close by, or by a fifth of the way when further off (RepathDrift): someone 40 m away taking a step doesn't change the way to him, only its end, which is found again nearer.
    • A safe-route check for cover searches at most 6,000 cells: a route to cover needing more is no route to cover. It searches into scratch memory, so a route it turns down doesn't cost the soldier the path it has.
    • Covers ruled out (an unsafe way, or none) are kept on the brain (SoldierBrain.CoverRejects, up to four) for 3 s or until the enemy is known 3 m from where he was, so a choice put off to the next tick, or made again soon, doesn't search for them again.
    • Soldiers scattered by a grenade pick cover again a little apart, by their seeds, over a quarter second.
    • Choosing cover scores every point within reach without a ray (points facing away from the threat are left out first, CoverMap.Faces), then ray-tests them best score first and stops once none left could win: the same pick as testing them all, for a few tests (8 a choice on the range, 113 on a station, from 56 and 308). Other soldiers' claims are checked only within reach of the search and 4 m past it.
    • Each cover point keeps its last ray tests' answers (the threat asked about, and whether it protects or can be fired from) until the level's geometry changes (ICollisionWorld.GeometryVersion, which a world whose geometry changes bumps; rays hit only the level, so nothing else changes them): on a station about two ray tests in three are answered so, mostly every soldier's tick-by-tick check that its cover still protects it while the enemy is where he was last seen. SoldierAiSystem.LastCoverWork counts the choices, points, claim checks and ray tests a tick.
    • Looking for an enemy goes over only the soldier's side's living enemies, gathered once a tick in candidate order, not every character in the level; LastSightWork counts the looks, the enemies in view and the sight lines cast.
    • A soldier that saw its enemy last tick looks every tick (it aims and fires by it); one that didn't casts its sight lines less often the further off the nearest enemy in its view is (LookInterval: every second tick within 15 m, every third within 30, every fourth beyond), spread over the ticks by seed. Why is that okay? A few ticks later to notice someone far off is nothing beside its reaction time, and most of those lines end on the wall he's behind.
    • At most four cover choices are made a tick (MaxCoverChoicesPerTick); the rest decide a moment later, so a squad that all wants cover at once (scattered by a grenade) spreads its choosing over a few ticks.

    To see where the time goes, every search is counted by what it was for (PathPurpose: chase, cover, cover route, room, search, roam) in SoldierAiSystem.PathSearches, with the cells it searched, whether it found a way, whether it fell back to the whole grid, and its time, which the developer overlay's AI section shows for the last second. The AI's tick is also timed in parts (AiSection: preparing, perception, cover choice, path searches and the rest), shown beside them. --headless <ticks> --soldiers <n> plays the level as the game does (a LocalSession: mission, doors, director, horde), adds n soldiers in squads of four around an invulnerable player where they have a way to him, and prints each system's time a tick, the AI's parts and the path searches; --level range is an open fight, a station seed one with doors.

  • Roles rotate every roleSeconds of the squad's scoring (data, 6 s). While one of the squad's flankers is still on its way round (it hasn't called out its flank, which it only does when it moves to a new spot, or hasn't reached it), the rotation waits, up to 12 s.

  • Tactical position system. Whether a point gives protection is decided at runtime against the threat: the cover has to face the threat, and a ray from the threat's eye to the hidden head has to be blocked by something next to the point. A point also needs a line of fire from its firing position. Scores add up travel cost, the role's preferred range band, loyalty to the current point and a pincer angle for flankers, and they exclude points claimed by squadmates or near grenades. Only points that could still win get the expensive ray tests.

  • Squad coordinator. Each squad has a blackboard (SquadBoard): the shared target and last known position, strength (alive against peak), and covering-fire and grenade timers. Roles are reassigned every six seconds and rotate: one suppressor, one flanker once there are three, and the rest riflemen. A squad down to half its peak retreats. A squad's machine gunner is always its suppressor, and a soldier with a shotgun or SMG is always its flanker.

  • Soldiers' guns (SoldierArms; how each frame changes the way a soldier fights is set by its archetype's soldier, a SoldierUse). Each soldier carries a gun picked by its seed: a pulse rifle (30 in 100), auto rifle (22), SMG (15), machine gun (13), shotgun (10) or marksman rifle (10). The client draws it in their hands, and their shots sound like it (riflemen keep the soldiers' own rifle sound).

    With an automatic gun the trigger is held for a burst (an auto rifle 0.3 to 0.55 s, an SMG a little longer, a machine gun 0.55 to 1 s). In the open, a shotgun or SMG fights at about half a rifleman's distance and a marksman at 1.4 times it. Indoors, the room sets the distance for everyone. Each gun's damage is scaled (SoldierUse.DamageScale) to hurt about as much as a pulse rifle alone. On the room-fight benchmark (ten stations), mixed squads take about 25% more health off a player clearing rooms than squads of pulse rifles do, and about the same off a player holding a room.

  • Behavior. Soldiers move to cover (sprinting on long moves and flanks), then alternate between hiding and exposing themselves.

    • Where the cover system has them in cover, exposing means peeking the way a player does: crouched at low cover and rising just over it, or leaning out around a wall's edge. Elsewhere they stand up or step out to the cover point's peek spot. They only shoot once they've peeked out.
    • Calm soldiers peek to fire. When pinned down by near misses, or shaken by their squad's losses (SquadBoard.Fear: the share of the squad lost, higher for 10 s after each loss), they spend stretches ducked, firing blind at where they saw the enemy in the last 3 s, and peek out less often.
    • Hits, reloads and near misses send them down.
    • The suppressor stays exposed longer and fires at the last known position even without sight, which lets riflemen bound to closer cover. It only does this if a line from its position reaches that spot (the squad shares where you are, not where each of them can shoot from; this is checked four times a second), so nobody fires into a wall or floor at someone they've never seen.
    • Near misses within 1.5 m suppress a soldier for 1.6 s.
    • A squad that knows where you are but hasn't seen you for 2.5 s may throw a grenade (one per squad every ten seconds). It's aimed with GrenadeSystem.PitchToHit, a little short to allow for the roll, and only thrown if its arc is clear (GrenadeArc: eight rays along it; a ceiling, a lintel or a wall more than 3.5 m short of the landing spot stops the throw). So soldiers don't throw into walls anymore, and under a station ceiling they lob no further than about 17 m.
    • Soldiers run from any enemy grenade within 7 m.

  • Indoors (SoldierAiSystem.Rooms, generated levels only). The level's rooms and doorways reach the AI as a RoomMap: which room a point is in, the doorways of a room, the route between rooms with the fewest doorways, and whether a doorway is shut (found by a ray across it and cached for a second).

    • Soldiers placed a room apart are joined into fire teams of up to four (FireTeamSize), so a station's one- and two-soldier rooms fight as squads.
    • A sighting alerts soldiers up to two rooms away by doorways, as well as anyone in earshot, and a squad learns where the enemy is from what its members hear.
    • Within 14 m indoors, cover and fighting distances shrink to fit the room (4 to 11 m for riflemen), and soldiers stop backing off across it.
    • A squad that loses the enemy in another room takes that room by plan instead of trickling through the doorway. It holds the doorway he went through from spread-out spots out of line with it, aimed at it, for 4 to 7 s once in place (whoever comes through gets shot at with the aim already settled). Then it stacks up on both sides, throws a grenade in, waits for it to go off and goes in together, with its flanker coming through another doorway if one can be reached without crossing the room.
    • Squads taking the same room stay in step and go in through different doorways.
    • The plans cost a few room-graph searches when the enemy changes rooms, and a handful of rays per soldier per step.
    • Going in isn't always right. A squad placed in the station contains first (holds the doorway 20 s longer, ContainSeconds), while one sent in as reinforcements goes in right away. A squad of fewer than three, or one that has lost anyone during the plan, holds the doorway and keeps him in rather than assaulting.
    • Soldiers have no keycards. A locked door that's still shut (or a closet's) isn't a way through for their paths (ShutDoors, refreshed whenever a door opens or shuts; the mission bot uses it too), so nobody crowds against one. A squad that knows the enemy is on the other side lies in wait instead: the same spread spots, on its side of the door and with a view of the door itself, aimed at it, flashlights off unless it's dark, for up to two minutes (AmbushMemorySeconds). It never stacks or breaches, and when the door opens whoever walks through is shot at once. If nobody comes through within 6 s of it opening, the squad makes a new plan through it.

  • Bounding (SoldierAiSystem.Tactics). A squad moves cover to cover a few at a time. At most a third of it (at least one) is moving while the rest stay in cover and put fire on where the enemy was, seen or not (with longer exposures while someone is moving). A soldier claims the bound and the spot the moment it picks them, so two never start in the same tick or head for the same spot.

    • Falling back, a bound is at most 10 m (BoundDistance), and spots past the squad's rearmost soldier score better.
    • Advancing, the squad leapfrogs. Only a soldier at the back of those taking turns (within 2 m of the rearmost, or any of them when they're level) may bound (AdvanceTurn). The ones taking turns leave out the suppressor (the base of fire, which stays back), a soldier that recently found no cover ahead, and anyone pushing or falling back (MayBound, SquadBoard.BoundFarthest), so one soldier that can't move never holds up the rest.
    • A bound goes to cover near the lead's line or up to 3 m in front of it (LeadSlack), or 4 m or more to catch up. It's up to 16 m (AdvanceBoundDistance), or as far as it takes to come up to the lead, with travel costing in the score so nearer cover wins.
    • The squad's ranges from the enemy (SquadBoard nearest, next nearest, farthest and next farthest) count each soldier at where it's going, its cover, so one in the middle of a bound already counts as being where it's bounding to.
    • Cohesion (Personality.Cohesion, data). Every cover pick except a fall back keeps a soldier within the cohesion distance of the rest of its squad, towards the enemy or away. The lead's line, and 3 m in front of it, always counts as within, since where a soldier bounds to has to be somewhere it can hold. A soldier that has cover within it won't leave it for a spot outside. One that has none (at the start of a fight, or after a push) or is already outside weighs the distance against the spot (and still takes cover: it keeps cover that protects rather than fight in the open). Spots within 4 m of a squadmate score worse, and soldiers keep 2 m apart.
    • A spot is only taken if the path to it (not the straight line) stays clear of the enemy, so nobody runs down the corridor he's in, or past him, to reach a flank.
    • A squad hunting an enemy more than 7 m away bounds the same way.
    • Whether a spot protects is a short ray from it towards the enemy, so cover still counts when he's gone to ground behind his own cover.

  • Measuring it (RoomFightTests, LASTFLEET_ROOM_SEEDS=N, about five minutes for 40 seeds). So do the tactics actually make soldiers harder? On generated stations, a bot that shoots whoever is in view (with a human reaction time and a wandering aim) holds a room next to a squad's, or clears rooms toward the nearest soldier, for 75 s. The damage it takes is compared with and without the indoor tactics.

    The surprise: the tactics alone came out about even (a few percent either way: soldiers mostly meet a player one or two at a time and are dead within about five seconds of fighting, so how they go in matters less than how they shoot). What made them harder was the duel itself: shorter pauses between bursts (0.5 to 0.95 s), tighter aim (4° on first sight, settling to 1.1°) and quicker reactions (0.4 to 0.65 s). Those raised the damage taken by 34% when clearing rooms and 22% when holding one, compared with the AI before this change. Behavior trees (LastFleet.AI.Behavior). What a soldier does from moment to moment is its behavior's tree (SoldierBehavior: content/defs/ai/<id>.json; the shipped soldier.json is built into the AI as SoldierBehavior.Default). Every tick, after perception and the awareness transitions (idle, alerted, engaging, hunting, searching, which are still code because they follow what the soldier sees and hears), SoldierAiSystem runs the whole tree from its root.

It's a reactive tree: no node keeps running from one tick to the next, so a soldier never stays in a branch after its reason is gone. The node types:

  • Composites are selector, sequence, all (runs every child, for things done alongside the main choice) and not.
  • Conditions are awareness, intent, check (compares one of the scoring's inputs), hasCover, squadAlive, distanceToEnemy and seesEnemy.
  • Actions are the soldier's own code (BehaviorAction): roam, watchEnemy, chooseIntent, holdCover, boundForward, pushEnemy, fallBack, fightFromCover (the four in one, picked by the intent), fightInOpen, clearRooms, goToEnemy, searchForEnemy, lookAround, topUpMagazine, throwGrenade and dodgeGrenades. An action node's value is its setting, and only searchForEnemy has one.

chooseIntent only sets SoldierBrain.Intent (every reconsiderSeconds). The shipped tree branches on it with intent conditions to the four cover actions, each a FightFromCover with its own mode. boundForward fails without doing anything when it isn't the soldier's turn or PickCover finds no spot ahead (AdvanceBlockedSeconds, 3 s), so the selector falls through to holdCover. The scoring sees both cases as advanceBlocked (which gates out advance in the shipped file).

A change of intent to hold keeps the cover a soldier has or is heading to; any other change looks again right away. A soldier on its way to cover it picked doesn't pick again until it gets there (and 1 s after), whatever its role, intent or timer, unless that cover stops protecting it, it gets stuck (2 s without progress gives up the cover) or it falls back. The shipped advance is gated on range > 12, so when they're close enough to fight from where they are, the squad holds.

The file is a flat list of nodes with ids and children (plus x, y and a note for Studio), compiled to arrays (BehaviorTree) so a tick allocates nothing. Loose nodes that can't be reached from the root are kept but not checked. The shipped tree started as a branch-for-branch copy of the hand-written state machine it replaced. A behavior's scoring sits next to its tree in the same file, and a behavior whose tree never chooses an intent doesn't need one. SoldierAiSystem.Behaviors holds them all, the soldier first, and SoldierBrain.Behavior is a soldier's own (BehaviorIndex(id)). The game loads every behavior file in content/defs/ai (AiContent).

A behavior's Needs (BodyAbility: fire, reload, throw, crouch, lean, blind fire; worked out from its tree's actions by BodyAbilities.For) must be in the capabilities of its variant's body type's graph. After all, a behavior that throws grenades on a body that can't throw would look silly! BodyNeeds (client) names any variant where they aren't (logged by AiContent, and shown as a problem in Studio's AI workspace), and any capability in a graph that isn't an ability or is missing its action (docs/plans/animation-2.md, phase 4).

Enemy variants (LastFleet.AI.Enemies, content/defs/ai/enemies.json; the shipped file is built into the AI as EnemyRoster.Default). Each variant is a kind of enemy soldier: the behavior it thinks with, its side (hostile, or the Choir), health, damage scale, grenades, run and sprint speed, whether it drops loot, the frames it carries by weight, and how often a squad from each source is that variant (EnemySource: a level's squads, the mission's reinforcements and closets, the director's patrols, and the Choir's squads sent with the horde).

A squad is all one variant, picked by the roster in play (EnemyRoster.InPlay, set by AiContent on load and by Studio while editing) from the squad's seed, so it's the same on every machine (SoldierSpawner.SquadVariant). Its soldiers' guns are picked from the variant's weights by their seeds (EnemyRoster.PickWeapon, the same draw the old kit used). SimulationSetup.SpawnSoldier takes the variant's body (SoldierBody). The brain keeps its variant (SoldierBrain.Variant), and SoldierAiSystem.BehaviorOf resolves a variant's behavior by id (a behavior set directly with SoldierBrain.Behavior wins; Studio uses that to try one out). The shipped soldier and Choir Lancer are the soldiers the game already had, number for number. A variant's Body (an animation graph) and Model (content/characters) are what the client draws it as, and each soldier's variant is sent with it (SoldierVariant on a client's replica).

Intent (UtilityModel, content/defs/ai/soldier.json; TacticalUtility scores with the shipped model). Every half second, an engaged soldier scores four intents from what it knows (TacticalInputs: its health, the target's health and whether it's reloading when visible, range, time since the squad saw the target, being pinned, the suppressor's covering fire, and its role). The scoring is data:

  • Each intent (IntentRule) starts at its base (or its base for the soldier's role).
  • It adds its terms (UtilityTerm: a weight times a curve over one input, or a weight by role, each only when its conditions hold). A curve (UtilityCurve) is a from-to line, or keys joined by straight lines.
  • It's multiplied by its factors (UtilityFactor).
  • An intent whose gates fail scores 0.
  • The current intent gets a stickiness bonus (0.1) so soldiers don't dither, and the term that added the most to the winner is kept as its reason (IntentReason).

The shipped file is built into the AI assembly (UtilityModel.Default), so a world without the content folder plays the same, and a test proves it scores the same as the hand-written code it replaced over 20,000 inputs.

Every squad gets a personality (Personality) by share, from its number and the level's seed (UtilityModel.PersonalityOf). A personality is a scale on each intent's score, plus how many of a squad of three or more flank:

  • Steady (half of squads) uses the scores as they are.
  • Aggressive moves up and pushes more and falls back less.
  • Cautious holds, falls back early and sends nobody round the side.
  • Flanking sends two.

The game reads the file at startup (AiContent) and falls back to the shipped model if it has problems. The AI debugger shows a soldier's squad personality.

Searching for a lost enemy (SoldierAiSystem.Search, the tree's searchForEnemy). If you duck out of sight, where should a soldier look? Not where you were, but where you probably went. SquadBoard keeps how he was moving when last seen (LastHeading, from his velocity; a heard position has none) and, once a member starts searching, its guesses (SearchLeads, eight at most). The guesses are:

  • straight on along his heading, as far as he'd get in a couple of seconds;
  • a point 2.5 m past each open doorway of his room and, out to the node's value in rooms (2 by default), past the doorways beyond. Each is weighted by how well it lines up with his heading (sharper the faster he was going; with no heading, away from the squad), with nearer ones counting more and each room further out counting half as much;
  • where he was.

Each guess is moved to the nearest walkable floor, close ones are merged, and the weights are turned into shares. A searching member takes the best free guess for it (the likeliest, with the nearer one winning near-ties; a guess whose taker hasn't worked on it for ten ticks is free again), walks there (aiming once within 8 m), and looks around it for 1.5 s. Then it's checked. An unreachable guess is checked right away.

The action fails when every guess has been checked, and the shipped tree falls through to lookAround. A sighting (Share) ends the search, and a heard shot (Hear) makes the guesses again from where it came from. The searchLeads input (unchecked guesses) lets a check node branch on it.

Barks (BarkLog, like the ShotLog). Soldiers say what they decided or what happened: contact, lost contact, searching, all clear, flanking left or right, covering fire, moving up, pushing, the enemy reloading, falling back, reloading, frag out, grenade, man down, pinned. A soldier speaks at most once every 2.5 s except for urgent warnings, and a squad doesn't repeat a bark within 2 to 10 s depending on the kind. The client (BarkFeed) shows barks within 40 m as text over the speaker's head and as subtitles. The words, with variants, live in the client (BarkLines); the simulation only says which bark.

AI tuning (F3, AI tuning). Pick a side to tune (the station's or the Choir's), then use the sliders for every SoldierTuning value (TuningFields, grouped the same way as Studio's Tuning tab). Changes apply on the next tick. Copy gives you what the side changes from the file's shared values, the way tuning.json writes it, and reset goes back to the file's values.

AI debugger (F3, AI section). A table of soldiers with their state, role and intent. Click one to see its intent scores, the reason, its inputs, its cover and its last bark. A timeline shows every soldier's role and intent over the last 20 s, and Labels writes each soldier's id, role and intent over its head.

Destruction (DestructionSystem, Destructible). Level parts named Break_* (found by BreakableLayout) become props with health based on material: glass 1, wood 90, metal 140.

  • A shot that hits a prop's surface damages it (and stops there, glass included).
  • A grenade damages every prop within its radius at 1.5 times its damage to people.
  • A broken prop's collision is switched off, so it stops blocking movement, sight and shots (the AI's cover checks and sight lines update at once), and a BreakRecord tells the client to hide the part (with a negative tint alpha) and throw debris.
  • DestructionSystem.RestoreAll repairs everything for a restarted fight.
  • Drops lying on a prop that breaks (weapons, piles, crates, cases: DropFallSystem) fall onto whatever is under them; the snapshot already carries their position.

One gotcha: the navigation grid is only built once, so the floor under a broken crate stays unwalkable for soldiers.

Levels (Levels, EncounterLayout). Each level module has an .encounter.json next to it with the player's spawn, the squads (each one its own squad for the AI) and the ammo crates. The client plays a station raid from a random seed by default (Levels.Choose), and the outpost with --level slice. Smoke and headless runs use the grey-box room (--level range), so their numbers stay comparable. FightSession owns a fight on the client: it spawns squads, crates and props, turns breaks into debris, shows AREA CLEAR when every soldier is dead, restarts the fight, and keeps a PlaytestReport for each attempt.

Grenades live in the simulation (GrenadeSystem), so players and soldiers share them. A fresh press of the grenade button throws one along the view at 16 m/s. Grenades bounce off the level and explode after 2.2 s for up to 110 damage within 6 m. They need a clear line to the body (so cover works), and never hurt the thrower's side, except the thrower. Explosions go to an ExplosionLog for the client.

Characters block each other in CharacterBodySystem, in the simulation rather than in Jolt: velocity into another capsule is removed and overlaps are eased apart, using start-of-tick positions so the order doesn't matter. Each character only looks at the bodies in its square of a grid (as wide as two capsules) and the eight around it, in the order they were gathered, which gives the same result as looking at every body.

Physics

The simulation never calls Jolt. Instead it talks to an ICollisionWorld: add a character, move it with a velocity, switch its capsule between standing and crouching, ask whether it can stand, cast a ray. There are two implementations:

Implementation Where Used by
FlatGroundWorld LastFleet.Simulation Unit tests and anything that needs no level: an endless floor at height 0, exactly reproducible
JoltWorld LastFleet.Physics The game, --headless and the physics tests: the grey-box room

Characters are Jolt CharacterVirtuals: kinematic capsules standing on their feet, 0.35 m radius, 1.8 m tall standing and 1.2 m crouched. The controller decides the velocity, and JoltWorld.MoveCharacter works out where it goes with ExtendedUpdate. That slides along walls, walks up steps up to 0.4 m, sticks to the floor going down stairs and slopes, and refuses slopes steeper than 46°.

A couple of details keep movement honest:

  • On walkable ground the requested velocity is first turned along the slope (JoltWorld.AlongGround: horizontal velocity kept, vertical speed added to stay on the surface), so walking up a slope at an angle keeps the line you're walking instead of sliding along the slope.
  • The velocity handed back is what was asked for minus what the level blocked, never more, so stepping up a stair or being pushed out of a wall can't add speed.
  • A character is only grounded when Jolt reports OnGround and it isn't moving up, so the tick a jump starts never counts as standing.

Crouching swaps the capsule. Shrinking always works; standing up fails if the taller capsule would overlap the level. While crouched, the body system asks every tick whether the character could stand, and MovementState.CeilingBlocked keeps the controller crouched (at crouch speed) until it can.

Mantling (Mantle, MantleSystem) only uses rays, so it works against any ICollisionWorld. It needs a wall at knee height in front, a flat top between 0.45 and 1.6 m above the feet (found by a ray cast down from above), and room to stand on it. The climb goes straight up, then level over the edge.

Cover and peeking (CoverSystem, the way Red Orchestra does it) works the same for players and soldiers. It only uses rays, runs every fourth tick per character (spread across ticks), and never changes mid-peek or mid-burst.

  • A character is in cover automatically when something is within 0.8 m in front of its body at waist height and it's facing it within 60°. No cover button needed!
  • If the view over it is clear at standing eye height, it's low cover, and a crouched character is in it (CoverKind.Low; the top is found by a ray down onto it, 0.8 to 1.6 m). The character ducks so its eye is 12 cm under the top (no lower than 0.7 m).
  • Otherwise it's a wall, and the character is in cover beside its edge (Left or Right) if there's room to lean 0.5 m to that side and the view straight ahead from there is clear (it checks the side it's looking towards first).
  • Holding aim peeks (MovementState.Peek, out in 0.2 s, back in 0.15 s, eased). For low cover it rises until the eye is 22 cm over the top (so the shots clear it, without standing up). For a wall it leans the eye 0.5 m out around the edge (MovementState.Lean).
  • The eye is MovementState.EyeAt(feet). It's where shots leave from, where the head's hit sphere is (the top of the body capsule leans 70% as far and the hips stay behind), where the camera is, and what soldiers see and aim at (the chest when it's showing, otherwise the head).
  • Firing without aiming while in cover fires blind (BlindFire, for the rest of a burst too): from over the top, or 0.5 m out around the edge, with the body hidden and 8° more spread.
  • While peeking a character moves at 30% speed. Moving 0.5 m from where it took cover, standing up from low cover, sprinting, sliding, mantling or being downed takes it out of cover.
  • The HUD draws a small half circle beside the crosshair on the side aiming will peek to (above it for low cover), fading as the peek comes out, and orange while firing blind (CoverHud). The gun goes up over the cover or out around the edge along with the shots, on your screen and on everyone else's.
  • Other players see the cover and peek as a byte in each character's entry (since protocol version 17) and work out the lean from the facing. The predicted player runs the same system.

Static geometry comes from the same MeshData the renderer draws. Each part (glTF node) with a body becomes one static Jolt body in its MeshPart.Shape (StaticBodies): a box fitted exactly with its rotation (walls, floors, ramps: the default), its triangles as they are (Mesh), or an upright cylinder around them (Cylinder).

Placed furniture gets its shape from its model's entry in content/props/colliders.json (ModelColliders, ColliderKind; see docs/plans/model-colliders.md): a desk is collided with as its drawn mesh, a barrel as a cylinder, and the part's triangles are what rays hit, so movement, shots, sight, cover and the navigation grid agree.

Parts can opt in or out by name and usage:

  • A part whose name ends in _NoCollision is drawn but not collided with or hit by rays.
  • One ending in _CollisionOnly is collided with but not drawn (MeshPart.Usage).
  • Generated levels also use PartUsage.Rays (hit by rays but with no body: a face that doesn't have to be a box, such as a ceiling, which rays only see from the side it faces) and PartUsage.Body (a box characters bump into that rays pass through).

Stairs use both: drawn steps over an invisible ramp, as most shooters do, so walking up them is smooth from any angle and the camera doesn't jolt on each step. Ray casts run against the mesh's triangles through TriangleRaycaster, a bounding-volume hierarchy that's deterministic, allocation-free and ignores back faces, like Jolt's own mesh shapes.

Breaking parts. ICollisionWorld.SetSurfaceSolid switches a level part's collision off or on. JoltWorld removes the part's box body (but keeps it, so a repaired prop can go back) and tells its TriangleRaycaster to skip the part's triangles. Worlds without parts ignore it.

Debris (DebrisWorld) is cosmetic and client-side. It's a second Jolt physics system with its own copy of the level's bodies (the same StaticBodies), stepped at frame rate on its own thread with Jolt's thread pool (up to four workers). Each frame finishes the step started the frame before, draws it, and starts the next one, so the step runs in parallel with the rest of the frame and the pieces are drawn a frame late. Every other call waits for any step in flight first.

A broken prop splits into a grid of box fragments (thin shards for glass) thrown in the direction of whatever broke it. They tumble, bounce and settle, then shrink away after six seconds. At most 160 are live at once. The pieces go to the renderer separately from the other moving triangles (FrameGeometry.Debris). They're drawn normally, in the ray prepass and in shadow maps, but never built into the ray-traced scene. Why leave them out? So a burst of them doesn't force a ray structure rebuild every frame (they cast no traced shadows and don't show in traced reflections). F3 shows the pieces, the step's time and how long the frame waited for it.

Surfaces. JoltWorld.AddStaticMesh returns the surface number of the mesh's first part. Part i is surface first + i, and every RayHit reports the surface it hit (RayHit.NoSurface for the flat-ground world). Gameplay uses it to find targets, and later destructibles and hit materials, from level parts by name (TargetLayout in LastFleet.Content).

Procedural levels

LastFleet.ProcGen turns a seed and a mission template into a level, in the design doc's three passes. It's headless, has no physics, and is deterministic: one seeded random stream per pass (DeterministicRandom.ForStream), modules in id order, and no decisions in the layout that depend on dictionary order or floating point. Why so strict? Because the same seed, template and module library have to give the same mesh on every machine. That's how a co-op client builds the host's level itself!

The procgen revamp (plans/procgen-revamp.md) moved rooms from hand-drawn modules to designer data, in phases. Since its last phase, every room is built from an archetype. Its foundations (Phase 1) and its structure and styles (Phase 2) didn't change any level; rooms in any shape (Phase 3) change them on purpose.

So how do we know when a change alters levels? GoldenLevelTests holds the fingerprints of the station raid's first hundred seeds, made with the repository's procgen data the same way the game makes them. A change that alters levels records the new values and says why.

  • Procgen data (Data/ProcGenData) is what designers author for generation, in content/defs/procgen. It's read once and shared by every level built from the same content. That's the content's tags (tags.json), surface styles (styles.json), room archetypes (archetypes.json), props and placement rules (props.json, rules.json), networks (networks.json), loot assignments (loot.json), room typing (typing.json), plans (plans.json) and districts (districts.json). Every problem is reported at once, by file, and a file the generator doesn't read is an error. LevelGenerator takes it (or just the built-in tags and styles if none is given). The client loads it from the build, and Studio generates with it while it's being edited.

  • Plans (Plans/) are the start of structure-first levels (plans/structure-first.md). The idea is to build a level from the outside in, its envelope first: the envelope, then the halls, then the rooms.

    The envelope. A LevelPlan (from plans.json) is a list of parts: a HullProfile, BoxModule, ArmPart, RingPart or PaintedMask, each adding cells or cutting them out in order. Envelope.Build makes the cells inside for a seed, each part drawing from its own stream (GenerationStage.Envelope, keyed by the part's index) in whole cells and plain arithmetic, so a seed makes the same envelope everywhere. Each part also records the shape it was drawn at (PartShape: a line with its length and half width, or a ring's radius and width).

    Halls and sections. PlanStructure.Build then:

    • carves the plan's halls (SpineHall, CrossHall, RingHall, each laid on a part and following its shape; corridors first, then service ways);
    • cuts the envelope into sections (SectionPlan: bulkheads across a part, or each part its own);
    • puts pressure doors where corridors cross bulkheads and hatches where service ways do;
    • stubs a hatch from each section's service way onto a corridor;
    • counts the compartments, and lists its problems.

    The stubs and the bulkheads' jitter draw from GenerationStage.Circulation.

    Rooms. PlanLevel.Build goes on to the rooms: the cells between halls in each section are plots, cut across their length into rooms from the section's DistrictProgram (districts.json: an anchor and rooms, each an archetype with widths, weights, runs and whether it's private), with slivers a cell deep merged into a neighbor.

    • Each room's door faces the hall it has the most frontage on; a room between two halls gets a second (usually if shared, rarely if private); a room with no hall gets a suite door.
    • A section's anchor can span up to 16 cells each way (PlanLevel.MostAnchor, ParametricRoom.MostCells: a carrier's hangar), other rooms 8.
    • Halls are cut into stretches within 8 by 8 cells (one two or more cells across only along its length, so each stretch is its whole width: PlanLevel.HallBlock), joined by openings as wide as the hall (ParametricRoom cuts a doorway as wide as its connector says).
    • Every piece is then built as a room module with exactly its doorways (so none goes unused), cached by archetype, shape and doors per set of procgen data, and placed.
    • A stretch of a hall that names a style (HallPart.Style) is built in it; one two or more cells across also gets its style's hall structure (RoomFinish.Frames, ParametricRoom.HallFrames: frames, arches, a colonnade or pillars at every cell boundary along it, frames if the style has none, each with a lamp up its faces toward the hall as a module light), never in a doorway or across the way through (ReachGrid.TryBlock).
    • Its pressure doorways through a bulkhead, side by side, become one connector its width (PlanLevel.WidenBulkheadDoors), and the assembler builds one door across them (DoorSpans: a pressure door or a gate, its frame, reader and sign at its edges); the doorways it spans get none.

    Layout() and Mesh() give the LevelLayout and mesh. PlanPicture draws one for --plan-png.

    Decks and stairs. A plan of several decks (LevelPlan.Decks) gets a structure per deck, its bulkheads lined up through them, and PlanLevel builds it deck by deck on one grid, pieces and doorways carrying their deck and placements their story. Stair cores (PlanCore, PlanPieceKind.Stairs) are placed before the halls are cut into stretches: a two-story stairwell turned to fit near its frame, clear of both decks' halls, its foot and landing joined to corridors by short passages.

    Pins. Pins (PlanPin, LevelPlan.Pins) hold set pieces in place on a part, as cores do, or round a ring as cells from its center scaled with its radius in whole numbers (PlanPin.CellOn, and MovedTo back for Studio's drag). A room pin's room is cut round the plot cell nearest its cell (PlanLevel.Pins, Pinned): the plot's strips stop short of it and it starts about its middle on the pin (round a ring, the wedge reaching it is it), the anchor if it names its district's anchor's archetype. The boarding pin makes the insertion the exit of the recipe's kind nearest it (PlanLevel.Boarding, MissionEmbedder). A plan without pins builds as it did.

    Hangars. A district's anchor may be a tall archetype (flight_hangar, two stories, vertical floor none), built its full height where no deck is over it (PlanLevel.StandsTall); Layout() puts its upper story in the envelope and its outward walls on the hull, so the skin wraps it. An archetype with bay gets a ship entrance (Modules/ShipBay, RoomFinish.Bays) in each wall wholly on the hull with no doorway, a pair across from each other if it has one (flown straight through). For each entrance, ParametricRoom:

    • leaves the wall open on every story but a post at each end;
    • adds the field (BayField_<n>, PartUsage.Body: characters bump into it, rays and shots pass) and a lit frame;
    • faces the dropship's nose to the first entrance;
    • and keeps its pad and the lane in from each entrance clear (RoomModule.KeepClear): out of the furnishing's floor and, in the level, blocking every placement rule (RoomFloor.Blocks) and reserved in its reach.

    The assembler cuts the skin and any wall dressing at each entrance (LevelAssembler.Outside); hull breaches skip those walls, and pod bays skip the hangar. The client draws the field's shimmer (BayFields), and a dropship's hold is roofed so it stands in a hangar of any height.

    Smooth halls. A ring hall (RingHall) is built smooth (Modules/AngledHall, Modules/HallArc): the structure carves it on every cell its band (across meters) touches and remembers its bend (PlanStructure.BandAt); its stretches are cut apart from any other hall's and built round the bend, the walls straight sides of at most 1.5 m, each a box turned to its side (MeshBuilder.AddTurnedBox), clipped to the stretch's cells. A room's door onto it gets a throat in to the wall (a room only counts a ring cell as frontage where the bend is within a cell of its door, AngledHall.Reaches); a narrow doorway across the band (a pressure door) gets jambs so its door seals. The module carries its band (RoomModule.Band, a HallBand: a HallArc round a ring or a HallLine on a slant), so the dressing hangs its panels along it, turned to it, and leaves its cells' edges without trims or services. A bridge part (BridgePart) runs out from a center at any angle; a spine on it is built smooth along its line (PlanStructure.CarveBridge), meeting the rings' halls either end; where smooth halls overlap, each one's walls stop on the other's floor. A ring part's plots are cut into wedges round its center (PlanLevel.CutRound).

  • Plan missions (Plans/MissionEmbedder, Plans/ExitPlanner, Missions/PlanRecipe): a template with a plan block (ship_raid.json) is laid out by a plan. LevelGenerator.LayOut picks the plan from the seed and builds its PlanLevel with the mission read in after the doors are placed and before the rooms are built (GenerationStage.Embedding), trying seeds made from the level's own (at most PlanAttempts) when it can't be.

    Here's how the embedder fits a mission into the ship:

    • It makes an airlock vestibule (a small room on the hull with one door onto a corridor, ExitPlanner.Vestibules) piece 0, the insertion, built against the hull side it found (RoomFinish.Hull, RoomModule.Hull).
    • Zones are runs of the fewest bulkheads crossed to get somewhere from it, so every door and hatch from one zone to the next is a gate (the deeper zone's lock id, every door of a gate its own Lock_<id>_<n> part).
    • The objective goes in the zone before the last (a room tagged for it, or one made into its archetype, of the kind picked by weight from the recipe's objectives: a hack, a download or a self-destruct, with a terminal set up in whatever room it is), finishing it opening the last gate.
    • Extraction is at the deepest exit of the last zone (a hangar the dropship lands in, or else an airlock it docks at: a vestibule's hull side, a room's side wholly on the hull, or any wall of a room on the hull with no doorway in it, ExitPlanner.Dock, PlanMission.Dock; the room keeps the floor before it clear, the squad stands 1.8 m in from it, and the assembler builds the outer hatch in its docking collar with an amber lamp over it, Airlock.BuildDock).
    • Each other keycard goes in the zone before its gate, off the way through.
    • Spawn closets are cut from void cells, or the corners of big rooms, beside the halls.

    The result (PlanMission) gives every piece its zone and role, every doorway its lock, and a MissionGraph in which each piece is the node of its own index, so the assembler, placements, the Director and the bot read it as they read a solver's. The client plays one as <plan>:<seed> (frigate:7), with generation settings after a slash as a station's (carrier:41/s150-cfuel-k150).

    A recipe can board by a hangar instead (Levels/Airlocks/Dropship: the dropship built on the hangar's pad, its hatch, intake and chute standing in for an airlock's, AirlockLayout.Landed), and give rooms on the hull escape pods (Levels/Pods/EscapePods, MissionLayout.Pods; the mission's EscapePod hatches and EscapedTag players). The insertion only cycles once every client has loaded (MissionSystem.Boarding). When extraction would rather leave by a hangar, the squad boards at the end farthest from it, and extraction in a hangar gets a dropship landed to take the squad off (PlanMission.Pickup, the pickup tag: its pad kept clear and the extraction point in its hold; Dropship.BuildPickup builds it with its hatch open). A vestibule's airlock goes on the hull side across from its door, or on one beside it only with the door two cells along, clear of the chamber.

  • Tags (Tags/) are dotted names that nest (room.storage.cold is in room.storage and room). They're the vocabulary every rule uses. TagRegistry numbers them in name order and adds every parent a name implies. TagSet (what a room, marker or prop carries) and TagQuery (any, all, none) match hierarchically. The generator's own tags are built in (BuiltInTags): role.* for what a room is for, deck.1 to deck.6 for the deck it's on, and layout.main_path, layout.dead_end and layout.on_loop. Content can use them in rules but can't define them again.

  • Room tags (Layouts/RoomTags, GeneratedLevel.RoomTags) give each placed room its role and where it sits: its deck, on the mission's main path, a dead end (one doorway), or on a loop (found from the layout's bridges). Spawn closets only get their role and are left out of the graph.

  • Streams (Seeding/Streams) are random streams per stage, per room and per rule, keyed by fixed names. Why bother with so many? So a change to one rule changes nothing else. Code written before them keeps its own named streams, so its levels don't change.

  • Rooms and structure (Rooms/Footprint, Structures/).

    • A footprint is a room's grid cells, in any connected shape (RoomModule.Footprint: every cell of a kit module's bounds, or any shape within them for a built room). The layout places, overlaps, joins and finds rooms by it, and a doorway can be on an edge where the outline steps in (ModuleConnector.Inset).
    • FineGrid is one story of it at half a meter, where shapes are carved and walls are found.
    • StructureMarkers each have a MarkerFrame (a point, the direction into the room, and the direction along it). MarkerFrame.OnWall runs along a wall with the wall on its right as seen from its room, so up is always up. Walls are built from frames and props are placed in them; nothing is rotated by hand.
    • EdgeClassifier finds a footprint's structure: a floor and a ceiling marker for each piece of the rectangles that cover its outline (FineRect), and along its outline a wall marker for each run between corners and doorways (WallOpening), a doorway marker for each opening, and a corner marker at each inner and outer corner (corner.inner, corner.outer; a run of three cells or more is wall.long).

  • Surface styles (Styles/, Data/StyleReader, content/defs/procgen/styles.json) say how a room is built and how it looks, so no designer has to model a wall.

    • StyleBuilder builds a room's floor and walls from its markers in its style.
    • StationDressing dresses every room in its style (RoomModule.Style): its ceiling, upper walls, lintels, baseboard and stripe (TrimStyle, a fixed color or the room's deck or role color), duct, pipes and ceiling light panels. It follows the room's shape: trims run along each straight run of wall (WallRun), broken at doorways; the duct and pipes run along the longest run in each direction; and the ceiling and its panels cover each rectangle of the room (RoomRects), each rectangle lighting itself.
    • A hall's style says what stands along it, what its floor and floor light are, and its accents (HallLook).

    A style only lists what differs from its base, which eventually is SurfaceStyle.Station. The station style and a style for each default deck theme are built in (SurfaceStyle.Builtins), so they exist whether or not the file lists them. Sizes the rest of the game relies on (a wall's thickness and height, a doorway's height, a panel's size) belong to the layout: a style decides how a room looks, not where people and shots can go. Colors are numbers or "#rrggbb".

  • Modules (RoomModule, ModuleLibrary) are the rooms a level is laid out from. They're all built, never drawn: the kit's rooms and a level's own rooms from room archetypes (ParametricRoom), and the halls between them (ParametricHall). The footprint is in 4 m cells, and the height is the number of stories it stands through (Grid.StoryHeight, 3.8 m: a 3.6 m wall plus the 0.2 m slab of a room above).

    A built room describes itself as a scene of nodes, the way a glTF file would: Door at each connector (a 2 m doorway in the middle of a cell edge, on the floor of one of its stories), spawns, the key, terminal and extraction markers, item spots, and a Module node with its role tags. ModuleReader reads that into the module and checks the kit's rules, failing with every problem by name: whole cells, doors on the outline, a clear lane inside each door, every colliding piece a box (BoxFit, shared with physics), markers inside the room, and each role's markers present.

    It also finds each room's spawn spots in its geometry (RoomModule.SpawnSpots): ground-floor points on a meter grid, clear of anything a standing soldier would touch, of the walls and of every doorway's approach, two meters apart, next to cover first, each facing its nearest doorway. Item spots are where weapons, keycards, cases and supplies can lie (the onTop anchor's spots, and the key marker too). The climb marker is the foot of a ramp or stairs up from the ground floor, and whatever gets placed keeps it clear. Every connector is open in the module, and the assembler walls up the unused ones.

  • The kit (Modules/KitRooms). A biome's kit of rooms is the archetypes whose kit names it (the station has 23, in archetypes.json): the insertion bay, four corridors, the stairwell, the spawn closet, the security office (a keycard room), the data vault and server room (objectives), the hangar (extraction), and rooms large and small: the atrium, the concourse, the cargo bay and catwalk hall (two stories), the reactor hall (5 × 5 cells, with a gallery around its core), crew quarters, the mess, the lounge, hydroponics, the lab, the office and storage.

    KitRooms.Build builds each one once, from a seed taken from its own id, so the kit is the same in every level and on every machine, and offers it to every level of the biome (LevelGenerator.ForKit). If a room's furnishing's required rules can't be met in its first footprint, it's tried in up to six, and a kit that can't be built throws an InvalidDataException naming the room. A kit archetype can specify its doorways exactly (doors: a side, a cell and a level each, for a rectangle of one size, with one-cell sides allowed: a corridor, a closet). Otherwise one is drawn on each side.

    Its set pieces are props placed by its furnishing's rules. A prop can stand in for a mission spot (PropDefinition.Marker, markerAhead meters in front of it), so the vault's terminal is wherever the vault rule put the terminal. The roles do the rest (ParametricRoom):

    • an insertion bay puts its squad's four spawns by the wall with no doorway, facing in, and keeps the airlock's 5.6 m clear of furniture;
    • an extraction room's marker is the free floor nearest its middle;
    • a key room's marker lies on its first top;
    • a passage (all of its roles are corridor) and a closet get no crate, items or guards of their own, and a closet gets three soldiers at most;
    • a stairwell's upper floor is a landing (across its north end, with its doorway a story up). Its stairs climb from the foot to it, and the floor under the landing is kept clear first.

    Rooms aren't drawn in Blender anymore. Only the hub is (docs/blender/hub.md), read by GltfNodes and HubLevel.

  • Room archetypes and shapes (Rooms/RoomArchetype, Data/ArchetypeReader, content/defs/procgen/archetypes.json; Rooms/ShapeBuilder, ShapeRecipe). These are the kinds of room built from the seed, as designer data. Each has:

    • a shape recipe: a rectangle, an L, a T, a U, a cross, or a blob of rectangles run together, within a fewest and most number of cells and how much a cut takes; or a stamp, cells drawn in Studio and built as drawn;
    • the content tags it carries (into GeneratedLevel.RoomTags) and the roles it can play;
    • the surface style it's built and dressed in, and its furniture;
    • whether its corners are cut at 45° (chamfer, 0.5 or 1 m) and whether pillars hold up its ceiling;
    • its second story if it's two tall (vertical, a VerticalSpec);
    • a weight.

    A template names the archetypes its rooms can be typed as (archetypes; see Room typing), and never a tall one in a one-story template. With none, its built rooms are rectangles of parametricRoomSize, offered alongside the kit's.

    A corner is cut where the room is on one side only and neither wall has a doorway (Levels/CornerChamfer). The cut is a wedge: a box rotated 45° (MeshBuilder.AddTurnedBox; boxes, rotated or not, are the only solids level collision accepts), with its back inside the walls. The wall runs that everything is laid along (WallRun: trims, the duct and pipes, lockers) stop short of it. Do shaped rooms make layout harder? Barely: LayoutBudgetTests keeps the layout's attempts with shaped rooms close to what rectangles took (1.53 per level with rooms typed, against 1.56, over the first hundred seeds).

  • Room typing (Typing: RoomTyper, TypingRule (CountRule, AdjacencyRule, DistanceRule), RoomTyping; content/defs/procgen/typing.json, read by TypingReader). Before the layout, each room of the mission graph gets a type: the kit's own (built once for every level) or one of the template's archetypes, so a level's room mix follows the designers' rules. A room's domain is only the kit's type where the mission's markers are needed (the insertion, keycard rooms, the objective, extraction, transit: these are pinned). Otherwise it's the kit's at kitShare and the archetypes that play its role for the rest, by weight.

    Rules work on each room's tags as each type would give them (its role, its deck, layout.main_path, and the archetype's content tags or type.kit). There are three kinds:

    • a count: the fewest and most rooms of a kind each deck, or the level, has;
    • an adjacency: no room of A joined to one of B (a room and the one it's reached from);
    • a distance: rooms of A no nearer than so many steps along the mission's routes to rooms of B, and each within so many steps of one.

    The search is wave function collapse over the graph. The rules narrow the domains:

    • pairs of rooms are kept arc consistent for adjacency and least distance;
    • a count's scope rules its rooms out once it's at its most, or forces them at its fewest;
    • a distance's most rules out a room of A with no room of B in reach;
    • and the level's budget of archetypes (parametricRooms drawn) rules out others once it's spent.

    Then the room with the lowest entropy (ties broken by node id) gets a type drawn by weight from the typing stream, backing up when a domain empties, within 4,000 steps. A rule set that can't be satisfied throws a RoomTypingException whose trace describes each contradiction (the rule, and the room or scope). Each room's typing is kept (GeneratedLevel.Typing, NodeTyping: what it could have been and at what weight, and what ruled out each type it isn't).

    One room of each archetype used is built, from a stream keyed by its id, and the solver places that module for each room typed as it (tried 24 times at a doorway, versus 8 for the kit's). An archetype whose furnishing can't be met is left unbuilt, and its rooms use the kit's. Kit rooms are tagged type.kit. The station raid's rules: never two cargo holds on a deck, no offices joined, labs three steps apart. About one mission room in six is built (for one in four of those an archetype is allowed), and a level takes about 145 ms. RoomTypingTests checks that a thousand seeds never break a rule, that typing is the same each time, that a contradictory rule set explains why within seconds, and that the layout builds each room as typed.

  • Built rooms (ParametricRoom, finished the way a RoomFinish says: look, furniture, cut corners, pillars). A template can ask for rooms made from the level's seed (parametricRooms; station raids build from three to five archetypes). Each is built once for the level (LevelGenerator) and placed for every room typed as its archetype. A template that names no archetypes gets rectangles offered alongside the kit's rooms for rooms on the way and side rooms.

    Each built room fills its footprint, with a doorway on its outline facing every direction (now and then two on a long side) and a post filling each corner that juts into the room. It's furnished by the rules of a furnishing (rules.json: offices, a warehouse, a lab, a plaza; the archetype's own, or one drawn at random), placed the same way as the level's rules (see Placement rules) on a half-meter map of the floor (ParametricRoom.FloorPlan: inside its shape, clear of its walls, cut corners and pillars), from the room's own stream.

    The shipped furnishings try seeded pieces anywhere, and each piece is only kept if it:

    • stays 1.2 m from every other;
    • stays clear of every doorway's approach (a lane in from the doorway and inside the room, never on the doorway's far side, where a shaped room's bounds have no space);
    • and leaves every doorway connected to every other with no open floor cut off.

    If a room's furnishing's required rules can't be met, it's built again in another footprint, up to four times, and left out if none work. The room is then built as boxes and markers (soldiers on open floor facing the nearest doorway, items on furniture) and read by ModuleReader like a drawn module, so the kit's rules and the spawn spots work the same way. Over 30 levels, about three rooms in ten are built.

  • Tall rooms (RoomFinish.Vertical, Rooms/VerticalSpec, Rooms/StoryFloor, Modules/TallRoom, Modules/UpperFloor, Modules/Climb). A built room can be up to four stories tall (VerticalSpec.MostStories, the most a mission allows), with a floor, or none, on each story above the ground (StoryFloor). Each floor is planned on the half-meter grid inside its shape:

    • a gallery around the walls, open in the middle;
    • a mezzanine across the end with the most wall;
    • catwalks along the two long walls with a 3 m bridge between them;
    • a stairwell's landing;
    • none (the story is open from the floor below).

    Each is as deep as the archetype says (2.5 to 8 m). Only the largest connected piece is kept, and nothing goes behind a cut corner, whose wedge runs up every story. A room of open stories is just tall. One whose middle story has no floor goes from its ground floor to its top floor with nothing in between.

    TallRoom plans the floors from the ground up. Each floor is reached from the nearest floor below where its stairs fit, otherwise from one further down, with the ground last. Stairs (Climb) climb one story at a time, either as one 7 m flight or as a switchback's two half flights with a landing. To go up more than one story they stack: either turning back at a landing a story up (two lanes wide), or running on straight, end to end with landings between (one lane wide, for a narrow room). The kind asked for is tried first, then the other. A flight is a box tilted up its slope (28.5°, within the character's and the navigation grid's limits). Stepped blocks fill in under the first story's flights (higher ones have a story's headroom over the one below, and railings on their sides).

    A stair position is only kept if:

    • it's a cell clear of the walls;
    • its column is open on every story it climbs through and arrives at, and each landing a story up is open on its story;
    • it's clear of other stairs on the stories they share;
    • from the ground, the doorways below stay connected and its foot can be reached (furniture keeps off it);
    • from an upper floor, its foot can be reached from where that floor's own stairs arrive, and so can everything else on that floor (doorways only where their lane is).

    Stairs arrive on the floor or, if no such place works, on a bridge a story up across open space to a full row of the floor, which then becomes part of it. A floor whose stairs don't fit anywhere is left out, and a room where none of the floors can be reached is built one story tall, as it always was. Railings (1 m) stand on every edge over open space, except where stairs arrive. Each floor has a doorway on each side where it reaches the wall (ModuleConnector.Level), so the layout joins it to rooms on that story, and two soldier spots. Each climb from the ground has its foot marked (Marker_Climb, RoomModule.Climbs), which later placement keeps clear. Floors and stairs use their own random streams, and up one story the plan is exactly what a two-story room always had, so rooms of one and two stories build the same as before.

    How do we know every floor can actually be reached? The tests build the navigation grid with no drops at all, so every floor has to be reached by its stairs from every doorway, in rectangles, Ts, Us, Ls and crosses. They also check 2 m of headroom over everyone standing, and walk a character up both kinds of stairs in Jolt. Light panels hang under every floor (see Station dressing), clear of the pillars under it.

    The station raid draws a galleried hall, a maintenance bay with catwalks, and stacked stores with a mezzanine. archetypes.json also has a mall's atrium (a U of galleries, three stories) and a light well (a mezzanine two stories over its ground floor, with nothing between) for missions of three stories or more. A mission only draws a tall room if it has that many stories.

  • Placement rules (Placing/ and its subfolders: PropDefinition, PlacementRule, RulePlacer, RoomFloor, LevelPlacer; Levels/Dressing/StationPlacements; content/defs/procgen/props.json and rules.json, read by PlacementReader). What goes in rooms is designer data.

    • A prop is a kind (furniture, a footlocker, a case, cargo, supplies, ammunition, a keycard, a tank) and a size. Furniture also has a color, what it breaks as, and its piece's name.
    • A rule places props (one drawn by weight for each place) in the rooms its tag query matches (role.*, layout.*, the content's tags), at an anchor: the floor (places drawn at random on the half-meter grid, rotated a quarter turn or not), against a wall, in the middle of a long enough stretch of wall, in a corner, as near the middle as it fits, beside a doorway, on top of something (the module's item spots and low furniture already placed), or under the ceiling. It also has a fallback for a prop its anchor has no place for. It says how many a room gets (a chance of between a fewest and most, or so many per cell), the clear space kept around each and the spacing between its own props, its priority, whether it blocks the way, whether it needs space in front to use it from, and whether it's required.
    • Furniture models (FurnitureModel). Furniture can name a model from the library (content/props/furniture.gltf, forged from forge.json), and the model's bounds are its size. ParametricRoom and StationPlacements stamp its solids as parts (one box each, keeping Break_ names for pieces that break; what's drawn is a name/Shape part that BreakableLayout groups with its owner). Its Socket_<Group>_<n> empties take designer loot (SocketLoot, rolled by FurnitureLootRolls on its own stream in LevelAssembler, only for sockets within pickup reach of walkable floor), or are tops that other rules place things on. Its Attach_<Group>_<n> empties hold other props (Attachment: chairs under tables, pulled out and turned a little, each checked against walls, the room's routes through and the arrangement's aisles).
    • A rule's arrangement (Arrangement, ArrangementLayout) places its props as a row or grid with gaps and aisles, all or nothing. The aisles are kept clear (RoomPlacements.KeepClear) and reserved in the ReachGrid, so every doorway can still reach every other.
    • Keycards (one per lock, in its zone) and tanks (the template's count, within reach of the hose) are counted by the mission and only placed by their rules.

    Each placed room's floor (RoomFloor) is its module's geometry transformed into the level (ModuleFloor, computed once per module, exactly from its boxes, rotated or not), with whatever was built after the rooms counted as solid (LevelPieces). It's a grid of half-meter cells a soldier's middle can stand on (ReachGrid). The lane in from each doorway and a square around the foot of each of its stairs (Marker_Climb) are reserved before any prop, and its doorways' ways in must stay connected.

    A place is only kept if:

    • the prop is entirely on the floor, no higher than 0.6 m under the ceiling (its ducts and lights), and clear of the room's pieces, of other props by its clear space, and of the mission's spots (spawns, weapons, soldiers, the floor under a hull breach), and spaced from its own kind;
    • if it blocks the way, it leaves every way in connected, cuts off no open floor, and stays off the lanes;
    • if it's used from the front, that floor is open and reachable (and then kept that way);
    • otherwise, wherever it's reached from is kept that way: a thing on a top has to be within pickup reach (1.2 m) of open floor.

    Each rule draws from its own stream for each room (Streams, the placement stage), and is tallied (RuleTally, GeneratedLevel.Placements): how many it wanted and placed in each room, and why each place it tried didn't work. The shipped rules keep a station as full as the placement code they replaced (over the first hundred seeds: 25 footlockers, 14 cases, 1.6 tanks, 6 crates of cargo, 51 piles and 55 ammunition crates per level), in the same time (140 ms per level). PlacementGuaranteeTests checks the first hundred seeds: no solid prop in a lane, and every required rule met.

  • Networks (Networks: LaneGraph, NetworkRouter, PipeBuilder, NetworkSpec; Levels/Supplies/ReservoirFeed; content/defs/procgen/networks.json, read by NetworkReader). Pipes are routed, not drawn.

    • A level's lanes (LaneGraph) are straight edges along its axes:

      • along every room's walls, a band at 3.2 m (over the doorways' 3 m lintels), a lane at 3.5 m (under the ceiling, or up a tall room's wall), and a standoff lane 0.6 m off the wall at 3.5 m (past a room's duct and pipes);
      • risers between them, and up a story on tall rooms' walls;
      • spans straight across a room's open ceiling from wall to wall;
      • ways through the walls rooms share (at a wall's middle, and beside a doorway), and the lintels of their open doorways, and up through the floor between rooms stacked in line;
      • the empty cells walled in by rooms (service cells, found by flooding the outside), entered through their walls.

      No lane goes out through the hull, through a doorway's opening, or up a wall where there's a doorway. Each lane is checked against the level as built so far (LevelObstacles, exact against triangles, bucketed by cell). A fitting in it (anything built after the rooms: light panels, ducts and pipes, alarm lamps, props) leaves it out. A room's own structure along a wall (a hall's rib, a column) makes it a way through.

    • A network (NetworkSpec, in networks.json) has integer costs for each kind of lane (per meter, or once for a wall or lintel passed through) and for each bend, the longest open span it can cross and the most service cells it can run through, a kind (networks of the same kind bundle side by side on a lane, LaneUsage; a lane taken by another kind can't be used) and its pipe's look.

    • NetworkRouter finds the cheapest route by A* over (node, the axis it came in along). Its estimate is the distance along the axes at the cheapest rate, with ties broken by node number. A route through more service cells than allowed is found again with none.

    • PipeBuilder builds it from boxes: a square tube for each stretch, a collar at each bend, brackets to the wall every 2 m, and a sealed duct (drawn only) through a service cell, with a part for each room's run.

    The reservoir feed connects each end to the nearest lane of its room that the other room reaches (by a way up and across that nothing is in the way of), and its valves along the way stand on the walls under the route, where the room's floor (LevelPlacer) reaches the floor in front of them. Each level records the networks it laid (GeneratedLevel.Networks, LaidNetwork: the route, what each stretch runs along, its cost, and the cheapest route it didn't take, found the first time it's asked for). Over the first sixty seeds with a reservoir always wanted, 49 have one (the rest have no deep room it fits in, or no outlet the pipe reaches). A level with a reservoir takes about 55 ms longer to generate. StationReservoirTests checks that the feed stays on walls and ceilings, over the doorways, inside the station and within its open span, and the gate seeds walk to every valve.

  • Loot assignments (Looting: LootAssignment, LootData, RoomLoot; content/defs/procgen/loot.json, read by LootReader). Where a level's loot goes is designer data, by tag (role.*, deck.*, layout.*, the content's tags). An assignment, for the rooms its query matches, can give:

    • a cache of weapons (its table and count, or the template's);
    • a weight for holding one of the deck's stashes (more for each room further off the main path and each soldier holding it, two at most; its table, or the template's);
    • the item tables its supply piles, footlockers, cases and cargo are rolled from;
    • item levels added to its caches, stashes, piles and containers.

    Every assignment that matches a room applies, in the file's order (RoomLoot.Of). The first cache and the first of each table win, and stash weights and item levels add up. stashesByDeck gives decks their own stash counts. The lobby's loot share scales the counts the assignments give, the same way it scales the template's (LootSettings.Share). The built-in assignments (LootData.BuiltIn, and the shipped loot.json, which says the same) put the loot exactly where the template always did: the first hundred seeds' weapons, piles, containers and cargo are unchanged, draw for draw.

  • Deck themes and their halls (DeckThemes, HallTheme, ParametricHall; the template's themedHalls, on unless turned off, and hallThemes, hallsPerDeck and hallShare, tuned in Studio's Missions mode). Each deck gets a theme from the seed, never the same as the deck before. The themes are the template's own, by name, and it can use any it likes; a stock theme with the same name fills in whatever it leaves out (HallTheme.Defaults, also used by a template that names none). A theme says how big and how open its halls are, and names the surface style they're built and dressed in (style), which says how they look (a theme that uses one of the old look keys is rejected, with a pointer to styles.json). The themes are:

    • service ways: one or two cells wide, low, ribbed, no ceiling panels, lit amber from a grated channel down the floor;
    • a public concourse: two or three cells, a third of them two stories tall, columns down the middle with glowing bases, light strips along the walls and runway dashes down the lanes;
    • cargo halls: two or three cells, half of them tall, arched frames, sodium light, hazard-yellow floor strips;
    • labs: one or two cells, slim ribs, cyan strips.

    Four halls in the deck's theme are built per deck (hallsPerDeck), two to four cells long, and the layout picks one of them for a corridor on that deck six times in ten (hallShare), otherwise the kit's.

    A hall two or more cells wide is open across its ends (ModuleConnector.Width and Shift: an opening wider than a kit doorway, centered on the hall), so two halls end to end read as one. Where openings of different widths meet, the way through is the part both are open over (Openings.Passage). Whatever either has beyond that is walled up in that module's own wall (Openings.Fill, which walls up unused openings too), the lintel spans the way through, and the room portals use it. Frames' beams and haunches have no top face for rays and their own body, like lintels. Floor light is drawn only, flush with the floor, with its own low lights (RoomModule.Lights) lighting the room the same way the panels do.

    What stands along a hall is ribs, arches, columns down the middle, or pillars against both walls. Its style's accents (HallAccents) add bands of light along the foot or the top of its long walls, and strips etched into (or the whole of) every face of a post, column or pillar that isn't against a wall. Every piece of glowing glass is its own rect light, facing out from what it's on, so a strip or band broken at a doorway leaves no light across it (ParametricHall.Lights.cs). The Missions mode's map names each deck's theme.

  • Pass 1, MissionGraph: production rules on the template build a tree from the insertion: the main path, locks on steps before the objective (regions count the locks crossed), a key branch before each lock, and loot rooms.

    A template with zones (the station raid has four) builds a chain of zones instead. Each zone is a small mission and its own region: its first room (the insertion, or a transit set piece behind a gate locked by the previous zone's keycard), rooms on the way through, dead-end side rooms (sometimes two deep), loot, and the keycard for the next gate on a branch. The objective and the arena are in the second-to-last zone. The gate to the last zone is the HackLock, which the hack opens instead of a keycard (LockedDoor.OpensOnHack), and extraction is at the far end of the last zone, so the squad fights its way out through a deck it hasn't seen. Counts, soldiers and loops are per zone.

    How the zones grow is the template's recipe (LevelRecipe, recipe; a template without one grows by exploreRooms, sideRooms and lootRooms, LevelRecipe.Of). It's a list of steps applied in order to each deck in turn: the way through first (MainPathStep: rooms on the way, and the last deck's share of them), one keycard step (KeycardStep: its detour chance), then any side-room and loot-room steps (SideRoomsStep, LootRoomsStep), each on every deck or only on the decks it lists. The reader rejects a recipe that doesn't start with the way through, or that has anything other than exactly one keycard step, so every level can still be finished. The station raid's recipe draws the same numbers its counts did, so its levels are unchanged. MissionGraph.Build can stop after any step, for Studio's step-through. A level of one zone keeps its own rules (locks on the way through), with the recipe's counts and no side rooms.

  • Pass 2, LayoutSolver: works zone by zone, each with its own search and up to 12 tries starting from where the previous zone left off. Why zone by zone? So a dead end deep in one zone redoes that zone, not the whole level.

    Within a zone it's depth-first, with undo. Rooms off the way through (side rooms, loot, the keycard's branch) try their own parent first and then any room in their zone with a free doorway. Each room is tried (16 times) at a random free connector of its parent, after up to two corridors, as a random module of its role rotated a random quarter turn. It's kept if its cells are free on every story it stands through and its doorways are within the template's stories, then the solver moves on to the next room. Stairwells are corridors with a doorway a story up, so levels climb wherever one is drawn (about half of station raids). A step budget and up to 60 fresh attempts bound the search.

    Loops come last:

    • First come pairs of free connectors in the same region and story that face each other (directly, or across up to three free cells filled with straight corridors), joining rooms at least three apart in the tree.
    • Then, while the zone wants more (two or three each in a station raid), any two free doorways of rooms at least four apart on one story, joined by the shortest corridor across empty cells that turns corners (corridor_straight and corridor_corner pieces, six cells at most), longest way round first.

    A station zone has about 2.3 loops (it used to have 1).

    The recipe's loop rules (LoopRules, the template's loopRules) say how far apart a loop's ends must be and how long its routed corridor can be, and set targets: a share of the rooms on a loop, and the most that can be dead ends. While a layout falls short, more loops are added (from dead ends first, for the dead-end target), and a layout that still falls short is tried again (up to half the attempts).

    They also give one-way shortcuts across each gate (oneWayPerGate): a doorway between rooms in neighboring zones, joined directly or across a straight gap, with its own locked door that opens for whoever stands at it on the deeper zone's side (Doorway.OpensFrom, LockedDoor.OpensFrom, MissionLockPlan.OpensFrom; MissionSystem opens it without a keycard, and the HUD says "Shortcut opened"). So it's the short way back once you're through a gate, never a way past one: LinearityReport.SkippableLocks floods the layout with each gate shut and finds no room beyond it reached. Spawn closets, the insertion, the objective, the arena and extraction never get one.

    Each level's linearity (LinearityReport, GeneratedLevel.Linearity) covers the share of its rooms on a loop and the share that are dead ends, the longest chain to a dead end, and how long the mission's route is (insertion, each keycard, the objective, extraction) and how much of it is walked twice. The station raid asks for two rooms in three on a loop and at most one in ten a dead end (it was about 0.6 and 0.12 before), and up to one shortcut per gate. LoopRulesTests checks that a thousand layouts meet it, and that two hundred with shortcuts at every gate skip none.

    GridDoor (a doorway in half-cell coordinates, the side it faces, and its story) is how connectors meet. Placement transforms module points, directions, yaws and connectors into the level.

  • Pass 3, LevelAssembler: module meshes are rotated and moved into one MeshData (part names kept, so Break_ props still break), with Plug_ boxes in unused doorways and Lock_<id> doors across locked ones. It also builds the encounter (players at the bay, a squad per room within the soldier budget, the objective and arena first) and the MissionLayout (keys, locks, objective, extraction, and reinforcement points just inside the arena's doorways, except the one the squad comes in by, so the escalation arrives from the way out).

  • Station dressing (StationDressing, part of pass 3) is what makes the rooms look like a station rather than boxes under the sky.

    • A ceiling over every room's top story wherever no room is above it. It's a face looking down, hit by rays from below (PartUsage.Rays), with an invisible slab for characters to bump their heads on (PartUsage.Body), so the navigation grid, which looks down each column, never finds a floor on a roof.
    • Light panels every two cells in each direction, set into the ceiling and drawn only. A panel is never placed over a piece that reaches up to the light, such as a column: it moves along or across the room to a clear spot, or is left out, because a light inside a column is shut in and shadows everything around it. Each has a LevelLight colored by what the room is for (cool white in corridors, amber in arenas, red at the objective, teal at extraction) that only lights the inside of the room. Six in a hundred are dead and six flicker. In a room taller than a story, panels also hang under every overhead floor (a thin, wide piece at least 2 m up and a meter below the ceiling: catwalks, landings), one every 3.5 m along it, the first one always working, each lighting only what's below it. Every lit panel is its own part, Break_Glass_Light_<light>: a glass prop (one shot or a nearby blast breaks it, and the light goes out, leaving its dark housing). Rays hit its underside and sides but it has no top (the navigation grid would take a top for a floor), and the client dims it as it flickers.
    • A baseboard and a stripe of the room's color at 2.4 m around its ground story, broken at every doorway, so you can see the way through at a glance.
    • A duct under the ceiling along one long wall (drawn and bumped into, but not seen by rays: it can run over a tall prop, and its underside there would look like headroom over a floor) and two pipes along the other.
    • A lintel over every doorway, leaving a 3 m opening, except for locked and closet doors, whose slabs fill the doorway (a lintel's underside inside one would open a way through the lock for the navigation grid).

    Notice a theme? Things that hang from a ceiling and are seen by rays have no top face, for the same reason as the ceiling.

  • Hull breaches (HullBreaches, part of pass 3) are weak spots in the station's outer hull for the horde to blow open.

    • Candidates are the middle of every ground-story wall cell with no room beyond it and no doorway in it, and every roof cell (no room above) that's clear of the light panels (where each lit one ended up) and over clear floor (nothing standing under it to catch a body dropping through: a container, a shelf, a column, a frame's beam).
    • Rooms other than the insertion and closets qualify.
    • Walls and roofs are picked in turn from a seeded shuffle, at most one per room, 14 m apart, one for every three rooms up to ten.

    Each breach is a patched plate (Breach_<i>, 1.5 by 1.8 m on a wall starting 0.3 m up, or 1.6 m square in a roof; standing 6 cm off, seen by rays from the front and sides only) over a ragged black hole (BreachHole_<i>, drawn only, 3 cm off). When the plate is blown away, the hole shows. It's in the MissionLayout as a HullBreach (plate, center, which way is in, wall or roof, room, zone). The session makes each one an unbreakable Destructible with a Breach component (MissionSystem.Spawn), replicated like closet doors through the broken-surface list. (The new parts renumber a station's surfaces, so peers must build the same thing: protocol version 18, and 19 with the gates' dressing.)

  • Decks and their gates (Decks, GateDressing, part of pass 3). A station's zones are its decks (a room's region; deck 1 is the insertion's). Each deck has its own color (blue, amber, green, magenta, yellow, teal) that every room's baseboard takes (dimmed), so the color at your feet tells you which deck you're on.

    Each gate between two decks (a locked doorway between regions) is marked on both sides in the color of the deck beyond it:

    • a bulkhead frame around the doorway (hazard-striped posts, a header in that color);
    • a sign beside it with that deck's number in blocks (on whichever side has wall for it; if neither does, as in a corridor, the number is painted on the floor in front of the doorway);
    • and a band of that color across the floor.

    These are drawn only (Gate_<doorway>_<room>). The HUD (DeckHud) shows the deck you're on in its color at the top right, and a banner as you go through a gate: "ENTERING DECK 3", or "BACK ON DECK 2" going back.

    A room several stories tall whose walls stop short of its ceiling (the reactor hall, cargo bay and catwalk hall have 5 to 6 m walls under a 7.4 m ceiling) has its walls filled up to the ceiling above the ground story, 2 cm behind the module's own face, with doorways left on the upper stories. These are only seen by rays on their sides, because a bottom face set into the module's wall looks to the grid like headroom over the floor inside the wall (which gave paths through walls; the gate's bot found it). The assembler records which room each part belongs to (GeneratedLevel.PartRooms; a doorway's parts belong to both its rooms), for the client's culling.

  • In the client, a level named grown:<seed> is generated this way from grown_raid.json (Levels.Load), a developer level since station raids moved over to plans (docs/plans/structure-first.md, Phase 12): station:<seed> is now laid out by a station plan from station_raid.json, as a ship raid is from ship_raid.json. The name is sent in co-op's welcome message, so a client generates the host's level itself.

Missions

MissionSystem (LastFleet.AI.Missions) plays a generated level's mission: infiltrate, hack, escalate, extract. It runs with the command systems, after the soldiers' AI, so it reads this tick's Interact. MissionPlans.From (in the client) turns the generated MissionLayout into a MissionPlan, finding each lock's Lock_<id> part. A level without a plan (the range, the outpost) has no mission, and the fight ends when the area is clear, as before.

  • Keycards are hidden. The keycard rule puts each one on top of something in a room on its side of its lock, in a room its where allows (rules.json: security offices and crew quarters, room.security, room.crew.quarters; its own keycard room two times in five, otherwise any of them; on the room's floor if nothing is free), within reach of open floor. The HUD has no marker for them, so the squad has to search!

    A level's keycard rooms are the kit's rooms playing key (archetypes.json: the security office and crew quarters), so the rule always has its own room to fall back on. If a designer narrows its where to exclude them, the card is left where the key room's module keeps it (the rule's tally shows the shortfall).

  • Spawn closets. After the loops, LayoutSolver attaches up to six 1 × 1 closet modules behind unused doorways where there's room: one per room, with the ones by the arena and extraction first. A Closet_<room> part is the closet's door. Players can never get in. The starting squads use each room's designer spawns and found spawn spots, shuffled.

  • State is one MissionState entity: the phase, keycards held and locks open (a bit per id), the hack's progress, waves sent, and the extraction hold. Keycards are MissionKey entities.

  • A locked door is a MissionLock on the same entity as a Destructible with infinite health, so shots can't break it. Its keycard opens it: the system marks it broken and switches its collision off.

    Why piggyback on destruction? It means doors travel in the snapshot's broken-surface list, so clients' collision and drawing follow along the same way they do for broken props, and DestructionSystem.RestoreAll shuts them on a restart. No new networking needed! The host builds the navigation grid with the locked doors open, so soldiers can follow players through once they're open. Nobody can be behind a door until it opens.

  • Compartments and pressure doors (a plan's level; LevelLayout.Bulkheads and Compartments, BulkheadDoor, BulkheadPlan, SealableDoor). Rooms are in compartments, parted where a corridor or a service way goes through a bulkhead. Each corridor's pressure door (Seal_<doorway>, or the gate's door where it's one) is a SealableDoor on its door's entity. Pressure doors stand open (broken, collision off, open for the navigation grid too).

    MissionSystem.Seal(compartment) shuts them all, gates too, until both sides are unsealed; a sealed gate ignores its keycard and once unsealed is as its lock left it. ShutDoors blocks sealed doors for soldiers' paths, and the horde's field counts pressure doors after the locks and closets. Service ways' hatches stay open: the way round a sealed door. The developer overlay seals the compartment you stand in (LocalSession.SealCompartmentAt) and unseals them all.

    Where a bulkhead crosses a smooth hall (BandCrossing: a ring's quarter, a bridge on the slant), the hall's walls run on through it and its door stands square across the hall at its middle, wall to wall, built of turned boxes; the doorway's passage (Openings.Passage, which room culling projects) is the whole crossing of the grid line.

  • Breaches (docs/plans/structure-first.md, Phase 10; WindowBreaches, owned by MissionSystem; BreachTuning from content/defs/breaches.json; the plan's breaches, BreachRule). Every window onto space (HullWindowSpot, MissionLayout.Windows, WindowPlan) is a HullWindow on its pane's Destructible, which Holds (damage wears it down to nothing but doesn't break it). Its parts (<window>_Pane, _Crack1, _Crack2, _Shards, _Shutter) are shown and hidden through the broken-surface set, so clients see the same.

    So what happens when you shoot out a window? Here's the sequence:

    1. The cracks show at the tuning's shares of its toughness.
    2. At nothing left, it's a breach (BreachPhase.Venting): the pane goes, the shards show, the alarm sounds, and a HullPull entity drags everyone in sight of it toward it (HullPulls.Drift, added to the character's move in CharacterBodySystem, falling off to nothing at its reach), hurts anyone in its core, and pulls out dropped weapons and items near it.
    3. After the seal timer the compartment seals (MissionSystem.Seal).
    4. Whether it's for good is rolled from the level's seed against the plan's permanentChance, and is allowed only if the compartment isn't in a never district and MissionWalk.Finishes (the mission can still be finished from insertion, through the doorways of the compartments left, to the keycards, the objective and an extraction) without it and every compartment already lost: then Lost, otherwise the shutter closes (Shuttered), the air comes back and the doors unseal (Closed).

    The latest breach is in MissionState (its phase, timer, place, pull and reach), replicated whole, so a joined client's prediction keeps a HullPull of its own (PlayerPrediction.FollowBreach) and feels the same pull. The HUD shows the countdown and a marker; the developer overlay's Blow out window breaks the nearest (LocalSession.BlowOutWindowNear).

    • A breach in a room with a door of its own on every doorway but a closet's (MissionPlans.RoomsSealedAlone, WindowPlan.RoomSeals) seals that room alone (MissionSystem.SealRoom, MissionState.BreachRoom), unless one of its doors is wrecked; any other seals its compartment. A room is lost for good as a compartment is (MissionWalk.Finishes(lost, lostRooms)), and only with nobody of the squad in it as it seals (its bounds, WindowPlan.RoomMin/RoomMax); otherwise the shutter closes.
    • What it looks like (BreachEffects, client only, from the mission's state and the window parts every machine sees): mist and frost streaming to the breach while it vents (look.mist a second), the doors it seals flashing amber over both faces (red once lost, and for rooms lost earlier: a window whose shards show and whose shutter never closed), steam from their edges as they seal. Sealing doors slide shut (DoorVisuals), and the shutter slides down (ShutterVisuals, look.shutterDrop), each keeping its part hidden (ApplyTints) until it's home. Sounds: breach_vent loops at the breach while the air goes, door_seal, shutter_close and door_slide play where they happen (GameAudio.PlayAt).

  • Rooms' own doors (a plan's level; RoomDoorSpot, LevelAssembler.RoomDoorPrefix, RoomDoorPlan, RoomDoorState, RoomDoors owned by MissionSystem, RoomDoorTuning from content/defs/doors.json). Every doorway from a room onto a hall (or into another room) that isn't a gate, a pressure door, a one-way shortcut or a spawn closet's gets a door, DoorKind.Auto or DoorKind.Interact as its room's archetype says (roomDoor; by hand between two rooms if either says so).

    • A sliding door opens for anyone on its own floor within its reach (the swarm too) and shuts a moment after, never on someone in its doorway.
    • One opened by hand toggles on a player's press of Interact looking at it (Interaction.LooksAt, from its own floor: MissionSystem.AtDoor), opens for a soldier who stands at it a moment, and is battered by swarmlings at it until it's Wrecked: open for good, and it can't seal.

    Open is broken, replicated through the broken-surface set; PropVisuals and RayParts know the prefix. The navigation grid is built with them open; ShutDoors blocks only sealed ones; the horde's field bars a shut one (HordeField.SetBarred: the way through, but nobody steps in until it opens) and shuts a sealed one. The HUD prompts for one opened by hand and says when one's sealed; the gate bot taps Interact at one in its way. The kinds look apart: a lit strip across a sliding door, a handle up the seam of one opened by hand, both under a cold white lamp.

  • Doors look like doors (StationDoors, DoorLayout, DoorKind; MissionLayout.Doors). Every door the mission opens (the locks, the spawn closets' doors, the airlock's inner door) is a slab filling its doorway (its part, solid as before), with both faces plated as two leaves (StationDoors.Plates, a part <door>/Leaves that belongs to it, so they go together). The paint depends on the kind, so a door tells you what opens it:

    • a keycard gate is grey with a red band and a keycard reader on its bulkhead's post;
    • the lockdown (the gate the hack opens) is striped yellow and black under an amber lamp;
    • a one-way shortcut has a no-entry sign on the side it can't be opened from, and a green band and release lever on the other;
    • a closet is grey over a hazard kick plate;
    • the airlock's door is in hazard paint;
    • a pressure door through a bulkhead (a plan's level) is grey with a red seal band and a white stripe under it.

    A lamp over each door (and on each panel) is red, or amber where it's waiting on the hack or on a hand at the release. Inside it is a green one (Door_<door>, which stays) that shows once the door is gone, along with the transom over the leaves. The client slides the leaves apart into the walls as a door opens (DoorVisuals, StationDoors.OpenSeconds, drawn with the moving models; a door that's already open when first seen doesn't slide).

    At a shut door, the HUD says why it's shut (MissionHud.DrawShutDoor: needs this deck's keycard, opens when the hack is done, sealed from this side, or step up to the release). The airlock's outer hatch is two leaves with ports inside a docking collar striped yellow and black, under a docking lamp that the client lights green while the dropship is docked and red once it casts off (AirlockLayout.DockLamp).

  • The rules.

    • Keycards: anyone standing (not downed) picks one up by walking over it, for the whole squad.
    • Doors: a door opens when someone standing comes within 3 m of it holding its keycard.
    • Starting the hack: hold Interact for 1.5 s within 2 m of the terminal.
    • The hack: 60 s, counted only while someone standing is within 7 m of the terminal and on its floor.
    • Reinforcement waves: three waves, 4 s after the hack starts and then every 18 s, of two soldiers plus one per player. A wave's soldiers wait (MissionState.Pending) until a spawn closet can take them: the escalation's closets first, nearest the terminal, with the door shut, nobody inside, and nobody standing within 15 m. They appear inside out of sight (SoldierSpawner.SpawnHunting, hunting towards the terminal), the door (ClosetDoor and an unbreakable Destructible, like a lock) opens, and it shuts again once the closet and its doorway are empty. A level without closets falls back to points inside the arena's doorways, but never one that anyone can see or is within 15 m of. Each wave is its own squad, numbered from MissionSystem.ReinforcementSquadBase.
    • Extraction: once the hack is done, everyone alive must stand within 5 m of the extraction point for 3 s, with nobody downed.
    • Objective kinds (MissionPlan.Objective, an ObjectivePlan; MissionState.Objective; protocol version 45). The hack is one of three, all started and held at the terminal the same way, for the kind's own Seconds:
      • a download (ObjectiveKind.Data) leaves a drive on the terminal. It's carried like a keycard, but by one player: whoever standing walks over it (MissionState.DataCarrier, DataAt), dropped where its carrier goes down or dies. Extraction waits for it to be within the extraction's radius too, and an escape pod gets it off only with its carrier aboard (DataOut); otherwise a pod is leaving early. The client draws it where it is (GameView.AddDrive) and the HUD marks it through walls.
      • a self-destruct (ObjectiveKind.Destruct) is armed. Countdown runs from the plan's seconds, the alert is held at lockdown (DirectorSystem), and every SealEvery seconds a compartment seals (MissionSystem.SealOneAhead): the one farthest from extraction (MissionWalk.Hops) that isn't the squad's or extraction's, nor on anyone's way down to it (MissionWalk.WayDown), with the gates already open. At zero the mission fails, unless someone got off in a pod first. The HUD counts it down.
    • Failure: nobody left standing. Playing alone, dying is enough.
    • The numbers are in MissionTuning.

  • The Director (DirectorSystem, LastFleet.AI.Director) runs after the mission and paces the fight, the way Left 4 Dead's does. The idea is that a fight should breathe: build up, peak, then give you a moment before the next push.

    • Stress: each player's Stress rises with damage (a whole health bar is 1.2) and with going down (0.5), and fades (0.08 per second) once they've been safe for 3 s and no soldier is fighting. The squad's stress is that of the most stressed standing player.

    • Phases (DirectorState): building up; a peak at 0.85, held for 5 s; then a breather of 15 to 30 s (it ends early once the squad is calm).

    • Roaming: while nothing is happening, 45% of the station's soldiers (not patrols or reinforcements) wander around their post (SoldierAiSystem.Roam). They walk to a walkable spot 3 to 11 m from where they were placed, on its floor, at 45% of full speed, look around for 3 to 9 s, then pick the next spot. Seeing or hearing anything works as before.

    • Patrols: while building up, a squad that's gone 35 s without a fight gets a patrol out of the nearest usable spawn closet, in a zone it has opened, by MissionSystem.SendPatrol, under the same closet rules as the waves. It's two soldiers plus one per extra player, at most one patrol per minute, and at most three patrol soldiers alive plus two per extra player.

    • Threat (a plan's level whose recipe has a threat block; MissionPlan.Threat, a ThreatPlan). Each room's threat is its zone's, rising evenly from the first zone's to the last's, scaled by its district and by the highest scale of its room tags, never over 2 (ThreatRule.Of, PlanMission.Threat). The threat where the squad is (DirectorState.Threat, the hottest room a standing player is in, MissionSystem.ThreatAt) sets the patrols before the alert speeds them (DirectorTuning.Threatened): the time between them and their size, each along the line from threat 0 to threat 1 (reinforcements.everySeconds, reinforcements.size), and as many more of them alive at once as a patrol is bigger. The wait for the next patrol is never longer than the time between them where the squad now is, so walking somewhere hotter brings one sooner. The stress phases still decide when.

    • Where patrols come from: a source closet (ClosetPlan.Source: on a service way, or off a hall with a doorway into a room with one of the recipe's sources tags, security offices by default; PlanMission.Sources) counts as half as far (MissionSystem.SourceFavor), and no closet is used whose compartment or room is sealed, or whose only way to the squad's compartment crosses a sealed one (over the walk's doorways, MissionSystem.Unsealed). When no closet will do, a patrol comes out of the nearest source room instead (MissionLayout.PatrolRooms, MissionPlan.PatrolRooms: its soldier spawns and found spots), while nobody is in it, within 15 m of a spot or can see one, under the same zone and seal rules.

    • The station's alert (MissionState.Alert, 0 to 1, and AlertStage; AlertTuning, the template's alert; protocol version 29). It rises with time on the level (0.05 per minute), with fighting (0.003 per second for each soldier fighting), with noise (0.002 per second for each tank pumping or pod drone inbound), and at once when the hack starts (0.15). It only falls while everything is quiet and calm (0.01 per second), and never below three quarters of its highest value.

      The Director reacts to it (DirectorTuning.Alerted): at full alert its waits are 2.5 times shorter (on top of the run's pace), each patrol has two more soldiers and three more can be alive, and in lockdown (0.9) a patrol comes without waiting for quiet. From 0.6, the run's hordes can come during infiltration no matter how few decks are open (HordeSystem.AlertHordeAt; never on a mission of the run without hordes).

      Its stages (0.35 alert, 0.65 high alert, 0.9 lockdown) are shown in the station, not on the HUD. There's an alarm beacon over a doorway in every room except the closets (two in a big room; StationAlarms, GeneratedLevel.Alarms). It's dark red glass while calm, and then lights up and turns: amber, then deep amber, then red and faster, lighting its room in its own color while the panels stay as they are (SceneLights.AlertStage; beacons cast no shadows). The PA also chimes and speaks (MissionHud). The developer overlay shows the number.

    • Hunting (DirectorSystem.Hunt, the way Alien: Isolation's director handles its alien: it knows where the squad is, but its soldiers don't). A patrol is told to look in the middle of the squad's room within 20 s of the last fight, a room next to it within a minute, and two rooms away after that (one step closer at high alert, and the squad's own room in lockdown). It doesn't know where anyone is standing (DirectorSystem.Guess, by the level's RoomMap).

      After looking and finding nothing, a patrol soldier sweeps on to a neighboring room, two times in three the one nearest the squad's, two rooms when calm and sweepRooms more at full alert (SoldierBrain.Sweeps). From the first alert stage, every nudgeSeconds (three times as often at full alert) the nearest idle garrison soldier within three rooms of the squad goes to look in its room or the one next door. And when hunters who aren't fighting have spent backOffSeconds in total within 12 m of a player, the Director pulls them two rooms further away and sends nothing for 30 s. A hand-built level has no rooms, so patrols go straight for the squad as before.

    • The starting population (LevelAssembler.Populate). Each zone's budget rises from the template's fewest soldiers in the first zone to its most in the last. Rooms take soldiers by threat (objective and arena 1, key room 0.8, loot and transit 0.7, extraction 0.5, rooms on the way through 0.6, side rooms 0.35), with the objective and arena first, and no room over its cap. Then come squads for a second, third and fourth player (SquadLayout.MinPlayers), each about a third of the zone's budget (soldiersPerExtraPlayer), in the spots that are left. FightSession spawns the squads for the players who are there at the start. When a player joins, each waiting squad turns up once nobody is within 30 m of it.

    • Stashes (LevelAssembler.PlaceLoot, after the soldiers): stashesPerZone weapons (or the deck's own count, in loot.json) from the stash table in each zone, at most one per room, among the rooms the loot assignments allow, drawn by their weight (see Loot assignments). By default that's a dead-end side room 1 (plus 0.5 for each room deeper down its branch), a key room 0.6, a room on the way through 0.25, plus 0.25 for each of the level's own soldiers holding it (two at most). A stash guarded by two or more of them rolls guardedLevels item levels higher. Over 40 seeds, a side room holds one about one time in five, and a room on the way through one in thirteen.

    • Waves: during a peak or a breather, the hack's waves wait (MissionSystem.HoldWaves), but never longer than 10 s.

    • The numbers are in DirectorTuning. The developer overlay's AI tab shows the phase, the stress and the patrols.

  • Replication. The snapshot ends with the MissionState, raw (protocol version 5), and the replica keeps it on an entity. MissionHud draws the same HUD from either world: the objective line, markers through walls, the hack and extraction bars, notices, and the end banner. Positions come from the MissionLayout, which every peer generates. FightSession ends the playtest report with mission complete or mission failed.

  • The gate (Phase 3: every seed can be completed). How do you prove every seed is beatable? You play every one! MissionBot (LastFleet.AI/Bots) plays a mission the way a player would, writing the same PlayerCommand a keyboard does: the lowest keycard still lying around, then the terminal (holding Interact), then extraction. It plans on the soldiers' NavGrid with the shut doors blocked (Pathfinder.SetBlocked: a lock whose card it doesn't have, and every spawn closet; only the floor around the door's sill, so a story above stays open). It replans every second and sprints when lined up. When it's not making progress it jumps (to mantle a lip), fires ahead (to break glass) and backs off to one side.

    It doesn't fight: GateTests make it invulnerable and clear out the soldiers, since the question is only whether the level can be finished. Ten classes of ten seeds (1 to 100) run in parallel in CI, taking about two minutes on two cores. A seed fails when its phase, keys and locks stop changing for four minutes of game time, and the failure reports where the bot was and what it was doing. Bonus: the gate found two navigation bugs that soldiers had too: the ground inside walls under an upper story counted as floor, and drop links passed under lintels.

  • The level map (F3, "Level map", generated levels only; DevOverlay.LevelMap.cs). The Layout view shows the rooms of a story from above, colored by role, with doorways, locks, keycards (greyed out once taken), the terminal, extraction, weapons lying around, and players and soldiers live. The Graph view shows the mission graph as an indented tree, one room per row, with the way through down the left edge, side branches hanging off it, and locks on the links they close.

Loot

LastFleet.Loot makes weapons (design doc, "Procedural weapons and loot"). Like the rest of gameplay, it's headless and deterministic.

  • Data. content/defs/loot/*.json holds rarities, archetypes (a simulation frame, base stats and clamps), manufacturers (a stat bias and model names per archetype), parts (a slot, an optional maker, a lowest rarity, the archetypes they fit, and stat ranges), affixes (prefix or suffix), relics, and loot tables (weighted archetypes, nested tables and relics, each between item levels, with rarity and maker biases). The same folder also holds the items, effects, traits, fleet supplies, crafting (crafting.json, CraftingReader) and how a round's push moves what it hits (impact.json, ImpactRules), each covered where it's used. Any file can hold any of the lists, and the catalog merges them. LootCatalog.Load checks every reference and range and fails with every problem listed.

  • The roll (WeaponRoller). One generator, seeded by (seed, table, item level), drawn in a fixed order:

    1. down the table to an archetype or relic;
    2. a rarity (weight × (1 + per-level shift × (level − 1)) × the tables' biases);
    3. a maker of the archetype at that rarity;
    4. a part per slot from the pool its maker and rarity allow (rarer parts favored when allowed, and a maker's own parts favored on its guns);
    5. the parts' ranges;
    6. then affixes (two means a prefix and a suffix).

    Stats are the base plus the rarity's bonus, then every addition, then every multiplication, then the archetype's clamps. A relic is fixed, with every range at its best. The result is a RolledWeapon (name, parts and what they rolled, affixes, power, and a WeaponProfile).

  • A weapon is its seed, so its roll never changes. Here's the catch with that: a player's weapons are saved as their identities and rebuilt from the loot data, so adding to the data must not change them. Imagine logging in to find your favorite rifle rolled into something else... heck no. So how do we add new parts safely?

    • Each part records the data generation it was added in (since, 0 for the first), and each weapon records the generation it was rolled in (WeaponIdentity.Generation, the newest part's generation in the catalog when it was rolled; 0 for weapons saved before this existed). A weapon only rolls from the parts of its generation and earlier.
    • The muzzle and grip slots (added after the first five) roll after everything else, each from its own generator (the seed hashed with the slot), so the first five slots, the affixes and the name roll exactly as they always did.
    • A weapon of generation 0 gets the bare muzzle and the standard grip, which change nothing.

    WeaponGenerationTests checks a set of seeds against the weapons they rolled before the slots were added.

  • Suppressors. A part's traits (a suppressor's suppressed) are added to the weapon's. A suppressed gun's shots carry the flag (ShotRecord.Suppressed, on the wire too). A soldier only hears one in the room it's fired in (its RoomMap; without rooms, within SoldierTuning.SuppressedHearingRange), the horde only within HordeTuning.SuppressedHearRange, and clients play it quieter, with a smaller flash.

  • Parts that do something.

    • A laser sight's part has the laser trait (hip, moving and airborne spread at WeaponStatsResolver.LaserHipSpread). The client draws its dot where the middle of the view meets the world, and a faint beam to it from the module's Laser marker (only on your own gun).
    • A light module's lamp becomes your flashlight's origin when you carry one, with a tighter beam that reaches further (Flashlights.GunReach).
    • An optic's secondZoom (a variable scope, or a magnifier behind a red dot) is a second zoom level that the zoom key (B, InputAction.SwitchZoom) switches to. It's client side only, since zoom belongs to the camera. ViewModel.Magnified multiplies the gun's zoom, and where that gives 2x or more the sight becomes a scope's lens (a magnified red dot uses ScopeReticle.Dot). A new gun in hand starts unmagnified.
    • A hostile soldier whose frame zooms 2x or more (a marksman) glints from its scope when it points within about 11 degrees of you, brighter the more directly it points (GameView.GlintOf). It's a cue drawn by the client, and no soldier's awareness uses it.

  • In the simulation a weapon carries its WeaponIdentity (seed, table hash, level and rarity rank) in its WeaponSlot, so it travels as resolved stats plus a few bytes, and any machine with the data can rebuild its card. A stock weapon's identity is empty. A WeaponDrop entity is a weapon lying in the world. WeaponPickupSystem gives it to a player who holds Interact by it for 0.5 s: into a free slot, or in place of the weapon in hand, which is left where the new one lay (one hold takes one weapon).

  • Where weapons come from. Generated levels put two or three on item spots in each loot room, and the Director also hides one to three stashes in each zone (MissionTemplate.Loot, LootSpot; see the Director's population), at an item level that rises by one per zone. FightSession rolls them fresh each run. Soldiers carry a LootDrop, and LootSystem (a command system) rolls each death once: one in eight leaves a weapon from the soldier table for the gun it carried (loot.station.soldier.<frame>: that frame, or now and then a hand cannon, its sidearm), decided from the run's seed and the soldier.

  • Traits (WeaponTraits; their numbers are in content/defs/loot/traits.json, read into a WeaponTraitRules that the host's world and a client's prediction share) are what a weapon does beyond its numbers:

    • each maker's signature on every gun it makes: Kessler steady hand (more damage crouched), Halcyon restoration (a kill heals), Rustline scrapwork (rounds jam now and then), Vashti corrosive or arc, Choir-forged hunger (kills banked as damage, shots cost health);
    • affixes' traits: of the Vigil (more damage while holding the objective), of the Leech (lifesteal);
    • relics' traits: Sixth Bell tolling (a precision kill puts rounds back), Lanternfall lifesteal.

    They live in WeaponStats.Traits, so they travel with the weapon. Damage changes ride on the shot (WeaponHandling.Fire), and jams come from the shot's hash, so a client can predict them. WeaponTraitSystem, after shots are resolved, does the rest: hunger's price per shot, lifesteal, corrosion (an Afflictions component on soldiers) and arcs on a hit, restoration, banked kills and tolling on a kill, and tracking who's holding the objective. An arc, or a kill by corrosion, is added to the HitLog, so markers, the kill feed and clients see it.

  • Co-op. Weapon drops are sent whole in the snapshot (protocol version 6); a player's own loadout already was. A character's corrosion is sent as a flag.

  • Client. LootContent loads the catalog once and caches rebuilt rolls. The HUD names carried weapons in their rarity's color. WeaponCard shows the drop in reach (stats compared with the weapon in hand, parts, affixes, the hold). Drops spin above where they lie under a beam in their rarity's color. The developer overlay's Loot Lab rolls thousands from a table and level (rarity, maker and frame shares, stat spreads, the power curve), rolls one to inspect, and drops it at your feet.

Networking

Co-op is a host-authoritative listen server (LastFleet.Networking). The host's machine runs the one simulation, exactly as it does when playing alone. Clients send commands and draw what the host sends back. Simple to reason about, and it's why the simulation never has to agree across platforms (see Simulation rules).

  • Transports. ITransport sends bytes to a peer on channels, either unreliably, unreliably sequenced (stale packets dropped) or reliably in order. There are three:

    • RakNetTransport is the real one (UDP, BRUTAL's RakNet binding).
    • LoopbackNetwork connects transports in memory, with latency, jitter and loss driven by a clock the test controls, so session tests are deterministic and need no sockets.
    • ConditionedTransport wraps a real transport with added latency, jitter and loss (--net-sim), so you can try a LAN game as if it were over the internet.

  • Messages (Protocol/, one record per message) are a type byte followed by little-endian fields written with NetWriter and read with NetReader. A reader that runs off the end throws, and the session drops the message. NetProtocol.Version must match or the host refuses the client.

  • Inputs. Every tick the client sends its next command plus the three before it, unreliably. Why the extra three? So a lost packet doesn't lose a command. The host's InputBuffer for that player runs one per tick. When none has arrived it repeats the last one (without its turning), and it folds a backlog of more than four into one, so a stalled client catches up without losing any of its turning.

  • Snapshots (SnapshotWriter, every SnapshotInterval = 3 ticks, 20 per second, unreliable sequenced) carry the tick, the last input applied for that client, every character's pose, stance, health and flags, grenades, pickups, weapons lying around, the set of broken props and the targets, the mission, the horde near the client, and the recipient's own player in full (exactly where it is and where it's looking, movement state, weapons, loadout, inventory, grenades). The client's ReplicaWorld is a GameWorld with no systems, whose entities keep the host's IDs, so events that name an entity mean the same one on both sides.

  • Packing. Characters are quantized (Quantize):

    • positions to 3 cm as 16 bits, angles to 1/182 of a degree, speed and eye height a byte each, health in halves;
    • how long it's been dead as a byte that's exact up to the last 4 s (the client counts on from when it heard; that way it changes four times a minute, not four times a second);
    • the hit direction as two signed bytes.

    A character's motion (x, z, yaw, pitch, y, speed, its horizontal velocity in eighths of a meter per second, and which snapshot that was taken in) is stored together, so a moving character is one run in the delta. The client moves each character on from where it was sent, along its velocity (for up to 2 s).

    So when does a character's position actually need sending? Only when the client's guess goes wrong. CharacterView, kept per client, only sends a character's position again when that guess is off by more than 10 cm at the client's feet (plus 1 cm per meter of distance, and three times that beyond 25 m for one the client can't see: a ray from its eye to the character's chest through the level, checked twice a second). It sends its facing again when it has turned 3 degrees (20 beyond 25 m), and its pace when that changes by a meter per second. In between, the entry repeats what it last said, which the delta makes free. So a soldier walking a corridor or a Stripper running at you is only news when it turns!

    Characters other than players face to 1/256 of a turn and look up and down to 1/128, so a turn changes one byte. Players are always sent fresh, with no velocity. The Choir's bigger bodies add their kind and what they're doing (CharacterKind.Choir), and the client builds each one from its kind (ChoirBodies), the same as the host. The client's own player has its motion left at zero in the table, since its full block has the exact values.

  • Delta compression (SnapshotDelta, SnapshotHistory). Each input message carries the newest snapshot tick the client has. The host keeps the last 32 snapshots it sent to each client, and sends the next one as its bytes XORed with the acknowledged one, coded as runs of unchanged and changed bytes. The client keeps the last 32 it rebuilt and reverses the process.

    The codec only knows the layout as sections. A snapshot starts with a table of its section lengths (19: the header, characters, the client's own player, grenades, pickups, weapons, items lying around, containers, cargo crates, cargo pods, supply tanks, broken props, targets, pings, a Spitter's acid in flight and its pools, the mission, the horde's table and its updates), and each section is compared with the same section of the baseline, so a list that grows doesn't shift anything after it. Within a list, each entity keeps its slot from snapshot to snapshot (StableSlots, shared by every client), and one that goes away leaves a hole the size it took (an ID of zero, then how many bytes to skip) for the next one to fill. So an entity that isn't moving is a run of zeros, and a moving one is a few bytes.

    A snapshot made against a baseline the client no longer has is dropped and counted, and the host sends whole snapshots until an acknowledgment it can use arrives. One gotcha worth knowing: anything that changes is sent again until the client acknowledges it, which takes about a round trip. That means something that changes now and then costs as much as something that changes every snapshot, which is why angles are held to a few degrees and far characters to their steps.

  • Events (TickEvents: shots, hits, explosions, breaks, barks) go with each snapshot. All the ticks since the last one go in one message (each with its tick), reliably in order on channel 1, and they drive the same presentation code on the host and clients: hit markers, kill feed, debris, bark lines.

    • Shots and hits are packed (EventCodec). A hit carries its shot (origin, direction and frame come from the hit), so only launcher rounds in flight are sent on their own. Positions are to 3 cm and directions to a hundredth of a degree. A bite on a character doesn't say where (the client puts it on the character).
    • A client hears every bite on its own player (each one turns its damage indicator), but only one per quarter second on each other character (a sound and a flinch). It gets no shot or hit more than 60 m from its player (except its own and those on it): a fight across the station reaches it in the snapshot (who is dead), not as tracers nobody nearby would see.
    • Of other players' fire, it only gets three pellets of a shotgun round, and beyond 15 m it gets the impacts of every other round (every fourth into a wall; the round's shot is still sent on its own, for its sound and tracer). Kills always come through.
    • Pickups are sent with their timers to the second, and the client's own player with its counting-up timers (time since it last fired, was hurt, stood on the ground) only as precisely as anything reads them.
    • Acid globs are sent as thrown (where from, how fast, when; the client flies them the same way) and pools as left (where, how wide, from when to when), so neither changes once sent.

  • Drawing. Other characters are drawn InterpolationTicks (6, 100 ms: two snapshots) behind the newest snapshot, interpolated between the two snapshots around that time.

  • Prediction (PlayerPrediction). Nobody wants to wait a round trip for their own feet to move, so a client runs its own player in a small world of its own: the host's controller, mantle, loadout, weapon and body systems, in the host's order, against a JoltWorld of the level with broken props switched off the same way the host's are. Every other character is in it as a PuppetTag entity, placed from the replica and never moved, so the predicted player bumps into them. Each command runs there the tick it's made.

    When a snapshot arrives, the predicted player takes the host's state (which reflects the commands up to the snapshot's acknowledged one), the capsule is placed to match (ICollisionWorld.PlaceCharacter), and the newer commands run again. The change in position becomes VisualOffset, which fades by 18% per tick; over 2 m (a respawn) it snaps. Health, grenades, flinch and anything else the host decides come from the host. Mispredictions mostly come from the host holding a late command for a tick (after which it keeps one in hand) and from other characters pushing you.

  • Downed, revive, drag and pings (Simulation). LifeSystem downs a player (with Rescue) at zero health when a teammate is still standing. Their command is cut down to DownedCommand (look, sidearm, reload, ping) and they bleed out. RescueSystem runs next.

    • Holding Interact within reach revives (and holsters the reviver's weapon through WeaponState.Holstered).
    • Drag toggles dragging (MovementState.Burdened slows the dragger in the controller, and the downed player is placed behind them).
    • Both flags are ordinary replicated state, so a client's prediction follows them without running the rescue rules.
    • PingSystem turns a Ping press into a Ping entity (enemy or place), replicated in snapshots.
    • Players' Rescue goes in every character record and in the client's own block.

  • Clock pacing and the jitter buffer. Each snapshot says how many of the client's commands were waiting on the host, and how many it should keep waiting (protocol version 7). A client below that runs its clock 4% fast, and one more than one above it runs 3% slow, so a late packet finds a command already waiting instead of making the host repeat the last one (which the prediction can't know about).

    So how big should the buffer be? The host sizes it per client from when its input messages arrive (ArrivalJitter). On a perfectly even link, the host tick a message lands on minus its newest command's number would never change. The jitter is the running mean of how much that changes from one message to the next (RFC 3550's estimator, in ticks). Three times the jitter, at least two and at most six commands, is kept waiting. Each one is a tick of input delay, so an ordinary link keeps two, as before. In the soak test at 80 ms of jitter, the host repeated a command 7 times in 120 client-seconds instead of 25.

  • Lag compensation (LagCompensation, Simulation). When hosting, a system after the body system records every character's feet and movement state at the end of each tick, for 24 ticks. Each input message carries the tick the client was drawing others at. NetHost passes it along with the command, and ShotResolutionSystem tests that shooter's shots against characters as they were then (interpolated between ticks, at most MaxRewindTicks = 20 back).

    Swarmlings aren't recorded. Why not? A client is only sent some of them each snapshot and moves the rest along their paths, so what it shows is a little off from anywhere they actually were. Instead, the host knows where each client was drawing each swarmling (it keeps the same tracks the client draws from: SwarmView.TryShown, through LagCompensation.Horde) and tests that client's shots against those.

    Other characters work the same way. A client moves each one along the velocity it was last sent, and is only sent it again when it strays (see Packing), so it draws them up to a few tens of centimeters from where they actually were. CharacterView keeps where it told the client each one was at each of the last 16 snapshots, and LagCompensation.Characters tests the client's shots against that, between the two snapshots around its view tick, the way the client draws them (stance and eye height from the history). In the horde soak, the host knows where each client drew every other character to within a millimeter (95th percentile). The host's own player and the soldiers shoot at the present.

  • Predicted hit markers (PredictedHits). A client resolves its own predicted shots the tick it fires them, against the level and the other characters where it draws them (which is where the host's rewind will put them), so a hit on an enemy shows its marker a round trip before the host confirms it: body or precision, and a kill when the shot's damage at that range is at least the health last sent. Teammates and practice targets are left to the host. CombatFeedback remembers the shots it marked, and the host's hit for the same shot (its ShotIndex) shows no second marker, only a kill the prediction missed. A predicted hit that the host calls a miss has already been shown, as in other shooters.

  • The lobby (Lobby/, protocol version 9). Between missions everyone is in a lobby: the host's LobbyHost (alone, or opened on a hosting transport) or a friend's, joined with a LobbyClient.

    • A client's first message is the usual Hello. The lobby checks the version, the content and the four-player limit, and makes names unique, since carried weapons are kept by name.
    • A level travels as its seed, so peers must have the same data. The hello carries the client's ContentFingerprint (a stable hash of the build's content/defs, content/modules and content/props, by path and bytes; GameContent), and a host that sets its own turns away a client with a different one (RejectReason.ContentMismatch; Handshake, shared by the lobby and session hosts; protocol version 40). The game sets it on its lobby host and client. Missions run over the lobby's connection, so the lobby is the gate.
    • After LobbyWelcome, the host sends LobbyState whenever it changes: the mission set up (by level name), whether one is running, a line on how the last one went, and each member's name, ready flag, whether they're in the mission and what they carry. Clients send LobbyReady, and the host starts whenever it wants.
    • The lobby's connection stays open from mission to mission. BeginMission gives the mission's NetHost a RelayTransport on top of it, injects a Connected for each member and sends them MissionStart. Each client's LobbyClient makes its own relay for the mission's NetClient, which then says hello to the mission as if it had just connected. Each lobby passes the mission's messages to its relay (on the host, only after that member's mission hello, so nothing from an older mission slips through) and keeps its own (MissionLeave, MissionRejoin, MissionEnd).
    • EndMission detaches the relay first, so the mission's host, disposed afterwards, doesn't disconnect anyone. A late arrival goes straight into a mission that's running, and a client who leaves it is only a Disconnected as far as the mission is concerned.

  • The hub (HubLevel, Levels.Hub). Between missions the host (or a player alone) plays the ship's hangar deck, built in Blender (content/modules/hub/hangar_deck.gltf, docs/blender/hub.md) and the same everywhere. HubLevel.Load reads it into a HubLayout, and rejects a file where something that collides isn't a box or a marker is missing (Spawn_Player, where the squad comes in; Station_Console, Station_NeedsBoard, Station_Armory and Station_StarMap, where each station is used from). It's a level with no fight (FightSession never calls it clear), started like a mission through the lobby (LobbyHost.BeginMission(level, keepSetUp: true), which plays it without making it the mission that's set up; Playing is what's being played), so friends in the lobby are on the deck too, and late arrivals come straight onto it.

    Its stations (HubStation, HubLayout.StationAt, 2.2 m):

    • the mission console opens the lobby screen over the deck;
    • the needs board shows the fleet (NeedsBoard: the Scourge, the supplies running shortest, the ships, the last jump and haul, and the refinery; FleetState, saved in fleet.json);
    • the armory opens the inventory.

    Launching from the console keeps what everyone has on the deck (parts fitted, weapons swapped) and ends the deck for everyone before the mission begins. When a mission ends, or you leave it, you come back to the deck with how it went. Opening the lobby to friends (or closing it) restarts the deck on the new connection. If the deck won't load, you get the plain lobby screen instead.

  • Missions from the lobby, and what players carry. A station's name carries the lobby's GenerationSettings after a slash (station:42/z3-s150-l50-f2-b6): soldiers and loot as shares of the template's, and decks, stories and built rooms, which only the grown raid (grown:<seed>) uses; a station is laid out by its plan, so the lobby and the star map set only its soldiers, loot and cargo. They're applied to the template before generating, so every machine builds the same level from the name.

    The host's LoadoutStore keeps, by player name, the weapons each player got out with (as WeaponIdentity values, rebuilt from the loot data, or a stock weapon's class), saved in loadouts.json beside the settings. A LocalSession arms each player from it as they spawn (SimulationSetup.Arm). So what do you get to keep?

    • When the mission is won, the players still standing keep what they carry (LocalSession.Squad).
    • Anyone else who played it, and everyone when it's lost or abandoned, goes back to the stock kit.
    • A player who dies drops the rolled weapons they carried where they fell, with their ammunition, and respawns with the stock kit (LifeSystem.DropFoundWeapons).
    • Restarting a fight keeps them.

  • The client's sessions. GameClient owns the window, the lobby, the menu and, during a mission, a GameView, which holds one IGameSession and everything drawn from it (models, feedback, debris, HUD, overlay). Starting a mission creates the view, and ending it (the host, eight seconds after the banner, or Back to lobby) disposes it.

    • The host's (or a lone player's) mission goes through a PendingLevel. The level is generated and its LocalSession built (collision, navigation, cover, squads: a few seconds for a station raid) on a worker thread, which reports its stage while the window draws a loading screen. Once that's done, the mesh goes to the GPU and the GameView is created on the main thread.
    • A client's mission goes through a PendingMission. The level loads on a worker thread while the mission's handshake (a PendingJoin over the relay) waits for the host, which answers once its own level is built.
    • With --level, --seed or a smoke run, the first mission builds behind the startup splash. Otherwise the game opens in the lobby. A smoke run builds right away, so its frames are the same every time.
    • The sessions are LocalSession (the simulation, with a NetHost when the lobby is open) and RemoteSession (a NetClient, its replica and the prediction). Only a local session has physics, AI, navigation, the fight's report and restart.

The horde

So how do you put 300 monsters on screen without melting the CPU? You don't make them characters! The Choir's swarmlings (LastFleet.AI/Horde, design doc "Horde simulation") have no controller, no Jolt body, no brain. A swarmling is a WorldPosition (its feet), ViewAngles (its facing), Health, Faction (Choir) and Swarmling (velocity, the navigation cell under it, what it's doing). docs/horde.md has the tuning.

  • One field for all of them (HordeField). Rather than pathfinding for every swarmling, they all share one field. It has two levels, like the soldiers' NavClusters:

    • Every 12 ticks, a search from the players outwards over clusters (each 4 m square of each floor of the navigation grid) gives each cluster the next one towards the nearest player (downed players count as a little nearer than standing ones).
    • Inside a cluster, a small field, made the first time it's needed and then kept, gives each cell the step towards that next cluster (or the ledge to drop off).

    The grid is built with the mission's doors open, so each doorway's floor is its own cluster, and a shut door closes it: the field goes around it, and nobody walks through.

  • Movement (HordeSystem, a late system). It runs after CharacterBodySystem, so it moves after everyone else and its bites, logged as hits, survive this tick's clearing of the HitLog. Each swarmling runs down the field, or straight at its target once it's within 8 m and in the open (a clear line on the grid, checked every fourth tick). It keeps 0.7 m from the others using a spatial hash of 1 m squares, and steps from cell to linked cell, sliding along whatever stops it, never leaving the grid. Drops are a short fall.

  • Bites. Within 1.35 m it stops and winds up for 0.3 s (the telegraph: it rears, its blades rise, it shrieks), then bites for 7 if the target is still within 1.9 m. A downed player loses 1.5 s of bleed-out instead. A bite is a HitRecord from the swarmling with no distance (origin and point the same), logged with HitLog.AddMelee so the shot totals only count shots. Clients draw no tracer or whiz for it, and the damage indicator points at the biter.

  • Hits. ShotResolutionSystem tests swarmlings after characters (SwarmVolumes: a low capsule body and the head out in front of it, which takes precision hits) and knocks them back instead of flinching them. 20 damage or more staggers one, breaking its lunge. Grenades damage them, knock them away and stagger them. A client's shots are tested against the swarmlings where that client was drawing them (see Networking, lag compensation).

  • Hordes come when the mission calls them:

    • every 75 s while the squad infiltrates (from 50 s in, and only while the Director is building up);
    • with every hack wave;
    • at the start of the extraction;
    • and as a trickle through the hack and the extraction.

    Sizes grow with the number of players. They come out of the floor 25 to 55 m away by walking distance, in a cluster no player can see, six per tick. At most 300 are alive at once. HordeSystem.Call sends one at any time (--horde, the F3 overlay's buttons). The field is made while the level loads (Prepare).

  • Through hull breaches. While the field is made, each breach gets a way in (Breachway): where bodies come out (in front of a wall's hole, a third of the way up it, or a body's height under a roof's) and the floor they land on (a step out from the wall, or straight down). A breach with something in front of it or no floor under it gets none.

    A fresh horde blows one open: every hack wave's horde and the extraction's, and half of the infiltration hordes, at most one every 25 s. It has to land 18 to 55 m from the players by walking distance, with nobody within 6 m of where it lands. The plate breaks (DestructionSystem.BreakOpen, thrown into the room), and a second later the horde comes out one body every 0.07 s, each one hopping out of the wall or dropping from the roof (SwarmlingAction.Fall) to a floor cell around the landing spot. The Choir's bigger bodies don't fit, so they come out of the floor somewhere else. Once open, a breach is a way in for later hordes and trickles (seven in ten of those that have nothing to blow open, if it's in reach), 40 bodies at a time.

    The client sees the plate break like any prop (debris), and for a breach's plate it also shows a flash, plays the blast's sound and has smoke pour out of the hole for 9 s (CombatFeedback.RecordBreach). --horde <n> --breach <i> sends a horde through breach i right away.

  • Climbers (SwarmSurface). Three in ten of the horde, when their target is more than 10 m away, look now and then (every 16 ticks) for a wall within 3 m, along any of the four directions a station's walls run, that goes all the way up to the ceiling (a ray at waist height and another just under the ceiling find the same face, and nothing sticks out of it on the way up). They make for it and climb it at 3.2 m/s (SwarmSurface.WallEast and the others: the body stands out from the wall, head up). At the top they go over onto the ceiling (SwarmSurface.Ceiling: feet on it, body hanging down) and follow the field along it at 85% speed, cell by cell, as long as each cell has a ceiling (found by a ray up the first time it's asked, then kept; 2.4 to 7.6 m over the floor) within 0.7 m of the last one.

    They let go within 4.5 m of their target (measured over the floor), but never nearer than 1.8 m: right over someone, they drop off to the side. They also drop where the ceiling ends or the route falls away, when nobody is left to go for, and when a heavy hit staggers them (off a wall, they're pushed away from it). They can't bite until they're down. Shots and grenades find them where they hang (SwarmVolumes.Of takes the surface into account: the body stands out from it), and so do soldiers. The client draws them rotated to the surface (the instance's last float, read by swarm.slang and swarm_shadow.slang).

  • The fleet (FleetState, FleetSim, in LastFleet.AI.Fleet; it's the host's, saved in fleet.json after every mission, with one life). Seven ships (the flagship, a farm, a refinery, a hospital, a fabricator and two transports), four thousand people, morale, and stores of the seven fleet supplies (fuel, food, water, medical supplies, munitions, alloy, electronics; Items.FleetSupplies), starting with eight jumps' worth.

    • Launching a mission, the fleet jumps (see the star map) if it has the fuel (4 per ship), which opens the gap on the Scourge by 1.3, give or take. If it's been caught (the Scourge's distance at or below zero), it makes an emergency jump at double the fuel. Without the fuel it drifts, and the Scourge closes half a mission more.
    • After each mission, won, lost or abandoned, what reached the fleet goes into the stores (a win's haul, plus what cargo pods' drones took, which counts on a loss too). The Scourge closes by the time spent (a mission's worth, depending on difficulty: 0.75, 1 or 1.25, and 3% more each mission), and if it has caught the fleet, its boarders hit a ship.
    • Each jump uses a jump's worth of everything: food (one unit per 160 people), water (one per 200, plus the farm's), medical supplies, munitions (more when caught), alloy (more with damaged ships, which it repairs) and electronics. Shortages kill people (from the transports first), cost morale and wear ships down (a ship with no condition left is lost with everyone aboard), and broken morale causes desertions. The farm grows food when there's water.

    All of that is each supply's rules, as data in content/defs/loot/supplies.json (SupplyDefinition, in LastFleet.Simulation.Supplies; read by SupplyReader, LootCatalog.Supplies, put in play by FleetSupplies.Use; without data it's FleetSupplies.Shipped, which is the same). The rules say:

    • what a jump costs (Use, parts that add up: per jump, per ship, per damaged ship, so many people rounding up or down, while a kind of ship is flying, while the Scourge is on the fleet; FleetSim.PerJump);
    • what running short does (Shortage, by the units short: people lost to a cause, morale, a ship worn down with a message, or just a message);
    • what having enough does (Stocked: morale, repairs, an item produced, each possibly only while a kind of ship is flying);
    • whether the jump itself burns it (Jumps: fuel; every such supply must have enough to jump);
    • what boarders take, how many jumps' worth a new fleet starts with, and the kinds of station that mostly hold it (the star map's stations are of those kinds, then two of a mix).

    Which supplies a station keeps in tanks comes from its mission template (its tank kinds' and reservoir's supplies). Every fleet supply item needs rules. FleetState.Version is 3 (a star system's Focus is a supply's item id). If the flagship is lost, or nobody is left, the run is over: you get a new fleet, and the kept weapons go with the old one. Cargo crates now also hold food, and fuel and water come from the stations' tanks. The needs board shows the Scourge, the supplies running shortest (jumps of reserve for each), the ships and what happened at the last jump (FleetSim.Needs).

  • The star map (StarMap, StarSystem). The fleet crosses sectors of ten to fourteen systems, from an entry on the left to a gate on the right. Each system links to its nearest neighbors ahead (and one back), so every system leads on to the gate.

    Each has a station or a ship to raid (StarSystem.Target, a CampaignTarget of CampaignTuning, content/defs/campaign.json: stations, and frigates, carriers and strike carriers by weight from the sector each turns up in, rolled on a stream of their own; the entry and the gate always stations; a ship is marked with a diamond and named by its kind; Levels.Of plays a station as station:<seed>/…, a ship as <plan>:<seed>/…): its seed, decks (more in later sectors; a plan's level has its plan's), how well it's guarded, and what its cargo mostly holds (a fuel depot, an ice mine, a shipyard, ...). That last part travels in the level's name (GenerationSettings.CargoFocus, a supply's item id, and CargoPercent, -cfuel and -k: two crates in three are that supply, and there are half again as many crates; for a supply kept in tanks, one more tank, holding only that). What a station can hold, each in its item's color, comes from the data (StationSupplies, put in play from the loot data by LootContent).

    At the ship's star map table the host picks a system one jump away and sets course for it (FleetState.Destination), which sets up that system's station as the mission at the console. Launching a station raid (FleetSim.Launch) jumps the fleet there (fuel, the gap on the Scourge, a jump's worth of everything used). After the mission (FleetSim.AfterMission) the haul is stocked and the Scourge gains by the time it took. A mission at the gate takes the fleet into the next sector (a new map, and the trail half again harder to follow). With no course set, the fleet heads for the system nearest the gate.

  • Knowledge and the Scourge on the map (StarMap.Observe, StarMap.ScourgeSystem). The fleet's sensors reach one jump: its own system and the ones next to it are seen (StarSystem.Seen: their stations are known), and the rest of the sector is just charted lanes. The Scourge follows the fleet's trail (FleetState.Trail, the systems jumped through in this sector). It's as many systems back along the trail as its distance in jumps, or not in the sector yet. When it's in sensor range it's sighted (SeenAt, SeenMission).

    After that the map shows where it was last seen, in a haze that grows with each mission since, and the board gives a long-range estimate of how far behind it is, rougher the older the sighting (FleetSim.ScourgeText). Here's the sneaky bit: that estimate, and the color of the board's Scourge line, come from the fleet's own reckoning (FleetState.ScourgeGuess), not from where the Scourge really is. The reckoning is set to the truth on a sighting and moved since then by what a jump or a mission is expected to do (FleetSim.ScourgeKnown), so if the Scourge is out of sight and has closed faster than expected, the board doesn't give it away.

  • A run's pacing (RunPacing, set by the host when a mission loads: HordeSystem.Pacing, and the Director's numbers) comes from the fleet (FleetSim.Pacing). The closer the Scourge, the sooner the Choir shows up:

    • While the Scourge is four or more missions behind, there's no Choir.
    • At three, it comes once the hack starts.
    • At two, from a deck before that.
    • Nearer, from earlier still.
    • Once it has caught the fleet, it's aboard from the start.

    Past coming from the start, the first horde comes 10 s sooner per step (down to 20 s) and the rest more often (down to every 45 s), and every horde is 8% bigger per step (up to half again). The soldiers' Director runs whatever the Scourge does, and every mission of the run makes it 12% quicker (up to twice as quick; twice that on Veteran): shorter quiet before a patrol, between patrols, and in its breathers (DirectorTuning.Escalated). A fight outside a run (tests, tools, the hand-built levels) uses RunPacing.Always: hordes as described above.

  • Three-way fights. The horde goes for anyone who isn't Choir: whoever is nearest, players or soldiers. The field leads it to the players and to soldiers in a fight (alerted, engaging or hunting: the noise draws it), with a soldier counted as 10 m further away than a player (SoldierCost). So a horde called on the squad falls on any squad of soldiers it meets on the way, and soldiers fighting elsewhere draw some of it off. Soldiers see, shout (BarkKind.Choir) and shoot the horde like any enemy, backing off as it comes (a little slower than it runs).

  • Choir Lancers (design doc: the Choir's elite squads). These are soldiers on the Choir's side: the soldier's brain, body and weapon, half again a soldier's health, no loot and no barks (barks are soldiers' words). They're drawn pale, with a white light for a face (with their model, lancer.gltf: bone-pale plate, violet, a crested helm). HordeSystem.CallLancers sends a squad out of sight like a horde, after the nearest player (SoldierSpawner.SpawnLancer, with a squad number of their own). They come with every hack wave from the second one on and at the extraction (two, plus one for every two players), and from the F3 overlay. The horde leaves them alone; soldiers and players fight them.

  • The Choir's bigger bodies (ChoirSystem, ChoirUnit, design doc "Factions"). These are characters like soldiers (controller, body, health, the Choir's side; sizes and health per kind in ChoirBodies) with a simple brain of their own instead of a soldier's (ChoirBrain). Each goes for the nearest non-Choir target, along a path over the grid (two searches per tick at most) or straight at them once within 9 m and in the open, and attacks after a wind-up that warns you (rooted, posed, a sound).

    • Strippers (450 health, 2.25 m tall, 4.3 m/s) close in and strike everyone in front of them within 2.1 m for 32, and every prop there for 220, so cover, crates and breakable walls go in a blow or two. When a breakable prop is between a Stripper and its target (it checks every 0.3 s), it goes to it and strikes it. The mission's locked doors can't be broken.
    • Spitters (110 health, quick) keep 9 to 22 m away with a clear line, sidestepping, and lob a glob of acid (AcidGlob: thrown on an arc to land where the target is heading). It burns whoever it hits squarely for 12 and leaves a pool 2.2 m across for 6 s (AcidPool) that burns any non-Choir standing in it for 5 every half second, so it's ground you need to move off.

    ChoirSystem is a late system (after the horde). It writes each body's command for the next tick, and its blows and acid are hits logged after this tick's shots have cleared the log. A horde brings a Stripper for every 40 called and a Spitter for every 30, with at most three and four around at once (HordeTuning), and you can send one at a time from the F3 overlay. They're drawn as stand-in boxes like the soldiers, with the Choir's pale plates over dark sinew and its white light (CharacterModels.AddChoir): a hunched Stripper whose bladed arms rise in its wind-up, and a gaunt Spitter rearing back with a glowing sac. Globs are bright blobs and pools are glowing discs.

  • Cost. So did the "not characters" bet pay off? 300 swarmlings with four players take under half a millisecond per tick for the whole world in the AI tests (HordeTests), allocation-free and deterministic. About 0.15 ms of a station's tick is the horde's.

  • Replication (SwarmView on the host, one per client; SwarmReplica on the client; protocol version 13). The snapshot ends with the swarmlings within 70 m of the client's player:

    • a table of their IDs, each in a slot it keeps (cheap to resend: it only changes as they arrive, leave and die);
    • a bit each for the dead;
    • updates for some of them, about 8 bytes each: position, to 3 cm relative to the client's feet; velocity to a quarter meter per second; facing to 1/64 of a turn; its action; and what it's clinging to, three bits (since protocol version 20).

    How many updates fit is set by the client's budget (SwarmView.Fit): whatever the rest of the fight left of 360 bytes from one snapshot to the next last time (64 kbps is 400), from 2 to 16 per snapshot. The horde takes what's left.

    Between updates the client draws each swarmling along a curve through its last few updates (Hermite, along their velocities), and on along its velocity for up to half a second past the newest (SwarmTrack). An update that puts it somewhere else is blended in over 6 ticks from the moment it arrives, timed from ticks alone, so the host can keep the same tracks and know exactly where the client is drawing every swarmling. It updates the ones drawn furthest from where they really are first (weighted by how close they are to the client: centimeters at its feet, half a meter 60 m away), sends any that start a lunge within 25 m right away, and sends newcomers right away.

    How well does it hold up? In the soak test (four players at 150 ms with 2% loss, firing into 300 around them, with the Strippers and Spitters the horde brings and the station's soldiers fighting both), that's about 63 kbps per client in total, with the horde down to its fewest updates. Within 15 m, a swarmling is drawn about 55 cm from where it is (median; 7.5 m at the 95th percentile). Without the soldiers, it's about 26 cm (1.4 m). The host knows where the client drew each one to within a centimeter (95th percentile; more only for a lost snapshot), and that's what the client's shots are tested against, so it hits what it saw however far that was from the truth.

    One caveat: those numbers are station 3's from before the procgen revamp's shaped rooms. The soak now fights on stations 3 to 10 and judges the budget and the median on their average, which has been 64.8 kbps and 1.16 m since then, over the bounds (64 kbps, 1 m), until the networking work in the roadmap's Phase 4, Step 3 is done.

  • Drawing: instanced, animated on the GPU. The swarmling is a model made and animated in Blender (content/models/swarmling.gltf, docs/blender/creatures.md): an armature, a skinned body, a cheaper body for far away (_LOD1) and six actions (Run, Idle, WindUp, Bite, Stagger, Death). AnimatedModel (Content) bakes it when it's loaded (on the loading thread). Every action is sampled at 30 frames per second and skinned on the CPU once, giving the position of each vertex on each frame, with its face's normal packed into the fourth float (vertex animation, the way textures would hold it, but in a storage buffer: about 2 MB). The renderer uploads it once (SetSwarmModel).

    Each frame the client (SwarmAnimator) sends one 64-byte SwarmInstance per swarmling: where it stands and faces, two poses (two frames each and how far between them), how far it has faded from one to the other, a hit flash, its glow and how far it has sunk. What it plays follows Swarmling.Action (Run at the speed it covers ground, a loop every 1.1 m; Idle when standing; WindUp; Bite when a wind-up ends; Stagger; Death by how long it's been dead), cross-fading over 0.12 s.

    The near ones (22 m) are one instanced draw of the full body, and the rest (out to 90 m) are one draw of the far body. They cast shadows with the far body. 300 swarmlings are two draws and 19 KB per frame, where the box stand-ins were 120,000 vertices built on the CPU. Huge win! The dead go dark and sink. They burst (ImpactEffects.AddBurst: chitin shards, glowing motes, a spray and a pool of dark violet ichor), and hits on them bleed ichor. Sounds: a shriek at each wind-up, a squeal at each death, the bite, and the chitter of the crowd near you, louder the more of them there are.

Renderer

The renderer uses Vulkan 1.3 core features only, so any 1.3 driver works without extensions beyond the swapchain (plus, where the driver has them, the ray tracing extensions, see Ray tracing below, and some presentation extras, see Sleep, wake and a lost GPU below). Why so strict? There are no optional extensions to probe, and dynamic rendering and sync2 keep the renderer short. Here's what that gets us:

  • Dynamic rendering: no render pass or framebuffer objects. Pipelines name their attachment formats instead.
  • synchronization2: vkCmdPipelineBarrier2 and vkQueueSubmit2.
  • Two frames in flight, each with its own command pool, command buffer, image-acquired semaphore, fence, and overlay and effect vertex buffers. Present-wait semaphores are per swapchain image, which avoids a reuse hazard the validation layer reports.
  • VMA (Brutal.Vulkan.Vma) allocates every buffer and image. Static meshes live in device-local memory, filled once through a staging buffer. Per-frame data lives in mapped, host-visible memory.
  • Rebuilds. The swapchain and the frame's images (FrameTargets) are rebuilt when the swapchain is out of date or suboptimal, or the framebuffer size changes, and together with the pipelines when the antialiasing changes. Everything new is created before anything old is destroyed, so a rebuild that fails leaves the old one in place. Frames are skipped while the window is minimized, and while the surface has no area (Swapchain.ChooseExtent). Windows reports that for a window that isn't minimized while its display is asleep or unplugged, and a swapchain can't be that size.
  • Present mode: FIFO with vsync; mailbox, then immediate, without.
  • Window modes. The window can be windowed, exclusive full screen (the display's own resolution and refresh rate) or borderless (an undecorated window covering the display), on whichever display holds most of it (GameWindow.SetMode, from the Display setting or F11). A change resizes the framebuffer, so the swapchain is rebuilt like for any resize. Going back to a window restores its old position.
  • Sleep, wake and a lost GPU. The rule here is simple: no wait in a frame is unbounded.
    • The frame's fence and the next image are waited for in 100 ms slices, with the window's events pumped in between. Why? A display that's asleep can hold on to a FIFO swapchain's images indefinitely, and a window that stops responding holds up Windows' own broadcasts on sleep and wake. Past RendererOptions.FrameWaitLimit (1 s) the frame is skipped and VulkanRenderer.PresentationPaused reports it, and the game and Studio then sleep between frames.
    • Rebuilds wait for the frames in flight (the only GPU work outstanding, since every other submit waits for itself) rather than for the device to go idle. One-off GPU work waits at most GpuWait.HangLimit.
    • A frame's fence is reset right before the submit that signals it, so a frame abandoned part way through never leaves a fence that nothing will signal.
    • When the driver reports the device or surface lost (Windows resets a driver that stops responding, often on waking), or one-off work hangs past its limit, VulkanRenderer destroys everything it created with the device and creates it again on a new one (CreateDeviceObjects, Recover). Then it re-uploads everything it was given: the world mesh, the horde's model, the skinned meshes and the UI textures. TextureStore keeps the textures' pixels and names them by its own IDs, so ImGui's and the icons' texture IDs stay valid. A frame that loses the device is skipped. Losing it more than three times in a minute is treated as fatal, with an error. --lose-device <frame> simulates a loss in a smoke run.
    • Where the driver has VK_EXT_swapchain_maintenance1, presents are fenced, so a swapchain's semaphores are only destroyed once presentation is done with them (without it, they're destroyed after the queue goes idle, as usual). Where it has VK_EXT_full_screen_exclusive (Windows), a borderless window forbids the driver from taking over the display, and full screen allows it.
  • Textures (TextureStore, the UI's textures: the logo, ImGui's font atlas, icons) are replaced and destroyed without waiting for the GPU. The old image is retired (RetireQueue) and released once every frame submitted before that has finished.
  • Pacing (FrameLimiter, Core): frames are capped at 180 per second, and at 30 while the window is in the background (unfocused). Waiting sleeps for all but the last 1.5 ms and only spins for that part, so a capped frame uses no CPU while it waits. On Windows the sleep is a high-resolution waitable timer (PreciseSleep), because a plain sleep there rounds up to the 15.6 ms system tick.
  • Validation. --validation enables VK_LAYER_KHRONOS_validation when it's installed. ValidationMonitor logs its messages and counts errors, which is what fails the GPU smoke test.

Each frame is drawn in high dynamic range. Here's the flow:

  1. The scene is drawn in linear light into a high-range image (FrameTargets.HdrFormat, RGBA16F), multisampled (RendererOptions.Antialiasing, VulkanRenderer.Antialiasing: 1, 2 or 4 samples, 4 by default, set by the Antialiasing setting or --antialiasing) and resolved at the end of the scene.
  2. Next to it is a second target for the indirect light (FrameTargets.Indirect: what the world's fill, its ambient and room bounce lit; also resolved). Its depth is kept, not resolved, because the ambient occlusion reads it (the first sample when multisampled).
  3. Then full-screen passes compute its ambient occlusion, add bloom, and bring it into the display's range in the sRGB swapchain, where the overlay and the UI are drawn last.

Ray tracing (RayScene, RayTracingSupport: the Ray tracing setting, --ray-tracing off|on, on by default where the GPU supports it; docs/plans/ray-tracing.md). Where the GPU has VK_KHR_acceleration_structure, VK_KHR_ray_query and buffer device addresses (RTX 20 and later, RX 6000 and later, Arc, and Mesa's lavapipe from 25, which is the driver CI and the tests use), the device is created with them whether or not rays are on, so changing the setting doesn't need a restart. Rays are cast as ray queries from the existing compute and fragment shaders, with no ray-tracing pipelines.

  • The level's bottom-level structure is built when it loads (seed 7: 77k triangles, under 2 s on lavapipe). Its drawn parts are merged into as few opaque geometries as the mesh's order allows. Each part that can disappear (a broken prop, a shot-out light's glass, an opened door, a blown hull plate: RayParts.CanGo in the client) gets its own non-opaque geometry, so a ray checks a per-frame flag for whether it's there (FrameGeometry.Gone: a part tint alpha of -1). A room that's out of sight is -2, Unseen, which rays still hit, because things out of sight still cast shadows into what's visible (RayGeometry, traceNearest in ray_common.slang).
  • Each frame, first (the Ray tracing pass), a compute pass poses the skinned bodies (ray_skin.slang, skinning them the same way skinned.slang does) and builds them into two bottom levels, one of them just the viewer's own body. The moving world's triangles go into a third. A top level goes over them, plus one instance per horde body of the horde's far mesh in its first frame, placed the way swarm.slang places it (RayInstances: an instance's kind and number in its custom index, one mask per kind).
  • The debug view (--ray-debug, or the F3 overlay's Ray debug view) draws what a ray from the eye through each pixel hits instead of the scene (ray_debug.slang): the level grey-blue, the moving world yellow, bodies orange, the viewer's own body green, the horde red, without bloom or occlusion. Rays see every room, while the scene only draws the rooms in sight of the player's room, so a camera moved away from the player (--camera) can see rooms in the debug view that the scene leaves out.

Shadows by rays (RayLighting, the Ray shadows pass, on wherever rays are traced). This runs after the atmosphere and before the scene, at half the screen's resolution.

  • A pre-pass draws what the scene draws (the level, the moving world, the bodies, the viewer's own body, the horde) as each pixel's surface, normal and distance from the eye (negative on a body or the horde; ray_prepass.slang and ray_prepass_body.slang with the scene's own vertex shaders).
  • Then a compute pass works out, for each pixel, the light its tile's lights give it before any shadow, and traces two rays (one at Medium and Low ray tracing quality, below). Each ray goes to one of those lights, picked by how much light it gives, at a random point on it (anywhere on a rect light's glass, so its shadows are soft). The share of the light that arrives is the picked light's share of the total where the ray is clear (ray_shadow_trace.slang, the ratio estimator of Heitz et al. 2018: only the shadow is noisy, never the light).
  • A body's rays ignore bodies and the horde (they'd shadow the body itself, and the horde's structure isn't posed). A flashlight's and a flash's rays ignore bodies (they start in someone's hands). A ray stops 4 cm short of a rect light's glass and 15 cm short of a point light.
  • The rays are guided (docs/plans/ray-tracing.md phase 6, like MegaLights' visible light list). Each 8 by 8 block of shadow pixels, a compute group, records the lights its clear rays reached by their keys (PointLight.Key, folded into 128 bits). Next frame, a pixel's rays go to the lights its block found clear 80% of the time, with each ray's light weighted by its chance under both picks so the estimate stays unbiased.
  • That share is carried over frames where the same surface was there last frame (ray_shadow_temporal.slang: found with last frame's view-projection, counted as the same if it was as far from the eye then and facing the same way; the new estimate's weight is one over the number of frames held, never under 5%, which is about a third of a second at 60 fps). The accumulated history is clamped to within two standard deviations of this frame's estimates around the pixel (luma and chroma, on similar surfaces in the 3 by 3: a neighborhood clamp, docs/plans/ray-tracing.md phase 7), so a moving body's shadow shows up instead of averaging away. The bounce isn't clamped. Then it's smoothed along surfaces by two passes of an edge-avoiding à-trous filter (ray_shadow_filter.slang).
  • The world shaders, given flag 16, take their tile's lights without their shadow maps and multiply by that share (rayShadowRatio in scene_lights.slang: the four half-resolution pixels around each pixel, weighted by how similar their surfaces are; set 0, bindings 9 and 10, in the general layout, with a placeholder texel where rays aren't traced). The gun in hand keeps its own lights.
  • Every light then reaches through its room's doorways (SceneLights.RaysTraced: its box covers the room and its neighbors, and the lights of rooms next to the visible ones are kept too), since rays keep it off whatever walls hide. At a point in another room, a light only counts where the line from the point to it passes through a doorway between the two rooms (throughDoorway: each room's doorways, from the layout's, RoomLighting.Doorways, up to 12 per room at set 0 binding 15, each a box across its wall grown by the light's size plus 30 cm; if none are recorded between the two rooms, or there are more than fit, the light is kept). This applies in the world shaders, the shadow rays and the rays' hits alike, so the ratio's lights are the ones shaded, and a light no ray could reach costs neither shading nor a ray.
  • The four half-resolution pixels each result is read from, and their weights, are computed once per pixel for all of them (rayTaps). The atmosphere still lights its air with the lights' shadow maps, with each widened light only in its own room (its room is in its box's w, airLight).
  • --ray-shadows, or the F3 overlay's Ray shadow view, draws the share alone: grey where a surface's light arrives, black where it's shadowed.

Without rays, a light lit its whole room through anything inside it. The airlock's chamber, walled to the ceiling, now has its own panel.

Bounce by rays (RayLighting, the Ray bounce pass, on wherever rays are traced; docs/plans/ray-tracing.md phase 5). From the same pre-pass's surfaces, at half resolution, each pixel casts one ray off its surface in a cosine-weighted direction, up to 30 m (15 m at Low quality; ray_indirect_trace.slang). At Medium and Low only half the pixels do this each frame, in a checkerboard that alternates each frame. The temporal pass keeps a skipped pixel's history as it was, or starts it from its four neighbors when it has none.

What the ray hits is lit the way the world shaders would light it:

  • the level's color and material there, read from the level's own vertex and index buffers (set 2) using the hit's geometry and triangle (each geometry's first index is RayGeometry's run), or a neutral grey for anything else;
  • times the lights there. That's its room's lights: each frame LightStore.WriteRoomLights lists, for each room, the frame's lights whose reach touches its box, nearest first, up to 31 (set 0 binding 12), or every light of the frame for a point in no room. One of them is picked by what it gives (a weighted reservoir), with a shadow ray to it; outdoors the sun is among them;
  • plus the sky's fill and that room's bounce, which stands in for the bounces after the first.

A ray that hits nothing takes the room's bounce where it ended up (so a short reach doesn't darken a big hall). One that hits the back of a face brings nothing back, and none brings back more than a luminance of 4 (a lone ray under a light would leave a speck). Averaged over the hemisphere, that's the light a white surface receives from all around, the way the world shaders count light.

It's carried over frames by the shadows' temporal pass (never under 3% new) and smoothed by four passes of their filter (steps 1 to 8), each result with its own set 1 over the shared guide (RayLighting's chains). The world shaders, given flag 64, use it instead of the room's bounce (rayBounce, set 0 binding 11; the reflection captures keep the room's bounce). --ray-bounce, or the F3 overlay's Ray bounce view, draws it alone. It's darker than the room's bounce on average (the room's bounce is a tuned estimate, RoomBounce.Gain) and varies across a room: lit where light comes through a doorway, dark under a table.

Occlusion by rays (RayLighting, the Ray occlusion pass, wherever rays are traced and ambient occlusion is on; docs/plans/ray-tracing.md phase 8). From the same pre-pass's surfaces, at half resolution, each pixel casts two cosine-weighted rays out to 1.5 m (ray_occlusion_trace.slang), each giving how far it got divided by how far it could go. The average is how open the surface is. It's carried over frames and smoothed the same way as the shadows (a third chain, clamped like theirs). The world shaders, given flag 256, apply it (rayOpenness, set 0 binding 13) to the sky's fill and to what a surface reflects (by the reflection's light where it reflects a cube map or the sky, otherwise by the fill). It isn't applied to the bounce by rays, whose own rays already account for occlusion. GTAO and the tonemap's occlusion term are skipped in that case. --ray-occlusion, or the F3 overlay's Ray occlusion view, draws it alone.

Reflections by rays (RayLighting, the Ray reflections pass, wherever rays are traced; docs/plans/ray-tracing.md phase 9). The pre-pass writes each pixel's roughness next to its surface (the look, set 1 binding 12). On surfaces smoother than 0.4 (RayLighting.TracedRoughness: glass and bare metal, not painted metal or plastic), each pixel casts one ray along the highlight's lobe (a GGX half vector for the roughness, with the view mirrored about it), up to 40 m (ray_reflection_trace.slang).

What it hits is lit the same way as the bounce's hits (ray_hits.slang: the level's color and material, the room's lights with one traced to, the sky's fill and the room's bounce), and its glow is added too, so a panel's glass shows up in a glossy floor. A ray that hits nothing takes the room's cube map along its direction, or the sky. It's carried over frames quickly (at least 20% new), clamped and smoothed twice. The world shaders use it (flag 1024, rayReflection, set 0 binding 14) instead of the cube map: fully up to a roughness of 0.24, and not at all past 0.4. Ambient occlusion only darkens the cube map's share. --ray-reflections, or the F3 overlay's Ray reflection view, draws it alone.

Ray tracing quality (RayTracingQuality, the video settings' Ray tracing quality under Ray tracing, or --ray-quality low|medium|high for one run; docs/plans/ray-tracing.md phase 11). It sets everything that affects the rays' cost together (RayQualitySettings.For):

  • High is two shadow rays per pixel and the bounce on every pixel every frame, out to 30 m.
  • Medium, the default, is one shadow ray and the bounce on half the pixels per frame.
  • Low also limits the bounce to 15 m with two filter passes, and uses one occlusion ray.

These reach the shaders in the rays' frame constants (quality, and the shadow rays in tuning). A change resets the rays' history.

Path tracing and DLSS (RenderPath, PathTracer, Streamline; the video settings' Renderer, or --renderer hybrid|path-traced; docs/plans/path-tracing.md). The Renderer setting chooses how the scene is lit:

  • Hybrid is everything above, raster with rays for shadows, bounce, occlusion and reflections.
  • Path traced is an RTXPT-style path tracer, on any GPU that traces rays.

The path tracer shares the frame's start (ray structures, light grid, shadow atlas, atmosphere) and end (bloom, tonemap, HUD) with the hybrid path and replaces what is between:

  1. A raster G-buffer drawn by the scene's own vertex shaders (path_gbuffer*.slang: position, normal and roughness, material, albedo, emission, motion in pixels).
  2. path_trace.slang traces each pixel's paths (1 to 8 bounces, 1 to 4 a pixel): next-event estimation with a shadow ray to a light picked by resampled importance from the pixel's light-grid cell, its room's panels and the sun, a diffuse or GGX (visible normals) bounce, Russian roulette after the second, a firefly clamp on indirect light, and paths that run out of bounces end in their room's bounce light, which is the engine's radiance cache (the Radiance cache setting; off is unbiased and darker). It writes diffuse and specular light demodulated by their albedos, the guides DLSS Ray Reconstruction needs, and the specular hit distance.
  3. Then one of two denoisers: DLSS Ray Reconstruction (NVIDIA RTX, through Streamline), or the built-in one (path_denoise_temporal.slang and path_denoise_filter.slang: reprojected history with depth and normal tests and a moments-based variance, then four à-trous passes guided by depth, normal and luminance, specular filtered for two).
  4. path_compose.slang puts light back on its albedo, adds emission and the air in front, and the transparent effects (drawn into their own target, which Ray Reconstruction also takes as its transparency layer).

DLSS renders at its mode's share of the display (Quality 67%, Balanced 58%, Performance 50%, Ultra Performance 33%, DLAA 100%), with a Halton(2, 3) jitter of at least 32 phases, and Super Resolution (or Ray Reconstruction) brings it up to the display into the frame's color. Everything in the Path tracing settings applies on the next frame; the path tracer's images are made again when the render size changes. Only loading Streamline is fixed at start (it has to be in place before the Vulkan instance is made), so the menu's "Load NVIDIA DLSS at start" asks for a restart.

Streamline (Streamline/, NVIDIA Streamline 2.14.16 in vendor/streamline, Windows only). StreamlineSession loads sl.interposer.dll only once StreamlineSignature has checked that it is signed by NVIDIA (Authenticode, then the Streamline key inside it, as the SDK's sl_security.h does), and starts with manual hooking: the renderer asks for the features' instance and device extensions and Vulkan 1.2 and 1.3 features (VulkanFeatureNames), hands its instance, device and queue to slSetVulkanInfo, and presents through the interposer's vkQueuePresentKHR.

Each frame: a frame token, the camera's constants (StreamlineCamera: view to clip and back, clip to previous clip, the jitter, motion vectors in pixels scaled to the render size, reverse depth), the mode's options, the tagged images (StreamlineImage) and slEvaluateFeature on the frame's command buffer. The structures are laid out as the SDK's headers lay them (StreamlineLayoutTests checks every offset against tools/streamline/layout.cpp). Where Streamline cannot run (not Windows, not NVIDIA, an old driver, an unsigned DLL, --no-streamline), DlssSupport says why and the menu shows it; the path tracer then renders at full size with its own denoiser.

What the GPU spends (GpuTimer). Timestamps are written between the ray structures, the shadows, the reflections' captures, the atmosphere's grid, the shadows by rays, the bounce by rays, the scene (the G-buffer, when path traced), the path tracing, its denoising, DLSS, the ambient occlusion, the bloom, and the tonemap with the HUD. They're read back when a frame's slot comes round again and smoothed into VulkanRenderer.GpuMilliseconds, which is shown in the F3 overlay and printed by smoke runs (GPU: Ray tracing ... ms, Shadows ... ms, ..., Ray shadows ... ms, Reflections ..., Atmosphere ..., Scene ..., Ambient occlusion ..., Bloom ..., Tonemap and HUD ...). docs/plans/lighting.md asks each of its phases to state its cost in these terms.

The client hands each frame a FrameGeometry: overlay triangles, effect triangles, one tint per world part, and the frame's point lights. The world is drawn in runs of consecutive parts with the same tint, pushed at offset 112 of the push constants (128 bytes in total, the most every device allows), so an untinted world stays a single draw and a flashing target adds two. Effects are built on the CPU by EffectBuilder (billboards facing the eye, beams rotated to face it, decals lifted 1 cm off their surface and rotated about it). Each quad carries its corners (-1 to 1) and an EffectShape, which the fragment shader cuts out, so round particles and splats don't need textures.

Culling by room (RoomVisibility, stations only). A station is one mesh, drawn by part runs, and it used to be drawn whole: looking towards most of the deck cost every room behind the walls, shaded with its lights. Ouch. So what can we skip? Rooms only see each other through doorways, so each frame the client walks from the camera's room through every doorway that's on screen, narrowing the part of the screen each room is seen through (portals), and hides the parts of rooms it doesn't reach (with a negative tint alpha, the same as broken props). Rooms standing on a visible room are drawn too, since their floors are its ceiling. Only visible rooms' lights are sent.

It's conservative: a doorway you're standing in shows everything its room shows, locked doors count as open, and a camera in no room (the death camera under the floor) draws everything. In seed 2026's insertion bay, the four directions drew 446 to 2,930 of the level's 57,534 triangles and 1 to 6 of 136 rooms, with 4 to 19 lights. Renders with and without culling are identical. A test casts rays through a grid of screen points from every fifth room in eight directions on three seeds, and checks that whatever they hit is drawn. F3 shows rooms, triangles and lights drawn.

Lighting. Outdoors (a hand-built level) the world is lit by a warm sun and a cool sky fill, as before. Inside a station there's no sun, only the faintest fill (0.018: a room with its lights out is black except for shapes right in front of you, which is a flashlight's job) and a black fog (0.028 per meter). The light comes from the level's fixtures:

  • Point lights. PointLights (position, color, radius and a box) only light what's inside their box, the room, so a light never shines through a wall. Through an opening wider than a door (an open hall's end), the box carries on into the slab of the next room straight beyond it, as wide as the opening (LightReach).

  • Rect lights (PointLight.Rect, LevelLight.HalfU/HalfV). A panel's glass, a hall's floor channel and each wall's floor strip are rectangles shining on one side (the cross product of their half axes), as bright as their glass glows. A panel is twice that (FixtureGlow.LightGain), so rooms keep their light without the glass blooming them away; strips and channels light as they glow (FixtureGlow.LightFor). A surface takes a rectangle's diffuse light by Lambert's formula for the polygon clipped at its horizon (rectIrradiance in scene_lights.slang, each edge by Heitz's fitted angle over its sine; RectLights is the same in C#, tested against the light summed over the glass), or as a point past four half diagonals (RectLights.FarFactor). Its highlight comes from Karis' representative point (where the reflected view meets the glass, with the lobe widened by the glass's narrower half over its distance). Each fades to nothing at its radius from its nearest point, and it's binned by its glass plus its radius, only in front of it.

  • Other lights. Flashes, blasts and alarm beacons are point lights that fade with a gentle inverse square, and flashlights are spotlights. Shadow maps are only for point lights and lights shining down (a hall's floor glow shines up, so what it lights is above it).

  • Bounce (RoomBounce) is the fill. Each frame every live light's strength goes into its room (the panels at their current brightness, flickering or fading, the alarms, the flashes, and a share of each flashlight's: SceneLights.Build). Each room's bounce is that light off its floor (onto faces turned down) and off its walls and ceiling (onto faces turned up), using their colors (RoomSurfaces, averaged from the room's parts) and spread over their area, plus a share of each neighbor's bounce through each doorway, by the opening's area (RoomBounce.OpeningArea). It eases to a change over a fifth of a second and jumps up at once for a flash. The shaders find a point's room in a grid of the layout's cells (RoomLighting: set 0, bindings 5 and 6, with the point moved 10 cm out of its face first) and take that room's bounce. A point in no room (a hand-built level) gets none, only the sky's fill. So a red alarm room is red in its shadows, and a room whose lights are shot out goes dark except for what comes through its doorways.

  • Aged panels. Panels are dimmed (0.5) and aged: each room's light drifts from its role's color towards a sickly fluorescent green or a dirty sodium yellow. 14 in 100 are dead (never a room's first) and 10 flicker.

  • Glow. Color channels above 1 glow: the excess is added unlit, which is how panels, a grenade's blinking light and rarity stripes shine.

  • A light and its fixture are one (FixtureGlow). A panel's glass is its light's color, as bright as the light's strength over the glass (LevelLight.Glow, set when the level is made), so a blue light has a blue panel. A failing panel's glass dims with its light (a part tint), and one that's shot out throws a spark and its light fades over 0.3 s. Alarm lamps, a gun light's lens (on along with its flashlight: WeaponModel.Lamps, Flashlights), a muzzle flash's flame and a hall's floor channels glow the same way, capped (FixtureGlow.MaxGlow) so a small lens doesn't blind you. Bright light rolls off above 0.8 instead of clipping.

  • Gunfire. Every gunshot and every blast adds a brief light (60 ms and 0.45 s), limited to the room it happens in.

  • The light grid. The client (SceneLights) sends the nearest lights whose box is in view, at most 128, flickering the failing ones. The renderer bins them into a light grid each frame on the GPU (LightGrid, light_grid.slang, the Light grid pass, first in the frame; clustered shading). The screen is split into 32 × 18 tiles, each split by distance from the eye into 24 slices (the first up to 1 m, then evenly in log distance up to 100 m, the last one beyond). One thread per cell finds the cell's bounds in the world and takes each light whose reach (its box, cut to its radius, and for a rect light to its lit side) overlaps them and comes within its radius, at most 31 per cell, nearest first (LightGrid.Touches makes the same decision on the CPU, for the tests).

    Each pixel only tests its cell's lights, found from its distance and screen position (lightCell in scene_lights.slang; the shadow rays and the atmosphere's cells find theirs the same way), so a nearby wall no longer tests the lights of rooms far behind it in the same part of the screen. Until phase 10 of docs/plans/ray-tracing.md the tiles were screen-only and binned on the CPU, 15 per tile and then 31 (0113's black squares were tiles crowded by lights from next door).

  • The view model, which has its own projection, reads the nearest eight lights directly. It keeps a little of the sun outdoors (35%) and a trace of light inside (2%, so in the dark it's a dark shape), but not a flashlight starting within a meter of it (that's its own).

Shootable lights and flashlights. A broken panel (see Station dressing) is hidden like any broken prop, and SceneLights leaves out a light whose panel is hidden while its room is visible. A PointLight can be a spotlight: a direction and the cosine of its cone's half angle (80 bytes per light), brightest along its axis, fading to nothing over the cone's outer half, falling off more gently than a bulb (a beam carries), and boxed to the cone's bounds.

Flashlights are the first use. CommandButtons.Flashlight (T) switches MovementState.Flashlight, which is predicted like any movement state and replicated as a bit of every character's flags, so everyone sees everyone's flashlight. The client adds a spotlight for each character with theirs on (the viewer's just right of and below the eye, so its shadows show a little; everyone else's from the gun, half a meter ahead of the body), with the viewer's first, ahead of every other light.

  • The first three cast shadows. Each gets a perspective map along the beam, redrawn every frame (it moves) into a cache slot reserved for it, from the rooms it can light (its room, the rooms through its doorways, and the rooms standing on any of them, whose floors are ceilings), and it's boxed to those rooms (the walls of any other room inside that box go into its map too, so the beam stops at them). The rest are boxed to their own room, like a panel.
  • Unlike a panel, a beam sends no bounce light to faces turned away from it, or into its own shadows. Why so careful? A box can include the far side of a wall, and a beam must never show through one (it used to, faintly, which was enough to tell someone was there).
  • A beam belongs to the room its carrier is in, whether standing, crouched or down, and starts inside that room, clear of its walls, however close to one they stand. (It used to be judged from the light's own position, which put a crouched soldier's beam in the story below, or in no room at all on the ground floor, boxed to nothing and shining through every wall.)
  • Soldiers switch theirs on in the dark and while hunting or searching (SoldierAiSystem.Flashlight, kept on for four seconds after the last reason). LightMap (AI) knows each room's working lights from the level (each one a breakable panel, checked every half second for being shot out) and calls a point dark when they barely reach it.
  • The client draws the nearest beams first, and those behind walls last.

A character's weapon frame is in every snapshot, and a player's roll is too, so everyone holds their own gun as rolled (CharacterModels).

Shadows. The nearest 16 panels in view (not flashes) cast shadows (ShadowAtlas: one 2048² depth image in 512² tiles, filled every frame before the main pass). A panel only shines downwards, so each map is a single view straight down from the light, 140° across, out to its radius, with no cube maps. It draws the light's own room (its parts, minus broken ones; GeneratedLevel.PartRooms) and every character (and the horde, with its far body, swarm_shadow.slang), so props, catwalks and soldiers all cast shadows. A room is a box and a light only lights its inside, so nothing outside the room could shadow it.

The pipeline is depth-only, both sides, with slope-scaled bias. The world shader pushes the point 4 cm off its surface and takes four hardware-filtered comparison samples half a texel apart, so edges are soft. A shadow removes the light's direct part, and the room's bounce stays. Points the map doesn't see (above the light, beside the panel) count as lit.

Cached. The level part of each map is drawn once into a second atlas, the cache (4096×2048, 32 slots), kept per light (least recently used is evicted) and only redrawn when its room changes (a hash of which of its parts are hidden: a prop broken, a door opened), at most six per frame, so walking into a room full of lights causes no spike (a light still waiting goes unshadowed for a frame or two). Each frame the maps in use are copied from the cache into the atlas, and only lights with a character within reach of their room draw the characters over theirs, so a quiet station draws no shadow geometry at all. Shadows can be turned off in the Video settings (GameSettings.Shadows) for slower cards.

Atmosphere (FroxelGrid, Atmosphere: the Atmosphere setting, --atmosphere off|low|high, High by default). The level's haze (FrameParameters.FogDensity: 0.028 per meter in a station, with the clear color as its color, black there) is computed in a froxel grid of the view every frame, before the scene, by two compute passes that read the lights' set (set 0, which compute shaders can see too).

  • Each cell's density is the haze, times its room's factor (RoomLighting.Haze, 0.7 to 1.6 from a hash of the room's position, so some rooms are dustier), plus smoke. Smoke is FogVolumes: what a blast leaves (a cloud that rises, swells and thins over eight seconds) and a breach's smoke while it pours (CombatFeedback.BuildFogVolumes; at most 32).
  • The light it scatters towards the eye is the haze's own color, the room's bounce, and every light of its screen tile (the same tiles the world shader uses) through its shadow map (airLight in scene_lights.slang: like arriving gives a surface, but without facing). It's weighted by Henyey-Greenstein's phase function with g = 0.7, so the air glows when you look towards a light or along a beam, and hardly at all across one. Dust scatters 6% of the light that falls on it, so a lit corridor stays dark rather than milky. Smoke scatters 50%, so a beam through it shows.
  • The second pass follows each column from the eye, gathering what each slice scatters as it dims, and writes what's been gathered and what passes through to each cell's far side. Every world draw and effect (except the gun in hand) reads it at its screen position and distance (set 0, binding 8; atmosphere_common.slang), adding the haze evenly past 60 m.
  • Off, the grid is one cell that's never computed, and draws apply the haze evenly (the distance fog the game always had). Reflection captures always do.

On lavapipe the grid costs about 70 ms on High and 11 ms on Low, against a 200 ms scene. CI's three GPU smoke runs take about a fifth longer.

Reflections (ReflectionProbes, RoomReflections). Each room has a cube map, 64² per face in high range, with five mips prefiltered by the GGX lobe from roughness 0 (a mirror, the capture itself) to 1, all in one cube-map array (set 0, binding 7; the device's imageCubeArray feature).

  • A room is captured from about eye height in the middle of its box (RoomLighting.CapturePoint), with the world shader. It's lit by its own steady lights and those of the rooms through its doorways (SceneLights.CaptureLights: panels still standing, a failing one at its average, and the beacons on alert, written into the frame's light buffer after its own and read directly, without shadows). It draws its parts and theirs as they stand (nothing broken), and doesn't reflect a cube map itself. Characters, effects, flashlights and flashes are never in a capture.
  • The world shaders find a point's room in the grid, follow the reflected view from the point to the room's box (RoomLighting.Boxes: inside the walls, floor to ceiling), and read the room's cube in the direction of where it hits the box, as seen from the capture point (box projection, eased towards the plain direction as the surface gets rougher), at the mip for its roughness. Outdoors (no rooms) a surface reflects a sky gradient, and in a room that isn't captured yet it reflects the light falling on it, as before.
  • The client (RoomReflections, per level) immediately requests every room the renderer doesn't have captured (RoomLighting.IsCaptured, which the renderer tracks: every room on the level's first frame, and all of them again after the renderer has dropped them for other rooms, as when Studio changes views; ReflectionCapture.Now does all of it in that frame, about two seconds on lavapipe for 134 rooms). After that it requests each room whose light changed (a panel in it or visible from it shot out or repaired, SceneLights.RoomVersion; the alert's stage where it or a neighbor has beacons), nearest to the eye first.
  • The renderer handles those in turn, one face per frame, then fades the room's cube to the new one over eight frames (the prefilter blends into what the mips held), so play never hitches and a shot-out panel's reflection goes dark in about a quarter of a second.
  • Studio's Look room is captured whole whenever it's rebuilt. Its stages have no rooms, so they reflect the sky.

Materials (docs/blender/materials.md). Every vertex carries a surface (MeshVertex.Surface, a 32-bit word, VertexSurface): the index of its material in the library (MaterialLibrary: content/defs/materials.json, built into LastFleet.Content as MaterialLibrary.Shipped) and, for a smooth material, a smoothed normal (three signed bytes; SmoothNormals, area-weighted across faces bent less than 50 degrees, within one material).

  • Loaders resolve a glTF material's library id from its extras (lastfleet_material, Blender's custom property; GltfJson.MaterialIds, since BRUTAL's glTF API drops extras), or by its name (MaterialLibrary.Resolve: Glass, Light or anything with an emission, Paint_ slots, and everything else the first material, plastic). GltfMeshLoader, ModelParts (per corner, ModelTriangle.SurfaceA to C), SkinnedModel (SkinnedVertex.Surface, with the smoothed normal in the bind pose, skinned like the normal is) and AnimatedModel (per material, ModelMaterial.Surface) all do this.

  • Code-built geometry is painted metal unless MeshBuilder.Material says otherwise (floors and ducts are worn metal, pipes bare metal), and emissive when its color glows. Placing a model rotates its smoothed normals with it (VertexSurface.Turned).

  • The renderer writes the library each frame into a table at set 0, binding 4 (GpuMaterial, 32 bytes each, at most 64; VulkanRenderer.SetMaterials, which Studio calls as its Look mode, LookMode over a MaterialWorkspace, edits the file).

  • The world shaders light a surface with a diffuse term and a GGX highlight (Smith visibility, Schlick Fresnel) from the sun and every point light and spotlight, using the face normal for lighting and the smoothed normal for highlights. Metal has no diffuse light: its color is what it reflects.

  • What a surface reflects (weighted by Karis' fit of the split-sum integral, taken from its diffuse share) is its room's cube map along the reflected view (using the smoothed normal for a smooth material), or the sky outdoors, or the light falling on it in a room that isn't captured yet.

  • An emissive material's color is light, drawn regardless of what lights it (a color past 1 is lit as before, with its excess glowing). A material with its own color (rubber) replaces the model's color.

  • Windows onto space (docs/plans/structure-first.md, Phase 9). A material marked space (space_window; GpuMaterial.SpaceFlag, 64) is drawn as the sky along the view through it, whatever is behind it: the world shaders, the path tracer's G-buffer, and both tracers' hits (ray_hits.slang, path_trace.slang) all call spaceSky (space_sky.slang). Neat trick: nothing is seen through a window but the sky, so decks never see each other through one, and room culling needs no portal for it.

    The sky is worked out per pixel from four rows of the material table past its 64 materials (GpuSky, VulkanRenderer.SetSky): the dark of space, two layers of stars (hashed cells on the sphere), a nebula and the galaxy's dust band (value noise), and the sector's sun (a disc with a glow). Its numbers are a SkyLook: a SpaceSky from content/defs/skies.json (SkyLibrary, built into LastFleet.Content; one a sector, ForSector) turned for the fleet's system's place on the sector's map and seeded from the level (SpaceSky.At, LevelSky.For). Studio's Look mode tunes them on its Skies tab, looking out from inside a box faced with the window material.

  • Windows are props (PropKind.Window: a size, a sill, a frame color) a level rule places at anchor hull: back against a stretch of wall wholly on the hull (LevelLayout.Hull, a plan level's HullEdges from each deck's envelope; IPlacementRoom.OnHull, RulePlacer.HullStretches). StationPlacements.BuildWindow sets one into the wall: a frame lining the opening from outside the hull's skin to just proud of the wall, round a pane of glass set back in it (<frame>_Pane, solid but not drawn: the client draws its glass, WindowGlass), and returns the opening, which is cut out of the wall and the skin once the skin is on (MeshBuilder.Cut: the pieces of each drawn triangle outside the box, as a part of their own, WindowOpening_<n>; a collided part is left whole for its collision and drawn by the new part instead). The shipped rule (hull_windows) puts them in bridges, crew quarters, lounges, messes and public halls; plan rooms carry their archetype's tags for it (RoomModule.Dressed), as the solver's built rooms do.

  • The outside (a plan's level with windows; LevelAssembler.Outside). What's out there:

    • the hull's skin (HullSkin: a plate a hand's breadth out from every side of a cell facing space, story by story, in two shaded panels with a trim along each deck's floor line, roofs and keels over the envelope's top and bottom cells, LevelLayout.Envelope, and running lights along the top deck; its look is the plan's skin, HullLook);
    • the sky round everything (SkyShell: a box 6 km out, faces turned in, in the window material);
    • the fleet (FleetInView: ships of boxes in space round the level, as the template's fleet says, FleetRule);
    • and the dropship docked outside the airlock (DockedDropship, hidden by the client once it casts off).

    All are drawn only and belong to no room; all but the dropship are PartUsage.Backdrop, left out of the mesh's bounds, so the navigation grid and the rest see only what's playable. Their material, hull_plating, is flagged outside (GpuMaterial.OutsideFlag): lit by the sector's sun (space_sky.slang's rows) and a trace of starlight, not a room's fill; anything out in space (sky or hull) is seen through only the first few meters of a room's haze (VacuumAir in world_shading.slang).

Grime. Indoors (the less sun, the more grime), the world shader dirties the level based on where each point is, with no textures: walls dark and brown up from each story's floor, streaks where something ran down them, a stained band under the ceiling, blotches; floors blotched and scuffed; ceilings smudged; everything a little drabber and darker. Each material takes as much as its grime value says (glass none, floors all of it), and grime roughens what it covers. Characters and the gun are drawn clean (a push-constant flag), since fixed grime would slide across anything that moves. Panels stay clean too.

What a hit looks like is all presentation, built from each tick's HitRecords on every machine and never fed back to the simulation, with no hitstop:

  • Characters (CharacterModels): a hit flashes the model and knocks its upper body away from the shot, pivoting at the hips (backwards if the direction isn't known), out fast and back slower, further and longer the bigger the hit's share of its health. A headshot also snaps the head back, and the feet shuffle a few centimeters. A headshot kill drops the body where it stands, knees first, then slumps it forward. Any other kill topples it full length away from the shot, or 40° to either side, or towards the shooter, whichever has room (two rays each, once per death). With no room anywhere, it drops where it stands. Bodies lie for 10 s, then sink away.
  • Blood and dust (ImpactEffects): a hit on a character throws a puff of mist back towards the shooter and a heavier one out of the far side, plus a spray of droplets that fall under gravity and spot the floor where they land, and, with one ray along the shot, a splat on any wall within 2.4 m behind. A body that has come to rest leaves a pool that spreads over 5 s. Shots into the level throw a puff of dust and a few chips. Blood darkens as it dries and lasts 30 s (pools 45 s). Everything lives in fixed rings (192 particles, 48 puffs, 96 marks, 16 pools) that overwrite their oldest entries, so a frame allocates nothing and costs a few hundred quads at most.

Textures live in TextureStore: RGBA8 images in device memory, each with a combined-image-sampler descriptor set, and identified by that set's handle, which doubles as Dear ImGui's texture ID. Art is uploaded as sRGB so sampling returns linear color; ImGui's font atlas is plain UNORM coverage. The swapchain is sRGB, so the UI shader converts ImGui's vertex colors (authored in sRGB) to linear before writing them. Uploads wait for the GPU, which is fine for textures that rarely change. Why our own ImGui renderer? BRUTAL's own ImGui Vulkan backend needs a render pass object, which this renderer doesn't use, hence the small renderer here.

Camera conventions. CameraMath uses row vectors, like System.Numerics (clip = position * ViewProjection). The projection is reverse-Z with an infinite far plane: depth is 1 at the 5 cm near plane and approaches 0 with distance, which keeps a 32-bit float depth buffer precise everywhere. The viewport has a negative height, so clip space is +Y up and glTF's counter-clockwise front faces stay counter-clockwise. The world shader receives the view-projection matrix as four rows in push constants and multiplies them out itself, so no matrix-layout convention can disagree between C# and the shader.

Shaders are Slang in content/shaders. The client build runs tools/LastFleet.ShaderCompiler, which compiles each file's vertexMain, fragmentMain or computeMain to <name>.vert.spv, .frag.spv or .comp.spv in the output's shaders folder. Slang errors become build errors with file and line. The game loads SPIR-V only and never ships the Slang compiler. LastFleet.Content.Tests also compiles every shader, so a broken shader fails the tests as well as the build.

Meshes. GltfMeshLoader walks a glTF scene, bakes node transforms into world space, gives every triangle a face normal and its material's base color, merges identical vertices and optimizes the result with meshoptimizer. The renderer uploads the resulting MeshData once.

Looking at one spot. Want a screenshot of an exact view? --smoke <frames> --camera x,y,z,yaw,pitch[,light][,crouch][,aim][,fire] puts the player at that spot instead of running the scripted play (unharmed and still, with the flashlight on with ,light, and holding crouch, aim or fire), so a screenshot shows a chosen view. That's how lighting, shadows and cover were checked.

Testing without a GPU. Camera math, overlay geometry, the push-constant layout and the PNG writer are tested in LastFleet.Rendering.Tests. The GPU path is tested by the gpu-smoke CI job: it installs Mesa's software Vulkan driver (lavapipe), the validation layer and a virtual X display, renders 150 frames of scripted play with validation on, fails on any validation error, and uploads a screenshot of frame 140 as the gpu-smoke-screenshot artifact.

One Linux gotcha: BRUTAL asks for the Vulkan loader as vulkan, which only resolves to the unversioned libvulkan.so that development packages install. VulkanRenderer.PreloadLoader falls back to libvulkan.so.1, so players only need the runtime package.

Sound

LastFleet.Audio plays through OpenAL Soft (Brutal.OpenAl) and knows nothing about the game. It has:

  • 48 voices. VoicePool decides who gets one when they're all busy: the cue's own oldest voice, then the lowest priority, but never a loop and never a higher priority. Each voice has a low-pass filter for sounds heard through walls.
  • One reverb they all send to, set from a space-station preset based on the size of the listener's room.
  • Master (the listener's gain, so reverb tails follow it), effects, interface and ambience volumes.
  • Sounds delayed by their distance.

With no device it plays nothing. Offline (a loopback device, as the smoke run and its tests use) it mixes into memory. The cues are data (content/defs/sounds.json). A cue's clips are its recordings (content/sounds/<name>.wav, <name>_2.wav) or, until it has any, a placeholder synthesized in code (PlaceholderSounds, about 0.8 s at startup, loaded in the background behind the splash). docs/sound.md is the sound designer's guide.

GameAudio (Client) turns what the client sees into sound:

  • Each tick that's the shots (one per shooter, using the weapon's frame on the ShotRecord; yours play the moment you fire), hits (impacts by surface, flesh, rounds whizzing past your head), explosions and breaks.
  • Each frame it's everyone's feet (a step per stride, landings, slides), grenades (thrown, bouncing, beeping with their light), your gun's handling, pickups, flashlight and health, the mission's changes, and the buzz of the nearest failing lights.

Sounds in rooms the renderer culls (RoomVisibility) are muffled. The game asks for sounds through ISoundOutput, which tests record, and which an FMOD engine could implement.

Decisions

Here are the big calls we've made, why we made them, and what would make us think again:

Decision Why Revisit if
BRUTAL as the foundation, with our own engine layer on top Direct, low-overhead access to Vulkan, GLFW, Jolt, FMOD and RakNet from modern C#, with no editor or scene graph to fight A required native library is missing from BRUTAL
Friflo.Engine.ECS behind LastFleet.Ecs Fast archetype ECS with relations, serialization and zero-allocation chunk queries. Consoles aren't a goal, so its LGPL-3.0 JSON dependency (see THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES) is acceptable, shipped as separate DLLs We want Native AOT, which the LGPL dependency rules out. Arch (Apache-2.0) is the fallback
Our own ISimulationSystem and GameWorld instead of Friflo's SystemRoot We own update order and scheduling, so systems can move onto BRUTAL's job system later without changes —
60 Hz fixed tick with commands as the only input Stable movement and physics, and the same model the host-authoritative co-op design needs —
Host-authoritative listen server for co-op Determinism across physics, hundreds of horde agents and floating-point AI is too fragile for lockstep —
Vulkan 1.3 core only No optional extensions to probe; dynamic rendering and sync2 keep the renderer short We need ray tracing or mesh shaders
Slang, compiled at build time A modern shader language with modules and generics; SPIR-V is checked on every build and the game ships no compiler Hot reload needs run-time compilation (a dev-only path, not a reason to ship Slang)
Reverse-Z, infinite far plane, 32-bit float depth Even depth precision from a 5 cm near plane to kilometers away; no far plane to tune —
Grey-box modules as text .gltf They diff and review as text; .glb goes through Git LFS Modules grow past a few hundred kilobytes
Collision behind ICollisionWorld The simulation stays engine-free and fast to test; Jolt can be swapped out or stubbed —
AI writes PlayerCommands Enemies follow the same movement, weapon and collision rules as players: fair by construction, and one code path to tune —
Layered grid navigation from rays, not a navmesh library (DotRecast) Exact match with collision; tiny, deterministic, allocation-free; stacked floors and drops cover the station's verticality, and the cover map and strafe checks work on its cells Large outdoor maps or caves where 0.25 m cells cost too much memory and search time, or crowds that need a navmesh's corridors and avoidance
Jolt virtual characters, not rigid bodies Kinematic control exactly as the controller decides, with stairs, slopes and shape swaps handled by Jolt We want physically pushed players
xUnit v3 on Microsoft.Testing.Platform The current .NET 10 test stack; dotnet test runs test projects as executables —
OpenAL Soft for sound (through Brutal.OpenAl), not FMOD as the design doc planned It ships inside its NuGet package with no account or license, so every clone and CI run plays (or mixes) sound; positional voices, filters and EFX reverb are all the shooter needs so far. Known quirks

A few oddities you might trip over:

  • Friflo prints one Assemblies loaded: … line to the console the first time an EntityStore is created. It's scanning loaded assemblies for component types by reflection.
  • That reflection scan is also why Native AOT would need Friflo's explicit NativeAOT schema registration, on top of the license issue above.
  • GLFW on Linux X11: synthetic mouse events (for example from xdotool) don't produce raw-motion events. Real mice do. If you automate input tests on Linux, turn raw motion off.
